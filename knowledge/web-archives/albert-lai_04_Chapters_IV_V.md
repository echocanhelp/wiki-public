Mr. Song
Aged 34, born into a poor shing village family, whose father was killed in the 2-28 massacre by
Chiang Kai-shek’s army, was raised together with three other children by his widowed mother. For
many years Mrs. Song supported the family alone by working as a maid and operating a vegetable

He nished his college education and worked for two years. Being the oldest, he tried to help his

decided to go to the United States where he knew he could make more money.
In 1963 he arrived in the East Coast. By working part time, he nished his master in computer
science. He moved to Los Angeles and found a well-paying job. Monthly, out of loyalty and
responsibility, he sent his mother, sister and brothers in college money.
Partially because his father was killed in the Massacre and partially because he experienced
poverty and hardship, he enthusiastically partook in the Movement. His involvement was learned, and
his family has been threatened and harassed ever since by the Nationalist Government, by the secret
police. Also, his brother’s application to go to the United States was rejected for the same reason.
Last year his mother died. He loved her very much and wanted to return for the funeral but was
unable to do so, for he knew that if they would not let his brother out that they would not allow him
to leave Formosa once he went back. This was the greatest tragedy in his entire life. He became more
infuriated with the government of Formosa. From this point on, he gave whatever sums he would have
sent his mother to the Formosa freedom movement.
One facet of Mr. Song’s life must be mentioned. In 1966, before becoming a member of the
Movement, he agreed to go home to Formosa to marry a “picture bride” selected by his mother. (A
picture bride is generally carefully chosen by the son’s family. A picture of the woman with a short
resume is then forwarded to the young man who either agrees to marry or not to marry the woman
selected.) Two months later, he traveled back to the U.S.A. with his Formosan bride.
The marriage itself has worked out well, but the bride has not been able to acculturate. For this
reason, too, Song feels that their home is Formosa and that one day they will go back when conditions
are better.
Formosa itself is in a transitional stage from agricultural to industrial. In general, picture brides
have been abandoned and rejected by educated Formosans in Formosa; but Formosans in the U.S.,
because of their traditional, conservative view on marriage, are not willing to marry non-Formosans.

CHAPTER IV
FORMOSAN CHRISTIAN CHURCH IN LOS ANGELES

This chapter is a presentation of the background, brief history, current status and prospect of a
newly organized church in Los Angeles -- Formosan Christian Church in Los Angeles, California -and the characteristics of the people in the community. I have spent my level IV year of San
Francisco Theological Seminary to work with this project in developing a Formosan community
church in Los Angeles.

I. Background
A. Christianity and Formosans
It was only six years ago that the Protestant churches in Formosa celebrated their centenary,
which marked the beginning of a second century of a rapidly progressing movement in Christianity
on the island of Formosa.
Dr. James L. Maxwell of the Presbyterian Church of England commenced the rst mission on
the island in 1865. This was followed by a continuous ow of missionaries from the Western World,
such as England, Canada, and the United States. Their sacri ce and tireless effort made the
spreading of the Gospel possible among the people who have had their rigid tradition, selfcontained culture, and conservative religious views for so many thousands of years. Now there are
about 400,000 members in the Protestant churches in Formosa, which constitute about 3 percent of
the island population. Although it is still a minority, the in uence of the church on the better quality
of education, medical care, social welfare on the island are evident.
Since there were practically no other Christian churches working among the islanders besides
the Presbyterian before the Nationalist Chinese were defeated by the Communists and their
withdrawal to the island in 1949, the Presbyterian church is the largest and oldest Christian church
in Formosa. It is estimated that three-fourths of Protestant Christians on the island now are
Presbyterians.
During World War II, all missionaries in Formosa were expelled by the Japanese government
and went back to their home countries. All nancial aids from churches in Western countries
naturally were completely cut off. This was a bitter and disappointing experience, but it taught them
an important lesson: Formosan churches could no longer depend on Western missionaries. If they
wanted to survive, they had to carry out the mission themselves. In other words, they had to be

independent denomination on the island, although about 50 other denominations entered the island

B. Formosans in Los Angeles
As mentioned previously, there were practically no Formosan immigrants in the United States
before World War II. Because of political panic, the number of Formosan college graduates
entering the States for advanced study have been increasing in recent years. There are about 10,000
students, engineers, doctors and other professionals in the United States presently. A majority of
them choose to stay in this country because of the abundant opportunities available and the
democratic freedom they enjoy so much.
It is estimated that there are about 1,000 Formosans scattered in Metropolitan Los Angeles.
This is an estimated 300 percent increase over the past ve years and is expected to double in the
next three years. They are young (average age is below thirty- ve), well educated (most of them have
or are working on their masters or Ph.D. education), highly motivated and extremely talented. Due
to the political situation, most of them are permanent residents with the intention of becoming
citizens, for they see no hope in returning. The reasons for such a Formosan community church are
as follows:
1. Sociologically.
Although all Formosans arrive with Chinese passports, they stick together according to
their dialects and differentiate themselves against either the Cantonese-speaking people or
the Mandarin-speaking people. On the other hand, they feel that assimilation with the
American culture is not possible with their cultural and language barriers and tend to be
socially introverted. Under such a condition, therefore, there is expectation that the church
will provide the only social activity center.
2. Politically.
As mentioned before, because of the differences in the political interests of the ruling
and the ruled, the opportunities in Formosa are very limited for Formosans after studying in
the United States to contribute their skills and knowledge. The reason is more political than
any other that there are more Formosans than any other nationalities who elect to stay in this
country in recent years.
3. Theologically.
The Presbyterian Church of Formosa is experiencing a severe brain drain, for they keep
sending their well-trained, young college graduates to the United States and seldom see them
return to their home churches. While on this side of the Paci c, there is practically no ethnic

that they are not culturally and linguistically ready to merge into the Anglo-American
churches.

II. Organizing The Formosan Community
A. Spirits Of Mission
The church is an organization in which a group of people can touch one another’s heart and
feel that they are one in Holy Spirit. The church is a living body in which people can be touched by
the proclamation of the Gospel and be sent to serve the world. In this spirit the Formosan church in
Los Angeles is organized to carry out the following missions:
To regroup the Formosan Christians who have been drifting away from the church and
Christian faith simply because there is no church paying due attention to them and answering their
special needs;
To function as a socio-cultural center for the people coming from the Orient and to provide
services which are pertinent to their needs, such as immigration, employment, health consultation,
recreational activities, etc.;
To witness the Christian faith among fellow non-believer Formosans, who are still the great
majority. The challenge of distributing the Gospel of Faith, Hope and Love to more Formosans
seems more urgent in this land and at this hour. An ethnic church can approach these people more
effectively and answer their needs more appropriately.
The Formosan Christian Church in L.A. is primarily concerned with people and not real
estates or facilities. The role of the minister and the church will be taken as mainly to serve these
socially and politically insecure people. Imagine that to these Formosans this time in history is
comparable to the pilgrims of the pioneer era. To the newcomer, direction in orientation,
information, introduction, comfort, and encouragement are seriously needed in order to live and

prosper in this racially and culturally alien society.

B. Steps Towards A Church
1. Family Prayer Meetings
A group of 20 to 30 were already meeting every other Saturday night rotating among the
Formosan Christian families in early 1970’s. This circle was formed out of spontaneous auspices.
Although some of the attendants had been associated with a Chinese church -- First Evangelical
Church -- they had intended to form their own separate church. The group chose to meet on
Saturday nights in order to allow those associated with other churches to attend on Sundays. This
prayer meeting circle made plans and held discussions before the actual organization of the church.
2. One-Month Experimental Church.
In August 1970, Reverend Jonah Chang of the United Methodist Church in Fresno, California,
a former minister of Presbyterian Church of Formosa, started a one-month experimental church to
test the feasibility of such a church group in the Formosan community. At the end of August, when
Rev. Chang returned to Fresno and the experiment was concluded, the majority of the congregation
envisioned the importance of carrying on such a fellowship among the Formosan people and
decided to organize a church despite the fact that they had no pastor or that no outside nancial
support was available. Through the assistance of Centenary United Methodist Church and its
minister, Rev. Peter Chen, they used the facilities of the Wesley Hall of the Centenary United
Methodist Church until they moved to Wilshire Crest Presbyterian Church in July 1971.
3. Contacts With Presbytery and Synod.
The Presbyterian Church is the largest and the only nancially independent denomination on
the island of Formosa. Most of the members of this newly-born church were members of the
Presbyterian Church of Formosa and are very proud of their Presbyterian heritage. Denominational
loyalties among these Christians are very strong by American standards. Right from the beginning
in forming this church, they seriously considered association with the United Presbyterian Church in
the United States of America.
Through a careful and continuous contact with various departments and staff at the Synod
Of ce of Southern California and Presbytery of Los Angeles Southwest, in February, 1971, the
Formosan Christian Church decided to join U.P.C.A. (United Presbyterian Church in United States
of America) and asked their nancial and moral support for a period of time. The Presbytery and
Congregational Development Committee of the Presbytery of Los Angeles Southwest gradually

III. Associate With United Presbyterian Church In U.S.A.
The church has been in communication with the Presbytery since late fall of 1970 about
becoming part of the United Presbyterian Church. The Presbytery Committee voted on March 27,
1971, to work with the leadership of the church to try to develop a workable plan for developing
within a three to ve year period a self-supporting United Presbyterian congregation, if and when
such a plan can be developed, to recommend to Presbytery formal action toward organization of a
United Presbyterian Church.
The Request for Funding of April 14, 1971, from the Committee to the Department of
Presbytery and Congregational Development of the Synod of Southern California states:
“The present request is for support of what the Committee feels to be already a highly signi cant
ministry and mission that o ers promise of becoming a successful model by a somewhat di erent form
of church development with ethnic minorities within our Synod. The intent is to enable the present
church to maintain its momentum while at the same time allowing the church and the Presbytery time
to make a truly sound judgment regarding its organization as a Presbyterian congregation.”
Although the church was using facilities of Centenary Methodist Church at that time without
any problems or obligations, the leadership of the church was not willing to discuss seriously
af liation with the Methodist church, because of deeply based ties in Formosan culture with
Presbyterianism, and considered that a permanently ecumenical approach involved too much
insecurity for the new congregation. The fundamental direction of the church is to develop full
acceptance as a United Presbyterian congregation.
Towards this end, the Presbytery Committee agreed to the Formosan Church’s request to try to
secure an invitation from a Presbyterian congregation to share facilities, which will become more
than a space-sharing arrangement.
Through a careful review of locations, facilities and training, the Formosan Church decided to
accept an invitation from the Wilshire Crest Presbyterian Church of Los Angeles. On July 27, 1971,
Formosan Christian Church moved to share Wilshire Crest’s facilities located near the intersection
of Olympic and La Brea in Los Angeles.
The proposed organization of Formosan Presbyterian Church and request of funding
presented by the Presbytery and Congregational Development Committee was approved by the
Presbytery of Los Angeles Southwest on September 28, 1971, at a meeting held at Brentwood
Presbyterian Church, Los Angeles.

IV. Strategy Consideration -- From Presbytery’s Point of View
Two areas of Presbytery strategy have compelled the Presbytery and Congregational
Development Committee to seriously consider the request of the Formosan group to assist them in
developing their ministry. One is the growing emphasis in Presbytery to use the church’s resources
toward efforts where there already exists a signi cant spark of life in the people to be served. The
Committee could not overlook the initiative and commitment of these people and believes it
feasible, in spite of the relatively small population to be served, that this ministry will attain selfsupport within a relatively short time. The other is the intent and practice of Presbytery to be
responsive to the changing urban community in which it nds itself. Their aid is consistent with this
commitment to seek to organize a new ministry in order to deal with particular new urban
phenomenon, the advent of these newcomers, even though the Presbytery faces dif culties in
sustaining its other congregations.
The design of the proposed new congregation is consistent with Synod and General Assembly
guidelines in the following ways.
1. It proposes to serve a very clearly de ned and strongly-felt community and to deal
particularly with needs peculiar to this community -- the Formosan immigrants.
2. The life-style of the group already includes a strong corporate ministry, as well as concern
for individuals, not only in a rich fellowship that is inclusive of both Christians and nonChristians but in dealing with the problems of relating these people to Anglo culture and
institutions.
3. It is the intent of this involvement to keep open options for change in the style of ministry
and to help the congregation avoid being encumbered in the near future with concerns for
building a facility.

of the church can be enhanced as these new United Presbyterians are enabled to actively participate
in the life of the Presbyterian denomination.

“sharing” this News to the unchurched.

doing janitorial services such as cleaning sanctuary and church school rooms. Everybody got the

In the Formosan Church, the activities are designed and carried based upon the fundamental
needs of the community and individuals. The following is a brief description of some activities in
the church at this time:
1. Sunday morning worship service in Formosan language.
The attendants presently average between 70 and 90 (including 15-20 children) -- a great
increase from the initial 20 of last September. Half of them are non-Christians who are
searching for fellowship and the enlightenment of Christian faith. The church is attracting new
attendants at a rate of 2-5 persons weekly.
2. A joint worship with Wilshire Crest United Presbyterian Church
on the last Sunday of every month has been one of the most meaningful and inspirational to
both congregations. The worship service is cooperated by pastors of both congregations.
3. Agape Meal
After worship service each Sunday morning, the church has an Agape Meal consisting of
potluck lunch of native food. Through this ancient Christian tradition of sharing love and care,
the congregation and their friends greatly enrich their community life.
4. Sunday School
There are about 15 to 20 Sunday school students in three different classes ranging from preschool to the sixth grade. The volunteer director of the program is a seminarian from School of
Theology, Claremont, California, majoring in Christian Education.
5. Choir
There are about 15 members in the choir. Under direction, the choir practices hymns, classics,
and Formosan folk songs as well. The choir has helped the service a great deal.
6. Social function
Every Saturday afternoon the church is open to the entire Formosan community in Los Angeles
as a social center. The church provides facilities for table tennis, volleyball, games, music as well
as folk dancing.
7. Family prayer meeting
There is a prayer meeting every other Friday night at homes of different members. This circle
was where all the plans and discussions had been located before the actual organization of the
church.
At this bi-weekly gathering, they study the Bible, theological textbooks and discuss the way of
Christian living. Through study, discussion and praying together, the church carries on
important adult Christian education in a very fruitful way.

8. Lectures on special interests
Many members of this congregation are experts in one eld or another, but their knowledge is
quite limited beyond their own eld. In order to share the knowledge and experience, the
church sponsors a series of lectures held once a month on various interesting topics as
"Computer and Modern Man," "How to Find a Doctor If You Are Sick," "Income Tax and
You," open to the entire community.
9. Retreat
The church had a very successful retreat at Lazy-W Ranch, a United Methodist's retreat
ground, during the last Christmas holidays. Seventy adults and children gathered to enjoy the
happiness of Christian fellowship. They vividly demonstrated to those non-Christians who were
among them the joy and exciting ful llment of being a Christian. They are planning to have
another one this coming Christmas.

VI. Preliminary Mission Design
The experience of nine months’ association with this particular congregation certainly is long
enough to formulate a list of suggestions regarding the future direction and strategy for this newborn church. Based upon the nine-month actual involvement with the church as well as in
discussions with groups and interviews with a number of individuals of the entire Formosan
community, I shall summarize a guideline for this project; of course, it is based only upon limited
observation and contact.

A. Style of Ministry.
The style of ministry of this new congregation should re ect the immigrant status of the
people to be served, the relatively small number in Los Angeles area, the culture native to them,
their relative sophistication, and their commitment to the Presbyterian church.
The outreach should be metropolitan and extensive, seeking to touch all Formosans in the area.
It should have particular emphasis on university centers. Strong emphasis should be given to
fellowship and the attempt to deal with the needs of the whole man. Included in this invitation to
fellowship and assistance should be both Christians and non-Christians. This congregation should
be an instrument for the self-development of this particular people. At the same time, there should
be emphasis on pastoral care and nurture of the congregation, particularly of new members and
those inquiring into the faith.
The denominational identity should be Presbyterian. The leaders of the initiating group are
Presbyterian by heritage with strong tie to Presbyterian families in Formosa. The language of

B. Concerns and Issues.
The shared facilities and shared life with the congregation of Wilshire Crest United

1. Dealing with needs of the new Los Angeles Formosan community, including:
a. Adjustment to an entirely foreign culture and coping with sometimes severe dif culties of
communication (i.e., as in describing symptom to an Anglo physician).
b. Psychic and psychological insecurity (i.e., there is an indicated need for marriage
counseling).
c. The problem of identity, as faced by other minorities in America.
d. Renewal of the spirit, which the church is uniquely equipped to deal with.
e. Planning for the inevitable change that come to the situation and needs of an immigrant
people as the second generation emerges.
2. The other concerns extend beyond this group in Los Angeles and include:
a. Dealing with the place of Asians in American society and of Asian churches within
Presbytery and Synod.
b. Strategies of ministries to Formosans in other areas of the nation.
c. Relations with the Presbyterian Church in Formosa in order to facilitate informal contact
in thinking about ministry to immigrants from Formosa to this country.
d. It is the intent also to develop strategy with other denominations in the Los Angeles area
regarding work with Formosans and to encourage continued development of fraternal
relations with the only other Formosan group in the metropolitan area, which is
approximately 30 miles from this church.
e. It is a strongly conservative, evangelical-oriented church.

CHAPTER V
INTEGRATION, INDEPENDENCE AND
IDENTITY OF FORMOSAN COMMUNITY

Up to the present point in our study, attention has been focused primarily upon the empirical
characteristics of the Formosan community and the church in the community (viewed as a social
institution). The rst two chapters traced the historical background of the community and its
people. The third chapter illustrated the growth of a new community in this country, sketched its
present psychological state and the social characteristics of its people. Chapter four brought
attention to the goodwill of a group of Christians in the community developing a community

B. Reference Groups and Their Americanization
The term immigrant, which Americans use in a more or less blanket-like fashion, falls in at
least four different categories. The four categories are as follows:
1. Emigrant is he who on his own decides to leave his country and who goes to another with
the intention of becoming its citizen.
2. Migrant is one who temporarily leaves his country to go abroad but is planning to return
to his own country when he achieves his objective of going abroad.
3. Refugee is he who seeks asylum outside of his own country in order to escape whatever
predicament (usually political in nature, but occasionally economic, religious or even
occupational) he could not escape at home. He has every intention of returning to his own
country as soon as the situation permits and has no intention, at the outset at any rate, to
becoming a citizen, which offers him an asylum.
4. Displaced person is he who by circumstance has been forced out of his country and
invited by another country as its guest.
All of them, irrespective of their original intention, inevitably undergo the process of
acclimatization, acculturation and indigenization however much they may be discriminated against.
But the emigrant is more likely to make deliberate efforts to accelerate this process while the other
three may resist it as long as possible. To put it bluntly, although they may all be looked upon as
“immigrants,” the emigrant is the only one to whom the U.S.A. is the sociologist’s so-called
“reference group” from the beginning. To the other three, the reference group remains their old
country.

D. Internal Integration And Cultural Harmony
In the process of cultural harmony, one should not ignore or dispute the value of
differentiation of each culture. Without differentiation, there is no integration. Modern
psychologists have concluded that individuality is the result of differentiation. Jung’s
“individuation,” Maslow’s “self-actualization,” Allport’s “functional autonomy” and the emergence
of the ego in Freud’s personality theory are concepts of describing the process of differentiation in
human growth. They have the idea of individuality in common. This concept not only explains
human growth but cultural integration as well. To be integrated is to become an independent,
autonomous culture. Erickson’s “ego-identity” and “integrity” have a strong cultural, individualistic
connotation also. Erich Fromm’s positive view of self-love is signi cant in the understanding of
individuality as a necessity of integration. This individuality is well expressed in Paul Tillich’s
“courage to be oneself.” It is embodied in the Oriental philosophy in the Confucian “ethical
individual” through self-cultivation, in the Hinayana Buddhist arhatship, and in the basis for the
Zen Enlightenment.
However, cultural differentiation needs to be accompanied by integration. From the dynamic,
functional point of view, “integrity” signi es the degree of integration. So often, “integrity” is
understood as consistency of values in one’s moral behavior in a static sense. If one replaces his old
values with the unconventional, he is regarded as losing his integrity. This understanding of integrity
is likely to regard integration as a state, rather than as a process.
In this study of Oriental immigrants in an Occidental culture, “integrity” should be seen from
the functional, process-oriented point of view. This process-oriented view of “cultural integrity” is
congenial to the Freudian harmony between the ego, the superego, and the id in the normal,
healthy person. It means without integrity there is no individuality in the true sense, for such
selfhood is divided.
This integration of the individual person is better understood as the internal harmony because
the word “Harmony” does not have the implication of rigidity. A mature person is not rigid though
he is internally integrated around a culture. He is exible to make necessary adjustments to his
environment. Although he wants to maintain his identity which has come from his past experiences,
he is capable of accepting new experiences, digesting and absorbing them, and integrating them
into a new identity. This capacity for exibility is the strength of the new immigrant in his struggle
for survival in a foreign land.
It takes real courage and stamina for a young person to leave the safety and support of his
family and culture to go to a strange and distant country to study with an intention of staying there
for a long period. When he studies abroad, he must necessarily compete with his host country’s
nationals playing the academic game on their own ground and by their own rules. He must adapt
himself to their educational pattern, work in their language, live on their kind of food, conform to

The internal integration is the source of strength for a new immigrant to cope with tension and

The source of one’s internal integration and external harmony is love which includes all its

I. Colonialism and Alienation According To Formosan Experience

Colonialism is not a new situation. It is indeed very old. In the past, colonialism was so
successful that it was able not only to maintain an oppressed man but an oppressed consciousness as
well. An oppressed consciousness is that which is domesticated by the situation in which it nds
itself. It is a consciousness which really becomes re exive, unable to be the subject, deprived of a
sense of direction and of historical vocation. Dominated by re exiveness, this consciousness could
not speak. Unable to enter in critical dialogue with their environment, as a consequence of the
relationship of domination which oppressed them, these societies became “mute”.
Fortunately, however, a remarkable change occurred in the Formosan people’s selfunderstanding. Like the self-awareness of the Third World and the Black man in the United States
of America, Formosan became aware that although to live without-freedom was terribly painful that
was not the most basic of their problems. They came to realize that they were not simply withoutfreedom. They were made without-freedom. Their without-freedom was the consequence of a
colonial relationship in which the powerful dominated and controlled the lives of the weak and
powerless. To Formosans, colonialism is not just a situation of the past. It is intrinsic to the relations
between ruler, Chiang, and ruled, Formosans, and indeed the cause of without-freedom of the
Formosans. But more than that, colonialism came to be seen as a relationship in which those who
are dominated are not allowed to become the creator of their own history. They are reduced to a

III. The U.N.’s Expulsion of Nationalist Government and Formosa’s Future
It is regrettable that the expulsion of the regime of Chiang Kai-shek has frequently been
identi ed with the expulsion of Formosa. The truth of the matter is that Formosa has never been
represented by any government in the United Nations. What was expelled by the majority of the
United Nations was the regime falsely claiming to be the government of China. The defeat of the
U.S. and Japanese policy was due precisely to their failing to distinguish the regime of Chiang from
the people of Formosa. The majority of the U.N. members felt justi ed in opposing the U.N.
proposal (endorsed by Japan), because as long as there was another government of China in the
United Nations, there would be no representation from the true China. Therefore, it seems only
reasonable that the government of China should be given its proper place in the world organization.
On the other hand, it is also true that as long as the regime of Chiang retains its seat, there would
be no true representation of Formosa in the United Nations. Thus it seems equally reasonable that
the government of Chiang pretending to be the government of China was nally expelled.
Ironically, the issue has not been the expulsion of Formosa or the representation of China. On
one hand, the expulsion of Chiang’s regime from the U.N. does not solve the problem of Formosa.
On the other hand, the loss of U.N. seat does not necessarily mean that Formosa will cease to be an
independent political entity. The crux of the matter is the future of the 14 million people on the
island.
The United Nations’ decision in seating Peking and expelling Chiang’s regime—President
Nixon’s proposed visit to the mainland—underscores the tangle of the Formosan issue. And now as
world leaders nally admit the absurdities of nationalistic Chinese fantasies, a new myth emerges:
That Formosa has been and must be an integral part of China to achieve full sovereignty, that she
must annex Formosa.

and relations were tumultuous. In the last 75 years the island has fewer than four years of union
with China, 1945-1949, and those were by no means the happiest in Formosan history. In 1947 an
uprising occurred which resulted in the massacre of over 20,000 Formosan leaders. Native
Formosans are of Chinese extraction, but have shared centuries of experience unique to them and

Certainly, we are living in an era of change, and in the process of change people very easily get
