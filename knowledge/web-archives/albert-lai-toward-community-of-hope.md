Half a Century of Faith and Mission
National Taiwanese Presbyterian Council, Presbyterian Church (U.S.A.)

In 1971, Dr. Albert S. Lai（賴信雄） published Toward A Community Of Hope —A Mission
to Formosan Community in Los Angeles, a work both theological and prophetic in nature. At a time
when Taiwanese immigrant churches in North America were still in their infancy—especially the
Formosan Presbyterian Church in Los Angeles (FPCLA), the rst of its kind in the continent—Dr.
Lai offered not merely a historical review, but a spiritual vision. He called the church to transcend
being merely a haven for immigrants and instead become a missional community, a beacon of hope
and a witness to the gospel in the larger society.
A Spiritual Call Amid Cultural Transition
In the early 1970s, most Taiwanese in North America arrived with hopes for education,
professional advancement, or temporary settlement. They faced linguistic barriers, cultural
alienation, and identity uncertainty. Churches quickly became one of the few familiar places in a
foreign land—a spiritual anchor amid transition. Yet Dr. Lai pushed the church to think beyond
comfort. He boldly asserted that if the church is indeed called by God, then it must live as a sent
people—not merely surviving, but witnessing to and transforming its surrounding society.
The Los Angeles Calling: From Ethnic Church to Missional Community
The publication of this book is inextricably linked to the establishment of the Formosan
Presbyterian Church in Los Angeles (FPCLA). As the rst Taiwanese church in North America,
FPCLA did not exist merely to bridge linguistic or cultural gaps. From its inception, it sought to
become a “mission base” in the local community. Dr. Lai documented both the struggles and
aspirations of the early church—how believers found spiritual meaning beyond work and family,
how they supported one another in a foreign land, and how that internal community gave birth to
an external calling to serve and transform.
The Theological Vision of a “Hopeful Community”
The idea of a “hopeful community” carries rich theological weight. It is not a slogan born of
optimism or human willpower, but a lived imagination rooted in biblical truth. Drawing from Acts
and Pauline letters, Dr. Lai described the church not as a safe retreat for insiders, but as a dynamic
body for the hope of the world. A hopeful community is one that chooses love in adversity, chooses

Continuing the Un nished Mission
Over ve decades later, Taiwanese churches in Los Angeles and across North America have
undergone multiple transitions—from immigrant churches to intergenerational, multicultural
communities; from language-based fellowships to agents of justice and public engagement. Yet, the
core challenge of this book remains timeless: Are we still a hopeful community? Are we still bearers
of the gospel light for Los Angeles and beyond? Are we still faithful to the vision rst cast in 1971?
Honoring the Past, Journeying into the Future
We give heartfelt thanks to Dr. Albert Lai for his vision and voice, and we honor the pioneers
who laid the spiritual foundation of FPCLA. Their faith, courage, and servant hearts gave rise not
only to a book or a church, but to a movement that has shaped the spiritual heritage of Taiwanese
Christianity in North America. May this commemorative reprint not merely mark a retrospective
moment, but sound a fresh call to mission—inviting a new generation to rise and respond.
Let us once again embrace the weight of this phrase—Toward A Community Of Hope —A
Mission to Formosan Community in Los Angeles—not merely as a book title, but as a journey still
unfolding.
To God be the glory!

Subtle Similarities
In the early 1970s, I attempted to engage in trade with the Communist Party and worked in
advertising, translation, printing, and publishing in Los Angeles. In the 1980s, I had the opportunity
to work in publishing, training, and international resource integration in China. In the 1990s, I was

and industries related to the internet and e-commerce in China. After 2000, I transitioned into
private English education and university education in China, until my retirement in 2020.
However, when I revisited the life work of Dr. Barclay in Taiwan; re ected on my father's
lifelong ministry; and also re-examined my own experiences over the past 50 years, I unexpectedly
discovered that my father and I, two generations, shared similar backgrounds in terms of birth,
upbringing, education, and work environments after graduation. The timeline spans nearly a
century, covering the era of the Japanese Empire, the White Terror under Chiang Kai-shek in the
Republic of China, the democratic transition in Taiwan, the United States, and the People's

A. The Characteristics of This Book - A Fusion of Theology and History
"Toward a Community of Hope — A Mission to Formosan Community in Los Angeles" is the
doctoral dissertation completed by Dr. Albert S. Lai（賴信雄） in December 1971 while studying
at San Francisco Theological Seminary. The main purpose of this work is to discuss why evangelism
is necessary for Taiwanese immigrants in Los Angeles and the importance and theological
signi cance of establishing an ethnic church speci cally dedicated to nurturing Taiwanese people.
Fifty years later, the political landscape in Taiwan has undergone signi cant changes, transitioning
from an authoritarian regime to a fully democratic system. Moreover, national identity has shifted
from identifying as Chinese to Taiwanese, with only about 2% of the population still considering
themselves "Chinese." This difference makes it dif cult for contemporary readers to understand the
political developments in Taiwan in 1970 and the emotional journey of the Formosan community in
North America.
As we take the time to read this book, we gain insight into the concern of the Christian faith for
Taiwanese people in North America, while also entering the political context of Taiwan in 1970
through the author's words.
As a doctoral dissertation from a theological seminary, its purpose should be to elucidate
Christian faith and doctrine. However, structurally, it dedicates half of its content to narrating
"Taiwan's" own history and politics—topics that were serious taboos at the time, making it a unique
theological dissertation. The reason for this phenomenon lies in the fact that the target audience for
evangelism in the text is "Taiwanese" (Formosan) in Los Angeles. In 1970, the American church
community was quite unfamiliar with "Taiwanese," so the author needed to provide background
knowledge on Taiwan's geography, historical culture, political development, and the psychological
and physical conditions of Taiwanese immigrants. Moreover, the concern for immigrants extends
beyond the physical realm to deeper spiritual and emotional needs. Without addressing a group's
history and culture, holistic care cannot be achieved. This book, as a dialogue between history and
theology, also asserts that the Christian faith cannot be separated from history and culture, and this
aspect of theological exploration is deeply discussed in this text.

C. The Gap Between Taiwanese Society and

With the democratization of Taiwan, Taiwanese civil society has become accustomed to the
freedom of speech and the freedom to travel in and out of the country. "Taiwan independence" can
now be publicly discussed and is no longer a taboo topic; there has even been academic research on

However, despite these freedoms, there remains an invisible gap in civil society regarding the
Formosan community in North America.
Firstly, civil society is quite unfamiliar with the knowledge elites overseas who support Taiwan's
"nation-building." What is often recognized are the opinions expressed by certain independence
advocates or their contributions to Taiwan's democracy noted in the news upon their passing, but
these "contributions" are largely disconnected from the lived experiences of the general public.
Secondly, for the majority of Taiwanese people, becoming an "American" is seen as a signi cant
marker of success in life. There has been a saying: “Comes, comes, comes, coming to National
Taiwan University; goes, goes, goes, going to America," which seems to imply that the ultimate goal
in life is to become an "American." Furthermore, if friends or family members obtain a U.S. "Green

societal atmosphere, the U.S. is perceived as a kind of "paradise," and living in America seems to
free one from the "ghost island" of Taiwan. This leads Taiwanese society to think: if people living in
the U.S. are enjoying such a ful lling life, why should they care about Taiwan's affairs? Or it may be
hard to understand why, despite living in "paradise," one could feel so miserable.
Lastly, beyond the Taiwanese public's "American" sentiment, changes in the political
environment have further widened this gap. Under the governance of the Democratic Progressive

from being marginalized in society to becoming politically "esteemed" gures. In summary, the
reasons for the gap between Taiwanese civil society and the Formosan community immigrating to
the United States can be discussed in two points: rst, civil society is largely unfamiliar with their life
experiences, often believing that immigration to the U.S. signi es success and a life of comfort;
second, while the overseas independence activists faced signi cant hardships, after the DPP came to
power, these activists gained considerable political standing.

D. Authoritarian Politics in Post-War Taiwan and
the Formation of Taiwanese Consciousness
Recently, a former mayor of Taipei has been detained and barred from contact due to
allegations of bene ting a consortium in a development project. In response to this political
controversy, a legislator with a Ph.D. in law from McGill University publicly declared it to be "green
terror," urging supporters to resist the current authoritarian regime. With the encouragement of this
law scholar and the promotion by supporters, terms like "green terror" and "authoritarian regime"
have circulated widely online and in the media, suggesting that the current political environment is
akin to that of the 1970s. This phenomenon indicates that many people in contemporary Taiwanese
society have no concept of the political system of the 1970s, allowing for the revival of the story of
"pointing at a deer and calling it a horse.”
To understand the life experiences of the Formosan community in North America today, it is
essential to return to the context of 1971 and understand the political environment at that time.
This section will explain the situation of authoritarianism after the war, the special relationship
between the Republic of China and the United States, and further elaborate on how Taiwanese
consciousness emerged within the North American Formosan community.
"Authoritarianism," as de ned by political scholar Juan Linz, is characterized as follows:
A political system that is a limited pluralism, but not responsible politics.
Lacking a comprehensive and coherent ideology, it is primarily based on the will of the ruler.
There is a lack of deep and sustained political mobilization, and it rejects spontaneous
grassroots political mobilization.
Political life is dominated by leader-centric political groups, where the exercise of power is not
xed but primarily aimed at enforcing the will of the leader.
When the Kuomintang (KMT) ruled mainland China, the military strongman Kaishek Chiang
（蔣中正） led the KMT to eliminate various political forces after controlling the central
government of the Republic of China. After retreating to Taiwan, they deepened military rule
through martial law. This political model is described as "authoritarian militarism," which translates
to "military authoritarianism" or "militarism." One criticism at the time was the failure to
promulgate a constitution to establish the rule of law; the KMT often acted in disregard of legal
norms. After Chiang retreated to Taiwan, this authoritarian political power was further
strengthened. On May 10, 1948, as Chiang faced continuous defeats in the Chinese Civil War, he
promulgated the "Temporary Provisions Effective During the Period of National Mobilization for
Suppression of the Communist Rebellion," freezing parts of the Constitution. As Chiang's rule

In addition to undermining the ve-powers system, Chiang further established numerous

administrative agencies and police institutions, could not legally order the operations of these
agencies, yet in practice, they were all directed by the Garrison Command. Although there was a
legal framework, intelligence units were unbound by it, extending the mentality of a military
strongman into the daily lives of Taiwanese society. This control over various aspects of life,
including publishing, speech, and assembly, characterized the political atmosphere in Taiwan at that
time.
Under Chiang's military authoritarian regime, elites in various sectors found little hope for
personal career development unless they entered the party-state system. For intellectual elites, the
suppression of thought and culture by the party-state resembled a prison for the mind. Furthermore,
Taiwanese elites faced another form of torment: the erasure of their personal subjectivity. After the
Republic of China retreated to Taiwan, it maintained the institutional and ideological constructs of
its former mainland-style governance, imposing them upon the island of Taiwan. As a result,
although Taiwan was merely a province of the Republic of China, it accounted for over 99% of the
territory of the Republic of China. Public employees and representatives at various levels in Taiwan
were allocated positions based on the quotas set for the "occupied regions," with Taiwanese people
receiving less than half of the available positions. Additionally, if Taiwanese individuals wanted to
work in government or educational institutions, they were required to be pro cient in "Mandarin,"
while speaking their "mother tongue" was considered a violation
subject to punishment. Under this mainland-centric system, Taiwanese people had to deny
their own subjectivity to enter the so-called "upper" society.

implement constitutional self-governance, but their request was denied by the party-state. During
the February 28 Incident, Chiang recorded in his diary on March 7, 1947, "This kind of Taiwanese
initially attached to us, having long suffered from the Japanese, forgot their homeland, thus they all

also the reason for the emergence of Taiwanese subjective consciousness.
Under this unequal rule, various degrees of resistance movements emerged among Taiwan's
political and intellectual elites, with some choosing to establish an independent nation. So how did
the Taiwanese independence movement unfold under the oppressive rule of the party-state? Indeed,
due to the omnipresent surveillance by the party-state, the independence movement within Taiwan
found it dif cult to develop, leading many activists to operate overseas. Japan and the United States
became important bases for the development of the movement. The historical connection to Japan
dates back to the Japanese colonial period, during which some Taiwanese had established networks
in Japan. Thus, intellectual and social elites like Wang Yu Te（王育德） and Lin Hsien Tang（林

intertwined with the international political landscape of the Cold War. During the Chinese Civil
War, as Chiang's forces began to collapse, the U.S. also ceased aid to the Republic of China.
However, with the outbreak of the Korean War and the formation of a confrontation between
democratic and communist camps, the previously abandoned Chiang regime became America's ally
in combating communist forces, receiving substantial aid. With American support, Chiang's forces
were able to take root in Taiwan, turning a crisis into stability. Therefore, while the KMT could
control the culture and economy on the island of Taiwan, it could not sever ties with the United
States. Moreover, Chiang's authoritarian rule also had to consider the American response and still
needed to present itself as a democratic nation. It was precisely because the Chiang regime was still
constrained by the U.S. that it allowed for a narrow space for Taiwanese intellectual elites to
develop. In this narrow space, Taiwanese elites, unable to develop in Taiwan, sought opportunities
in the U.S., which re ects the reality of “goes, goes, goes, going to America.”

E. Understanding Taiwanese Politics Through American Literature
With an understanding of the historical context, we can gain valuable insights from this book.
Firstly, it was written in November 1971, a critical moment when the Republic of China was
expelled from the United Nations. The development of the overseas Taiwanese independence
movement was in uenced by this event, and their discourse and actions were subsequently adjusted.
The author documents how Taiwanese intellectual elites articulated Taiwan's subjectivity before
1972. Interestingly, the book also explains why the Formosan community in the United States uses
"Formosa" instead of "Taiwan," primarily to distinguish themselves from the Republic of China's
"Taiwan" and to establish a new narrative of Taiwanese subjectivity. Therefore, this book offers
unique insights into Taiwanese identity, history, culture, and the post-war political system in Taiwan.
While some aspects of historical and cultural understanding may require revision based on
contemporary academic research, this text presents a rare historical narrative centered on Taiwan,
especially in the absence of "Taiwanese historiography" within Taiwan itself.
Notably, the author's discussions of Taiwanese history reveal that the sources cited are
predominantly foreign works. This suggests that the historical narrative is shaped by the author's
perspective of Taiwanese subjectivity, informed by external literature. This re ects the American
Taiwanese intellectual elite's understanding of their homeland's history and contributes to the
broader process of expanding Taiwanese historiography.
Secondly, nding uncensored articles within Taiwan under an authoritarian regime is
exceedingly dif cult. For researchers studying post-war Taiwanese history, it is clear that relevant
of cial documents have been censored and are often propaganda. Without a profound
understanding of authoritarian systems and a careful analysis of the ideological context of the

I. The Land -- Formosa
The island of Formosa, which the Chinese and the Japanese call Taiwan, is a link in a chain of
islands stretching from Kamchatka to the Malay Peninsula and sheltering the coastline of the East
Asia mainland from the open Paci c. Formosa is parallel to the mainland of China and is separated
from there by the Formosa Straits, which is 90 miles wide at the narrowest point. It extends from
north-northeast to south-southwest with an extreme length of 243 miles, a breadth generally
ranging from 60 to 80 miles, and an area of nearly 14,000 square miles - approximately equal to
that of Massachusetts, Rhode Island, and Connecticut combined. Lying between Japan and the
Philippines, the most northeasterly point of Formosa is about 350 miles west by south of Okinawa,
and its southernmost tip is 225 miles north of Luzon. Due eastward, the nearest land is Iwo Jima,
some 1,000 miles distant; and southeastward, 1,500 miles away, lies Guam. In the straits, about 25
miles from the coast of Formosa, is a cluster of low-lying, storm-swept islets, the Pescadores. These
islands historically have been a political appendage of Formosa. With an aggregate area of 25
square miles, they are important only because of their strategic position. The Japanese during their
occupation maintained a naval station there.
About two-thirds of Formosa is mountainous, and 42 per cent of the entire surface is of an
elevation exceeding 500 meters (1,640 feet). Parallel mountain ranges run longitudinally along the
eastern part of the island, reaching their highest elevations in Mt. Morrison, 13,075 feet, and Mt.
Sylvia, 12,972 feet. Seventy-seven other summits of the central range approximate or exceed a
height of 10,000 feet. The eastern part of the island is generally too rugged for extensive cultivation.
Most acres or 23 per cent of the total area, lies along the western slopes and in the coastal plain
stretching toward the straits.
Formosa has a coastline of more than 1,000 miles. Characteristic features of the west coast are
wide, dune-covered beaches and long stretches of mud ats exposed to tidal changes. The sea is so
shallow that sea-going vessels have to keep almost out of sight of land and can nd ingress only at a
few river mouths. These approaches, moreover, are often choked with sand bars on the east coast;
while depth permit ships to come in close to shore, the mountains are a barrier to penetration of the
island.
Formosa has only three natural harbors: Keelung in the north, formed by a deep indentation in
the coast and protected except from direct north winds; Suao in the northeast, which is somewhat
remote from the main centers of population; and Kaohsiung in the southwest, where the harbor is
formed by a long lagoon. The Tamsui River is navigable for small junks as far as the capital, Taipei,

Formosa is bisected by the Tropic of Cancer, and the climate at sea level is tropical or

resources are not abundant. The most valuable are coal and gold. A more thorough exploration of
the wild eastern section may yield further discoveries. Another resource probably capable of
substantial further development is water power.
Ethnographically, Formosa is roughly divided into longitudinal strips corresponding to the
topographical divisions. A major lowland strip capping the head of the island in the north and
following the line of the foothills to the southern tip, together with a narrow lowland strip paralleling
the east coast between the central massif and the coast range, are densely populated by people of
Chinese origin. A major highland strip along the central massif and a minor highland strip along
the east coast range are sparsely inhabited by primitive aborigines.

III. Early Settlement of Formosan
Formosa’s earliest inhabitants came from the Malay-Indonesian-Philippine area in prehistoric
times. Their descendants are today’s 170,000 tribal aborigines, most of whom inhabit the central
mountains, although some have been assimilated into coastal villages and a few live near Taipei or
Sun-moon Lake, where their settlements are a tourist attraction. Nationalist of cials consider the
aborigines the only “Formosan natives.”
Chinese and Japanese pirates and sailors presumably stopped off at Formosa earlier, but the
rst known permanent Chinese settlement was in the sixteenth century near the site of Hainan at
about the same time Portuguese sailors exclaimed “Ilha Formosa”—island beautiful.
In the age of European exploration and colonization, the Spanish and Dutch were also
attracted by Formosa’s strategic location in the sea lanes between Southeast Asia, China, and Japan.
“There was no of cial with whom he (the Dutch captain) could confer,” according to one historian,
“nothing to suggest Chinese authority on the island……” For about forty years thereafter, the
Dutch East India Company exploited the island’s southern coast near their base at Tainan, holding
tenuous authority over 25,000 farmers who had migrated in the previous century from the Fukien
and Canton coast of China and perhaps 75,000 tribesmen.

mainland. Eighteen years later, in 1662, the son of a famous Ming pirate led his refugee band across
the Formosa Straits, expelled the Dutch, and made Formosa his personal kingdom. This son of a
Chinese pirate and a Japanese mother is known in history as Koxinga and is revered for different
reasons by Communists, Nationalists, and Formosan natives.

V. Formosa Under Japanese Rule
The Sino-Japanese War of 1894-1895 was fought for control of Korea and southern
Manchuria rather than Formosa, where no battles were fought. China was more concerned with her
continental tributaries than with three million unruly islanders across that Taiwan Straits; and when
the Treaty of Shimonoseki was negotiated, Chinese Imperial Viceroy Li Hung-chang ceded
Formosa and the Pescadores to Japan.
Nowadays, Formosan memories of Japanese colonialism have become intertwined with the
more recent experience of Chinese Nationalist administration. Some observers think that only the
more literate of the Formosans retain a favorable image of Japan and that the vast majority of
farmers and youths have been in uenced to a much greater degree by postwar Nationalist
indoctrination. The Formosans, however, regardless of age, social class, or other differences, all cited
the comparative virtues of Japanese rule—not because they were pro-Japanese, but to emphasize
their dislike for the Nationalists.
In retrospect, Formosans appeared to have realized that the half-century of Japanese rule had
prepared the ground for a genuine sense of national unity. It had provided the rst effective islandwide administration, substituted comparatively modern education for old superstition, cut off most
ties with mainland China, raised standards far above those on the turbulent Asian continent, and
encouraged a cash-crop economy with per capita foreign trade 39 times greater than China’s and
higher than that in Japan itself.

I. FORMOSA IS NOT CHINESE
Formosa became a Japanese territory as a result of the Sino-Japanese For in 1894-1895. For
roughly half a century, until Japan’s defeat in the Second World War, Formosa was under Japanese
rule. This period coincided with a time of confusion and chaos in China. War, civil strife, and
colonialism served to intensify China’s backwardness; but Formosa by contrast enjoyed relative
social stability and economic well being. Improved methods of cultivating rice and sugar cane were
devised, and island achieved a certain degree of industrialization. Formosa escaped destruction in
World War II, and at the war’s end the Formosans enjoyed better living conditions than the people
of devastated Japan.
When the Chinese Nationalist landed on the island in the wake of the Chinese Civil War, the
Formosans with considerable justi cation regarded Chiang’s troops as morally and socially inferior
and Chiang Kai-shek’s government as authoritarian and corrupt. The 14 million islanders, who are
mostly of Chinese origin, outnumbered the newly arrived “pigs” loyal to Chiang Kai-shek by a ratio
of 8-1. In 1947, the ‘’Two-Two-Eight Incident” occurred; riots started in Taipei and spread
throughout the entire island. Chinese reinforcements were hastily dispatched from the continent,
crushed the rebellion; and the Formosans still harbor a deep distrust of the Nationalist Chinese.

II. In A Danger Of Being Bargained Away
As a result of the ideologies cultivated by the Nationalist regime, the Formosan people have
been psychologically “disarmed”. Their sense of identity is obscured, and they are uncertain as to
what strategies to use in struggling for status. The police control of the Nationalist government has
deprived them of organized military strength and political organization. Under the term of the
“emergency order” enforced since 1949, there is no freedom of speech, assembly, association or
peaceful petition. A potential opposition party that promised to re ect the demands and interests of
native Formosans was crushed even before it was organized, and a principal initiator, Lei Chen, was
sentenced to ten years of imprisonment for sedition. The only in uential Formosan newspaper,
headed by Li Wan-chin, was taken over by the government when Li also played a part in the
attempt to organize a constructive opposition party.
In order to prevent organized armed revolt, a substantial percentage of Formosan draftees are
sent to Quemoy and Matsu -- a hundred miles away from Formosa. Formosan soldiers stationed
inside Formosa are denied access to ammunition. By preventing the Formosan people from
becoming effectively organized and armed, the Nationalists have been able to keep them in
subjection. As every political analyst knows, it is extremely dif cult in the contemporary world to
initiate a successful, popular revolution without the support of armed forces. In Formosa key
military positions are occupied exclusively by Nationalist generals. Native Formosans, on the other
hand account for more than 80 percent of the rank and le of the Nationalist armed forces. Hence,
it is not altogether certain when the balance of military strength would be during a crisis.

III. The Victims in the Myth of Formosa’s Economic Progress
As for wealth, the regime has amassed substantial resources by their economic policies and by

In talking about Formosa’s economic progress, the land reform program quickly occupies the

deprivation happened to be law-abiding Formosans. (Aside from bene ts to tenant farmers, one

result of the market operation of supply and demand, but one of administratively determined price
set by the government, and obviously disadvantageous to farmers.

IV. Under a Permanent “State of Siege”

VII. Dissatisfaction Among Young People
Under such a government and such a system, the younger generation are generally underemployed or inadequately employed. As many Formosans see it, their job opportunities have been
blocked by thousands of “unproductive Chinese” from the mainland who ought to have been
retired long ago.
Dissatisfaction among college graduates is most serious and prevalent. Many of them have
been compelled to accept jobs far beneath their capabilities. For thousands of college graduates,
their last hope is to study abroad—notably in the United States, Japan, Canada, and more recently
in Western Europe. Many of them leave Formosa with a sense of desertion; but though they have
few illusions about the hardships that may be encountered in foreign lands, they simply cannot
conceive that their exile abroad could be more miserable than life in Formosa under Nationalist
regime’s control. The regime has wisely left this door open as a safety valve for the dissatis ed.
In any event, there are substantial grounds for believing that many dissatis ed elements are
generating in Formosa today. On the one hand, as a consequence of monopoly control over the
armed forces, police, party organization, national treasury and national enterprises, government
machinery, and the youth movement, the Nationalist Chinese have created a misleading appearance
of calm. On the other hand, as a consequence of accumulating political inequality, discrimination,
and economic deprivations, a keen sense of “intolerable deprivation” has spread among the people
who have no minimum assurance of “life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.” Their indignation
is most strikingly aroused in their military service, where the division between mainland Chinese
and native Formosans is so clear-cut.
In such a background and situation, naturally people raise a wish to get out of the miserable
political and military control, particularly among the younger generation. Of course, there are some

I. Refugees In A Modern Exodus
After realizing the political and economical situation existing in Formosa nowadays, as
previously discussed in the prior chapter, we shall have no dif culty in understanding why there was
practically no Formosan in the United States before World War II, why in the last two decades
nearly two thousand Formosan students came to the U.S. yearly for their graduate studies and why
only less than 5 per cent of them return to Formosa upon completing their graduate studies.
An enquiry made by the Institute for International Education revealed that 30 per cent of the
Asian students desired to remain in the United States but that 100 per cent of Formosan students
wanted to stay.
It is important to keep in mind that Formosans in the United States come from such
extraordinary backgrounds. Strictly speaking, the Formosans in the United States, unlike emigrants
from other countries, are refugees.
An emigrant is one who on his own decides to leave his country and goes to another with the
intention of becoming its citizen; whereas, a refugee is one who seeks asylum outside of his own
country in order to escape whatever predicament (usually political in nature but occasionally
economic, religious or even occupational) he could not escape at home. He has every intention of
returning to his own country as soon as the situation permits and has no intention, at the outset at
any rate, to become a citizen, which gives him asylum.
The main reason for the “exodus” (estimated 2,000 annually) from Formosa to the United
States can be summed up in one word — PANIC. It is a panic created by the Nationalist Chinese as
sketched in previous chapters.
It is very important to recognize the psychological differences that exist between the Formosan
refugees and the Chinese who come from Formosa. Americans are likely to identify both groups as
“Chinese.”
In the United States today, there are perhaps more mainland Chinese students than native
Formosans, although the number of the latter recently has increased substantially. Their
perspectives regarding the future of Formosa are strikingly distinct.
Mainland Chinese students do not identify Formosa as their home even though for the most
part they have been reared and educated in Formosa. When they leave Formosa for the United
States, they generally have a long-term plan. For them, what the future holds for China (or Formosa)
is uncertain, and they take the view that an individual can do little to change the course of China’s
future. The future will take care of itself; therefore, there is little rational ground for an individual to
pursue an uncertain path full of hazards. What counts is to live today with a decent income and an

assurance of permanent residence in the United States (or even better, with U.S. citizenship).

Engineers and scientists are the most well off among the aliens living in the United States. Many

and prepare for the future with increasing experience and skill and with some personal wealth
accumulated by conscious saving.
Should Sino-U.S. relations become normalized and mainland China once again become a
desirable place in which even those Chinese from “reactionary elements” or with “capitalist
indoctrination” can feel secure, they probably would go back to the mainland. They have been
patiently waiting for the day they can join their brothers on the mainland in building up a “new

can prevent China from reasserting its proud and rightful place under the sun. While at the moment
the regime “on the other side of the Straits” happens to be Communist and totalitarian in every
sense of the word, they expect this phase to pass as China eventually evolves into a community more
tolerable than it appears to be now. Hence, their immediate concern while in the United States is to
equip themselves without getting frustrated by the tide of world events, which are beyond their
immediate comprehension or control. As for the future, it belongs to those who prepare for it.
The native Formosans are in a somewhat different position. Impressionable as they are, they get

become reconciled to the idea of staying in the U.S. for the time being at least.

B. Formosans in Los Angeles
As mentioned previously, there were practically no Formosan immigrants in the United States
before World War II. Because of political panic, the number of Formosan college graduates
entering the States for advanced study have been increasing in recent years. There are about 10,000
students, engineers, doctors and other professionals in the United States presently. A majority of
them choose to stay in this country because of the abundant opportunities available and the
democratic freedom they enjoy so much.
It is estimated that there are about 1,000 Formosans scattered in Metropolitan Los Angeles.
This is an estimated 300 percent increase over the past ve years and is expected to double in the
next three years. They are young (average age is below thirty- ve), well educated (most of them have
or are working on their masters or Ph.D. education), highly motivated and extremely talented. Due
to the political situation, most of them are permanent residents with the intention of becoming
citizens, for they see no hope in returning. The reasons for such a Formosan community church are
as follows:
1. Sociologically.
Although all Formosans arrive with Chinese passports, they stick together according to
their dialects and differentiate themselves against either the Cantonese-speaking people or
the Mandarin-speaking people. On the other hand, they feel that assimilation with the
American culture is not possible with their cultural and language barriers and tend to be
socially introverted. Under such a condition, therefore, there is expectation that the church
will provide the only social activity center.
2. Politically.
As mentioned before, because of the differences in the political interests of the ruling
and the ruled, the opportunities in Formosa are very limited for Formosans after studying in
the United States to contribute their skills and knowledge. The reason is more political than
any other that there are more Formosans than any other nationalities who elect to stay in this
country in recent years.
3. Theologically.
The Presbyterian Church of Formosa is experiencing a severe brain drain, for they keep
sending their well-trained, young college graduates to the United States and seldom see them
return to their home churches. While on this side of the Paci c, there is practically no ethnic

II. Organizing The Formosan Community
A. Spirits Of Mission
The church is an organization in which a group of people can touch one another’s heart and
feel that they are one in Holy Spirit. The church is a living body in which people can be touched by
the proclamation of the Gospel and be sent to serve the world. In this spirit the Formosan church in
Los Angeles is organized to carry out the following missions:
To regroup the Formosan Christians who have been drifting away from the church and
Christian faith simply because there is no church paying due attention to them and answering their
special needs;
To function as a socio-cultural center for the people coming from the Orient and to provide
services which are pertinent to their needs, such as immigration, employment, health consultation,
recreational activities, etc.;
To witness the Christian faith among fellow non-believer Formosans, who are still the great
majority. The challenge of distributing the Gospel of Faith, Hope and Love to more Formosans
seems more urgent in this land and at this hour. An ethnic church can approach these people more
effectively and answer their needs more appropriately.
The Formosan Christian Church in L.A. is primarily concerned with people and not real
estates or facilities. The role of the minister and the church will be taken as mainly to serve these
socially and politically insecure people. Imagine that to these Formosans this time in history is
comparable to the pilgrims of the pioneer era. To the newcomer, direction in orientation,
information, introduction, comfort, and encouragement are seriously needed in order to live and

A group of 20 to 30 were already meeting every other Saturday night rotating among the

In August 1970, Reverend Jonah Chang of the United Methodist Church in Fresno, California,

support was available. Through the assistance of Centenary United Methodist Church and its

Methodist Church until they moved to Wilshire Crest Presbyterian Church in July 1971.

I. Search For A Cultural Integration In The United States
A. Historical Shadows
Napoleon once mused that China was a “sleeping giant” and advised Europe to let the giant
sleep on. He was among the rst, but by no means the last, to warn of the much exaggerated danger
that has long haunted the Western people — the “Yellow Peril.” Fear of the Oriental peoples has
grown stronger in the 20th Century. The fear is derived from racism, from memories of invasions
from Asia, and from terror of the unknown. The fear is largely unfounded; but it is a signi cant
force.
In the meantime, historically immigration of Oriental people into the U.S.A. began as
“contract labor” (Chinese during gold-rush era and Japanese in the early part of this century), and
as such they were not expected to be integrated into American society. It is quite accurate to say that
they were expected to remain “alien” to American society “forever and ever.” This stand of the U.S.
public towards Oriental peoples was legalized under the Oriental Exclusion Act of 1924 and

fi

fi

fi

fi

remained unchanged until it was rescinded by the Walter-McCarran Act of 1952. This means that

historically public racial discrimination against Orientals as well as Negroes and American Indians
existed and probably to some degree the discrimination still exists today.
Although, as we have mentioned, there were practically no Formosans in the United States
before World War II and furthermore they have nothing to do with “Pearl Harbor,” “Yellow Peril,”
“Japanese economic invasion” or “Chinese Communist’s threat,” they are still classi ed as Oriental
-- and indeed they are Oriental. It is inevitable that they have to share all kinds of problems and
dif culties of integrating themselves into the American culture. In the meantime, we must realize
that there are a great deal of differences among Orientals in their stance on the issue of integration.
We cannot be of real service to the Formosans without recognizing their unusual background.

B. Reference Groups and Their Americanization
The term immigrant, which Americans use in a more or less blanket-like fashion, falls in at
least four different categories. The four categories are as follows:
1. Emigrant is he who on his own decides to leave his country and who goes to another with
the intention of becoming its citizen.
2. Migrant is one who temporarily leaves his country to go abroad but is planning to return
to his own country when he achieves his objective of going abroad.
3. Refugee is he who seeks asylum outside of his own country in order to escape whatever
predicament (usually political in nature, but occasionally economic, religious or even
occupational) he could not escape at home. He has every intention of returning to his own
country as soon as the situation permits and has no intention, at the outset at any rate, to
becoming a citizen, which offers him an asylum.
4. Displaced person is he who by circumstance has been forced out of his country and
invited by another country as its guest.
All of them, irrespective of their original intention, inevitably undergo the process of
acclimatization, acculturation and indigenization however much they may be discriminated against.
But the emigrant is more likely to make deliberate efforts to accelerate this process while the other
three may resist it as long as possible. To put it bluntly, although they may all be looked upon as
“immigrants,” the emigrant is the only one to whom the U.S.A. is the sociologist’s so-called
“reference group” from the beginning. To the other three, the reference group remains their old
country.
The immigrant from Formosa is a refugee in the proper sense of the term who has left Formosa
and crossed the Paci c Ocean in order to escape not only because of the political panic situation but
also because of the economic condition. Most Formosan refugees are highly trained in one

fi

fi

fi

fi

fi

fi

fi

professional or technical eld or another and nd it extremely dif cult to nd satisfactory

employment in Formosa. Due to the long-time disappointment, they nally decide to go to the U.S.
This certainly is a motivation for emigration.
Compared with early immigrants from China, Japan and other countries, present day
Formosan immigrants feel much closer to the United States. Some of the basic reasons can easily be
discerned. Formosan immigrants come to this country with a B.A. or B.S. degree. The United States
itself has functioned inside Formosa in the form of foreign aid to military, culture (music, lms,

obliterated the geographical distance and to a great extent even the socio-cultural distance between

D. Internal Integration And Cultural Harmony
In the process of cultural harmony, one should not ignore or dispute the value of
differentiation of each culture. Without differentiation, there is no integration. Modern
psychologists have concluded that individuality is the result of differentiation. Jung’s
“individuation,” Maslow’s “self-actualization,” Allport’s “functional autonomy” and the emergence
of the ego in Freud’s personality theory are concepts of describing the process of differentiation in
human growth. They have the idea of individuality in common. This concept not only explains
human growth but cultural integration as well. To be integrated is to become an independent,
autonomous culture. Erickson’s “ego-identity” and “integrity” have a strong cultural, individualistic
connotation also. Erich Fromm’s positive view of self-love is signi cant in the understanding of
individuality as a necessity of integration. This individuality is well expressed in Paul Tillich’s
“courage to be oneself.” It is embodied in the Oriental philosophy in the Confucian “ethical
individual” through self-cultivation, in the Hinayana Buddhist arhatship, and in the basis for the
Zen Enlightenment.
However, cultural differentiation needs to be accompanied by integration. From the dynamic,
functional point of view, “integrity” signi es the degree of integration. So often, “integrity” is
understood as consistency of values in one’s moral behavior in a static sense. If one replaces his old
values with the unconventional, he is regarded as losing his integrity. This understanding of integrity
is likely to regard integration as a state, rather than as a process.
In this study of Oriental immigrants in an Occidental culture, “integrity” should be seen from
the functional, process-oriented point of view. This process-oriented view of “cultural integrity” is
congenial to the Freudian harmony between the ego, the superego, and the id in the normal,
healthy person. It means without integrity there is no individuality in the true sense, for such
selfhood is divided.
This integration of the individual person is better understood as the internal harmony because
the word “Harmony” does not have the implication of rigidity. A mature person is not rigid though
he is internally integrated around a culture. He is exible to make necessary adjustments to his
environment. Although he wants to maintain his identity which has come from his past experiences,
he is capable of accepting new experiences, digesting and absorbing them, and integrating them
into a new identity. This capacity for exibility is the strength of the new immigrant in his struggle
for survival in a foreign land.
It takes real courage and stamina for a young person to leave the safety and support of his
family and culture to go to a strange and distant country to study with an intention of staying there
for a long period. When he studies abroad, he must necessarily compete with his host country’s
nationals playing the academic game on their own ground and by their own rules. He must adapt
himself to their educational pattern, work in their language, live on their kind of food, conform to

The internal integration is the source of strength for a new immigrant to cope with tension and

harmony and vice versa.
From this, we can see the ideal personality for immigrants in a foreign land is the one which is
internally integrated himself and externally in harmony with people in the new land. However, the
polarity of the self is not enough; the polarity of the people of the new land has to be taken into
consideration in order that the immigrants mature and live with other people. One’s internal

I. Colonialism and Alienation According To Formosan Experience

Colonialism is not a new situation. It is indeed very old. In the past, colonialism was so
successful that it was able not only to maintain an oppressed man but an oppressed consciousness as
well. An oppressed consciousness is that which is domesticated by the situation in which it nds
itself. It is a consciousness which really becomes re exive, unable to be the subject, deprived of a
sense of direction and of historical vocation. Dominated by re exiveness, this consciousness could
not speak. Unable to enter in critical dialogue with their environment, as a consequence of the
relationship of domination which oppressed them, these societies became “mute”.
Fortunately, however, a remarkable change occurred in the Formosan people’s selfunderstanding. Like the self-awareness of the Third World and the Black man in the United States
of America, Formosan became aware that although to live without-freedom was terribly painful that
was not the most basic of their problems. They came to realize that they were not simply withoutfreedom. They were made without-freedom. Their without-freedom was the consequence of a
colonial relationship in which the powerful dominated and controlled the lives of the weak and
powerless. To Formosans, colonialism is not just a situation of the past. It is intrinsic to the relations
between ruler, Chiang, and ruled, Formosans, and indeed the cause of without-freedom of the
Formosans. But more than that, colonialism came to be seen as a relationship in which those who
are dominated are not allowed to become the creator of their own history. They are reduced to a
situation of re exiveness. This means that the lives of the colonial groups are not planned according
to their needs, but according to the needs of the politics of the ruling group, the KMT. Thus, the
lives of Formosans became, then, sub-systems which always reacted according to the stimuli of the
masters without ever being able to change the relationship of domination under which they were.
Without-freedom accordingly began to be understood not in temporal but in relational terms, as
without-freedom, as the result of a relationship whereby the powerful and free dominate the
powerless and without-freedom.
By Formosan experience, their past was the history of their impotence under the domination
of foreign powers. They could not remember when they had been the subjects of their own history.
The only thing that their memory contained was the presence of foreign powers which forced them
to the situation of re exiveness. As far as they could remember, their future had been imposed and
not freely chosen. They had never been the creator of their own history but rather the object of
someone else’s history.

III. The U.N.’s Expulsion of Nationalist Government and Formosa’s Future

A. Hope Is Possible
We human beings, who need hope more than anything else in life, have written little about it.
Dr. Karl Menninger, one of the most distinguished psychologists, notes humorously that even in the
Encyclopedia Britannica there are many columns on love, and still more on faith, but not even a list
on hope. As a matter of fact, not only do psychologists seldom touch on the subject, theologians
have also stayed away from “Hope” until the recently begun Hope Movement.
Hope has, apparently, a bad reputation; few people want to have anything consciously to do
with it, much less write about it. In the book Image of Hope, William Lynch suggests that there is a
supreme irony going on around about it. He says:
“… for many people hope really means despair. As we use language, when we say that a man has
hope, we mean that he is in serious trouble. When we say that someone has hope, we usually imply
that he has nothing else, and that he is close to despair.”
Hoping is a process, both emotional and intellectual, of image, of a future in which our
possibilities might come to fruition. William Lynch provides a very suggestive de nition on hope:
“I de ne hope… as the fundamental knowledge and feeling that there is a way out of di culty,
that things can work out, that we as human persons can somehow handle and manage internal and
external reality, that there are solutions in the most ordinary biological and physiological sense of that
word, that, above all, there are ways out of illness.”

“What we are already saying is that hope is, in its most general terms, a sense of the possible, that

B. Hope Is Adventure
In his scholarly work Homo Viator, Gabriel Marcel often speaks of hoping, the verb, and not
hope, the noun. I think this is a rst step in the study. Hoping is a process; a psychic activity of the
person. It is not just an idea but a real, live occurrence in a concrete and knowable setting. In what
kind of setting does it occur? Hoping occurs, according to Marcel, when a person is visited by a
calamity. Only when a person feels caught can he hope. If everything goes perfectly well, hoping
cannot enter the picture. In order to hope one must rst have a sense of captivity, of being caught
by the human condition, in the platonic sense of the word. The less life is felt as captivity, the less is
a person susceptible to hope.
We note the increase in despair, of un-hope, among some Formosan people in the United
States, especially after Nixon took his step on New China Policy. While working with Formosan
people in Los Angeles areas as my level IV project of San Francisco Theological Seminary, I tried to

Oriental immigrant in a big city in this country, hopelessness is also illustrated in the uneasiness and

In hoping, one lives life as an adventure indeed.

C. Hope Is Sharing

A. Hope and Christian Faith
Hope is a psychic concomitant to life and growth. When hope has gone, life has ended, actually
or potentially. Hope is an intrinsic element of the structure of life, the dynamic of man’s spirit. It is
closely linked with another element of the structure of life, Faith. Faith is not a weak form of belief
or knowledge; it is not faith in this or that; faith is the conviction of the not yet proven, the
knowledge of the real possibility, the awareness of pregnancy. Faith, like hope, is not a prediction of
the future; it is the vision of the present in a state of pregnancy.
In Hebrew the word “faith” (emunah) means certainty. Amen means certainly. It is certainty
about the reality of the possibility — but it is not certainty in the sense of the unquestionable
predictability. This is a paradox of faith: It is the certainty of the uncertain. It is certainty in terms
of man’s vision and comprehension; it is not certainty in terms of the nal outcome of reality. We
need no faith in that which is scienti cally predictable, nor can there be faith in that which is
impossible. Faith is based on our experience of living, of transforming ourselves. Faith that others
can change is the outcome of the experience that I can change. Hope is the mood that accompanies
faith. Faith cannot be sustained without the mood of hope, and hope can have no base except in
faith.
To love means to commit oneself without guarantee, to give oneself completely in the hope
that our love will produce love in the loved person. Love is act of hope and whoever has no hope
also has no love. Accordingly, hope keeps love alive. Hope opens the freedom for love to express
itself completely, to take on pain and suffering; and suffering presupposes love. He who does not
love does not suffer. One can avoid suffering by not attaching oneself to anything, by making oneself
insensitive to happiness and pain. All things and conditions and life relationships then become
replaceable and exchangeable. Only when one loves something or someone, does this thing or
person become irreplaceable. At this point, one becomes open to injury, vulnerable, and the
possibility of suffering develops.
If we attempt to overcome suffering by the creation of a culture of non-attachment, or of
attachment, which are loose and may at any time be dissolved, and create a consumer situation of
the replaceability of all things, then pain can indeed be avoided; but the frustration of life,
emptiness and boredom, ensues.

C. Hoping Community
The primary reality of human consciousness is not the individual but the community; the
individual person develops creativity only in the context of a community. Such a community, to be
healthy, must respect the uniqueness of each person. Each person, to be healthy, must respect his
brotherhood with others. Community and person are interrelated and cannot be understood apart
from one another.
For a human being, the search for identity and the search for community are one and the same.
A man discovers who he is both in solitude and in the company of others. A community is a
brotherhood, both of mutual reconciliation and mutual criticism. To love, to hope, and to faith, men
must rst nd themselves; to nd themselves, they must nd one another. Identity and community
are both found by one same decision. A decision to live in a certain way, a decision to live open to
both one’s own self and to the self of each of one’s brother.
Keep in mind this understanding of person and his relationship to community; then we can go
forward to view the church as a community of hope. William Lynch says: “Hope is an act of City of
Man, ... Hope must be some way or other be an act of a community, ... people develop hope in each
other.”
In his study of the Epistle to the Ephesians (Commenting on Ephesians 2:14), Professor Markus
Barth says that the metaphor of the broken wall points to the true meaning of the work of Christ
who has broken down every division between men. And he added: “To say Christ means to say
community, coexistence, a new life, peace.” And Dietrich Bonhoeffer has written: “There is in fact
only one religion from which the concept of community is essentially inseparable, and that is the
Christian religion.”
Indeed, Christian Church is a community, but it is not a Community of “Past”; it is not a
Community of “Now”; and it is not even a Community of “Future.” For hope is historical. Michael
Novak is right when he says, “To be a Christian is to be a member of historical people.” So
accordingly, hope is not to be mistaken for a futurism that ees from the burden and the joys of the
present. It is not an escapist trick; it is not creating a new “God of the gaps” so that we merely slip
into the future the problems we cannot solve in the present. If anything, it calls us to the front line of

From Exile to Democracy: Lessons from Taiwan's "Quiet Revolution"
The Taiwan depicted in "Toward a Community of Hope" is a society struggling to survive
under authoritarian rule, where people are forced to leave their homes in fear, seeking freedom and
hope elsewhere. However, today, half a century later, Taiwan has successfully established a thriving
democratic society through a peaceful transformation known as the "Quiet Revolution." This
transformation is a concrete realization of the ideals of "hope" and "self-determination" expressed
in the book.
Taiwan's democratization process began in the 1970s, with a series of diplomatic crises and the
rise of a new, self-aware middle class causing cracks in the Kuomintang's authoritarian rule. The
Kaohsiung Incident（美麗島事件） in 1979 led to the arrest and military trials of opposition
movement activists, yet it also sparked international attention to Taiwanese human rights, facilitated
by overseas Taiwanese churches and communities. In 1986, the Democratic Progressive Party（民
進黨） announced its establishment under party prohibition, marking the beginning of political
pluralism in Taiwan. In 1987, President Chingkuo Chiang（蔣經國） announced the lifting of the
38-year martial law, reopening political parties and the press, laying the foundation for Taiwan's
democratization.
In the 1990s, under the leadership of President Tenghui Li（李登輝）, who had a
background in the Presbyterian Church, Taiwan's democratization entered a critical phase. In 1990,
Taiwanese students initiated the Wild Lily Movement（野百合學運）, which prompted the
government to convene a national conference to consolidate various opinions on democratic
reforms.. Subsequently, the abolition of the Temporary Provisions Effective During the Period of
National Mobilization for Suppression of the Communist Rebellion, the formulation of
amendments to the Constitution of the Republic of China, the comprehensive re-election of the
National Assembly, direct elections of provincial and municipal heads, and the rst direct
presidential election in 1996 were signi cant political reforms that successfully transformed Taiwan
from a one-party dictatorship into a democratic and free nation. Entering the 21st century, Taiwan's
democracy continued to deepen, completing three transfers of power and holding national
referendums, further improving its democratic system.
The "Quiet Revolution" nature of Taiwan's democratization process lies in its avoidance of
large-scale bloodshed; this is not only the result of political elite compromises but also relates to the
historical memories of early violent suppression. The bloody lessons of the 228 Incident and the
Kaohsiung Incident likely made the Kuomintang aware of the unsustainability of continued
repression, while the opposition learned the high costs of direct confrontation. This lesson drawn
from history prompted both sides in later democratic transitions to be more inclined toward
peaceful negotiation and gradual reform, resulting in this unique "Quiet Revolution." This peaceful
transformation, in the eyes of believers, is a manifestation of "God's grace," a blessing that

international community. In 2021, the Economist Intelligence Unit (EIU) ranked Taiwan eighth in
