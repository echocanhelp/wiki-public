The **Democratic Progressive Party**[[I]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-word1-5) (**DPP**)[[II]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-abbr-6) is a [Taiwanese nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwanese_nationalism "Taiwanese nationalism")[political party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_party "Political party") in [Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan "Taiwan").[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-economist-7)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-8)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-9) As the dominant party in the [Pan-Green Coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Green_Coalition "Pan-Green Coalition"), one of the two main political camps in Taiwan, the DPP is currently the [ruling party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruling_party "Ruling party") in Taiwan, leading a [minority government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minority_government "Minority government") that controls the [presidency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Republic_of_China "President of the Republic of China") and the [central government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_the_Republic_of_China "Government of the Republic of China").

Founded in 1986 by [Hsu Hsin-liang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hsu_Hsin-liang "Hsu Hsin-liang"), [Roger Hsieh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Hsieh "Roger Hsieh") and [Lin Shui-chuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin_Shui-chuan?action=edit&redlink=1 "Lin Shui-chuan (page does not exist)")[[zh](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%9E%97%E6%B0%B4%E6%B3%89 "zh:林水泉")],[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-10)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-11) a year prior to the end of [martial law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martial_law_in_Taiwan "Martial law in Taiwan"), the DPP is one of two [major parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_party "Major party") in Taiwan, the other being the [Kuomintang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuomintang "Kuomintang") (KMT), a Chinese nationalist party previously ruling the country as a [one-party state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-party_state "One-party state"), and its smaller allies in the [Pan-Blue Coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Blue_Coalition "Pan-Blue Coalition"). It has traditionally been associated with a strong advocacy of [human rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights "Human rights"), emerging against the authoritarian [White Terror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Terror_(Taiwan) "White Terror (Taiwan)") that was initiated by the KMT, as well as the promotion of [Taiwanese nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwanese_nationalism "Taiwanese nationalism") and [identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwanese_people "Taiwanese people"). [Lai Ching-te](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lai_Ching-te "Lai Ching-te") has been [chairperson of the DPP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chairperson_of_the_Democratic_Progressive_Party "Chairperson of the Democratic Progressive Party") since 2023; he also serves as the incumbent [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Republic_of_China "President of the Republic of China") and is the [third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_presidents_of_the_Republic_of_China "List of presidents of the Republic of China") member of the DPP to hold the presidency; he succeeded fellow DPP member [Tsai Ing-wen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsai_Ing-wen "Tsai Ing-wen") in May 2024.

The DPP is a longtime member of [Liberal International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_International "Liberal International") and a founding member of the [Council of Asian Liberals and Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Asian_Liberals_and_Democrats "Council of Asian Liberals and Democrats"). It represented Taiwan in the [Unrepresented Nations and Peoples Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unrepresented_Nations_and_Peoples_Organization "Unrepresented Nations and Peoples Organization") (UNPO). The DPP is widely classified as [socially liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_liberalism "Social liberalism"), having been founded as a party for human rights, including factions within the party supporting [same-sex marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Same-sex_marriage_in_Taiwan "Same-sex marriage in Taiwan") and [other LGBT rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGBT_rights_in_Taiwan "LGBT rights in Taiwan"). On foreign policy, the DPP is more willing to increase military expenditures to prevent military intimidation from the [People's Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China") (PRC) owing to the ambiguous [political status of Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_status_of_Taiwan "Political status of Taiwan"). It favors closer ties with democratic nations such as [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan "Japan") and the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"), as well as the nations of [ASEAN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASEAN "ASEAN") as part of its [New Southbound Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Southbound_Policy "New Southbound Policy").

In its policy on [cross-strait relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-strait_relations "Cross-strait relations"), the DPP as a ruling party chooses to keep the _[status quo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political\_status\_of\_Taiwan "Political status of Taiwan")_ of Taiwan, instead of approaching _de jure_ independence, despite being a pro-[independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_independence_movement "Taiwan independence movement") group in its [party platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_platform "Party platform"). The party considers that Taiwan is [already a sovereign country under the name "Republic of China"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huadu_(Taiwan) "Huadu (Taiwan)") and not subordinate to the PRC.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-12) The DPP is frequently accused by the [PRC government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_China "Government of China") of being a primary force in Taiwan to "prevent the Chinese nation from achieving [complete reunification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_unification "Chinese unification")" and "halt the process of national rejuvenation"[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-13) due to the party's outspoken advocacy of the [Taiwanese nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwanese_nationalism "Taiwanese nationalism"), Taiwanese [self-determination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-determination "Self-determination"),[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-14)[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-15) and its firm opposition to "[One China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_China "One China")", including the alleged "[1992 Consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Consensus "1992 Consensus")" narratives by both the PRC and the KMT.

The DPP's roots were in the [_Tangwai_ movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangwai_movement "Tangwai movement"), which formed in opposition to the [Kuomintang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuomintang "Kuomintang")'s [one-party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-party_state "One-party state")[authoritarian rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarianism "Authoritarianism") under the "[party-state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dang_Guo "Dang Guo")" system during [martial law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martial_law_in_Taiwan "Martial law in Taiwan"). This movement culminated in the formation of the DPP as an alternative, but still illegal, party on 28 September 1986 by eighteen organizing members at [Grand Hotel Taipei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Hotel_(Taipei) "Grand Hotel (Taipei)"), with a total of 132 people joining the party in attendance. The new party members contested the [1986 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1986_Taiwan_legislative_election "1986 Taiwan legislative election") as "nonpartisan" candidates since competing parties would remain illegal until the following year. These early members of the party, like the _tangwai_, drew heavily from the ranks of family members and defense lawyers of political prisoners, as well as intellectuals and artists who had spent time abroad. These individuals were strongly committed to political change toward democracy and freedom of speech, press, assembly, and association.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-16)[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-17)

The _tangwai_ were not a unified political unit and consisted of factions which carried over into the early DPP. At its founding the DPP consisted of three factions: the Kang group, a moderate faction led by [Kang Ning-hsiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kang_Ning-hsiang "Kang Ning-hsiang"), [New Tide faction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Tide_faction "New Tide faction"), consisting of intellectuals and social activists led by [Wu Nai-ren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_Nai-ren "Wu Nai-ren") and [Chiou I-jen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiou_I-jen "Chiou I-jen"), and the Progress Faction led by [Lin Cheng-chieh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin_Cheng-chieh "Lin Cheng-chieh"), a _[waishengren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waishengren "Waishengren")_ opposed to independence. Moderates would later coalesce around the Formosa faction, founded by those arrested during the [Formosa Incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formosa_Incident "Formosa Incident") after their release from prison. In the early days of the party, the Formosa faction focused on winning elections by wielding the star power of its leaders, while New Tide would focus on ideological mobilization and developing grassroots support for social movements. As a result, the Formosa faction would become more moderate, often bending to public opinion, while New Tide would become more ideologically cohesive. By 1988 the Formosa Faction would dominate high-level positions within the party.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-18)

The party did not at the outset give explicit support to an [independent Taiwanese national identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_independence_movement "Taiwan independence movement"), partially because moderates such as [Hsu Hsin-liang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hsu_Hsin-liang "Hsu Hsin-liang") were concerned that such a move that could have invited a violent crackdown by the Kuomintang and alienate voters, but also because some members such as [Lin Cheng-chieh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin_Cheng-chieh "Lin Cheng-chieh") supported unification. Partially due to their waning influence within the party and partially due to their ideological commitment, between 1988 and 1991 the New Tide Faction would push the independence issue, bolstered by the return of pro-independence activists from overseas who were previously barred from Taiwan. In 1991, in order to head off the New Tide, party chairman [Hsu Hsin-liang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hsu_Hsin-liang "Hsu Hsin-liang") of the moderate Formosa faction agreed to include language in the party charter which advocated for the drafting of a new constitution as well as declaration of a new [Republic of Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Taiwan "Republic of Taiwan") via referendum (which resulted in many pro-unification members leaving the party).[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-rigger27-32-19)[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-1991article-20) However, the party would quickly begin to walk back on this language, and eventually in 1999 the party congress passed a [resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resolution_on_Taiwan's_Future "Resolution on Taiwan's Future") that Taiwan was already an independent country, under the official name "Republic of China", and that any constitutional changes should be approved by the people via referendum, while emphasizing the use of the name "Taiwan" in international settings.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-resolution-21)

Despite its lack of electoral success, the pressure that the DPP created on the ruling KMT via its demands are widely credited in the political reforms of the 1990s, most notably the [direct popular election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_popular_election "Direct popular election") of Republic of China's [president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Republic_of_China "President of the Republic of China") and all representatives in the [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_of_the_Republic_of_China "National Assembly of the Republic of China") and [Legislative Yuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_Yuan "Legislative Yuan"), as well as the ability to openly discuss events from the past such as the [February 28 Incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_28_Incident "February 28 Incident") and its long aftermath of [martial law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Terror_(Taiwan) "White Terror (Taiwan)"), and space for a greater variety of political views and advocacy. Once the DPP had representation in the [Legislative Yuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_Yuan "Legislative Yuan"), the party used the legislature as a forum to challenge the ruling KMT.

In 1996, DPP Chairman [Shih Ming-teh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shih_Ming-teh "Shih Ming-teh") united with the [New Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Party_(Taiwan) "New Party (Taiwan)") to run for the President of the Legislative Yuan, but lost by one vote. The vote that was missing was legislator [Chang Chin-cheng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang_Chin-cheng "Chang Chin-cheng")'s failure to vote for Shih. As a result, Chang was expelled from the DPP.

Post-democratization, the DPP shifted their focus to anti-corruption issues, in particular regarding KMT connections to organized crime as well as "party assets" illegally acquired from the government during martial law.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-rigger-39-49-22) Meanwhile, factions continued to form within the DPP as a mechanism for coalition-building within the party; notably, future President [Chen Shui-bian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Shui-bian "Chen Shui-bian") would form the [Justice Alliance faction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice_Alliance_faction "Justice Alliance faction").

### 2000–2008: in minority government

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Democratic_Progressive_Party&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: 2000–2008: in minority government")]

[![Image 5](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f9/Chen_Shui-bian_from_VOA.jpg/250px-Chen_Shui-bian_from_VOA.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Chen_Shui-bian_from_VOA.jpg)

Former President Chen Shui-bian, the first DPP [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Republic_of_China "President of the Republic of China") (2000–2008)

The DPP won the presidency with the [election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_Taiwan_presidential_election "2000 Taiwan presidential election") of [Chen Shui-bian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Shui-bian "Chen Shui-bian") in March 2000 with a plurality, due to [Pan-Blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Blue "Pan-Blue") voters splitting their vote between the Kuomintang and independent candidate [James Soong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Soong "James Soong"), ending 91 years of KMT rule in the Republic of China. Chen softened the party's stance on independence to appeal to moderate voters, appease the United States, and placate China. He also promised not to [change the ROC state symbols or declare formal independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noes_and_One_Without "Four Noes and One Without") as long as the [People's Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China") did not attack Taiwan. Further, he advocated for economic exchange with China as well as the establishment of transportation links.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-rigger-39-49-22)

In 2001, the DPP ran an advertisement promoting the party that featured [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler") and others in an attempt to encourage young people to engage with politics.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-23) The DPP initially responded to criticism from the Israeli and [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Institute_Taipei "German Institute Taipei") representative offices in Taiwan by saying the advertisement was meant to highlight "people who dared to express their opinions," and claimed some had deliberately misrepresented the content. The DPP's then chair [Frank Hsieh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Hsieh "Frank Hsieh") later said the advertisement would be retired to avoid causing misunderstanding among [victims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victims_of_Nazi_Germany "Victims of Nazi Germany") of [Nazism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism "Nazism").

In 2002, the DPP became the first party other than the KMT to reach a plurality in the Legislative Yuan following the [2001 legislative election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Taiwan_legislative_election "2001 Taiwan legislative election"). However, a majority coalition between the KMT, [People First Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_First_Party_(Republic_of_China) "People First Party (Republic of China)"), and [New Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Party_(Republic_of_China) "New Party (Republic of China)") prevented it from taking control of the chamber. This coalition was at odds with the presidency from the beginning, and led to President Chen's abandonment of the centrist positions that he ran his campaign on.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-rigger-39-49-22)

In 2003, Chen announced a campaign to draft a referendum law as well as a new constitution, a move which appealed to the fundamentalist wing of the DPP. By now, the New Tide faction had begun to favor pragmatic approaches to their pro-independence goals and dominated decision-making positions within the party. By contrast, grassroots support was divided largely between moderate and fundamentalist wings. Though Chen's plans for a referendum on a new constitution were scuttled by the legislature, he did manage to include a largely symbolic [referendum on the PRC military threat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Taiwanese_cross-Strait_relations_referendum "2004 Taiwanese cross-Strait relations referendum") to coincide with the [2004 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Taiwan_presidential_election "2004 Taiwan presidential election").[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-rigger-39-49-22)[President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Republic_of_China "President of the Republic of China") Chen Shui-bian would be narrowly re-elected in 2004 after an [assassination attempt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-19_shooting_incident "3-19 shooting incident") the day before the election, and in the [later legislative election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Taiwan_legislative_election "2004 Taiwan legislative election"), the pan-blue coalition opposition retained control of the chamber.

President Chen's moves sparked a debate within the party between fundamentalists and moderates who were concerned that voters would abandon their party. The fundamentalists won out, and as a result the DPP would largely follow Chen's lead. The DPP suffered a significant election defeat in nationwide [local and county elections in December 2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_Taiwanese_local_elections "2005 Taiwanese local elections"), while the pan-blue coalition captured 16 of 23 county and city government offices under the leadership of popular [Taipei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taipei "Taipei") mayor and KMT Party Chairman [Ma Ying-jeou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma_Ying-jeou "Ma Ying-jeou"). Moderates within the party would blame this loss on the party's fundamentalist turn.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-rigger-39-49-22)

The results led to a shake up of the party leadership. [Su Tseng-chang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su_Tseng-chang "Su Tseng-chang") resigned as DPP chairman soon after election results were announced. Su had pledged to step down if the DPP lost either Taipei County or failed to win 10 of the 23 mayor/magistrate positions. Vice President [Annette Lu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annette_Lu "Annette Lu") was appointed acting DPP leader. Presidential Office Secretary-General [Yu Shyi-kun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yu_Shyi-kun "Yu Shyi-kun") was elected in a [three-way race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Democratic_Progressive_Party_chairmanship_election "2006 Democratic Progressive Party chairmanship election") against legislator [Chai Trong-rong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chai_Trong-rong "Chai Trong-rong") and [Wong Chin-chu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wong_Chin-chu "Wong Chin-chu") with 54.4% of the vote.

Premier [Frank Hsieh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Hsieh "Frank Hsieh"), DPP election organizer and former mayor of [Kaohsiung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaohsiung "Kaohsiung") twice tendered a verbal resignation immediately following the election, but his resignation was not accepted by President Chen until 17 January 2006 after the DPP chairmanship election had concluded. The former DPP Chairman Su Tseng-chang was appointed to replace Hsieh as premier. Hsieh and his cabinet resigned en masse on 24 January to make way for Su and his new cabinet. President Chen had offered the position of Presidential Office Secretary-General (vacated by Su) to the departing premier, but Hsieh declined and left office criticizing President Chen for his tough line on dealing with China.

In 2005, following the passage of the [Anti-Secession Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Secession_Law "Anti-Secession Law"), the Chen administration issued a statement asserting the position that Taiwan's future should be decided by the people on Taiwan only.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-24)

On 30 September 2007, the DPP approved a [resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resolution_(law) "Resolution (law)") asserting a separate identity from China and called for the enactment of a new constitution for a "normal nation". It struck an accommodating tone by advocating general use of "[Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan "Taiwan")" as the country's name without calling for abandonment of the name Republic of China.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-25)

[Tsai Ing-wen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsai_Ing-wen "Tsai Ing-wen"), the second DPP [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Republic_of_China "President of the Republic of China") (2016–2024) and the leader of the DPP (2008–2022). Tsai is the first female leader of the DPP.

### 2008–2016: return to opposition

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Democratic_Progressive_Party&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: 2008–2016: return to opposition")]

In the national elections held in early months of 2008, the DPP won less than 25% of the seats (38.2% vote share) in the new Legislative Yuan while its presidential candidate, former [Kaohsiung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaohsiung "Kaohsiung") mayor [Frank Hsieh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Hsieh "Frank Hsieh"), lost to KMT candidate [Ma Ying-jeou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma_Ying-jeou "Ma Ying-jeou") by a wide margin (41.55% vs. 58.45%). In May, the DPP elected moderate [Tsai Ing-wen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsai_Ing-wen "Tsai Ing-wen") as their new leader over fundamentalist [Koo Kwang-ming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koo_Kwang-ming "Koo Kwang-ming").[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-rigger-39-49-22) Tsai became the first female leader of the DPP and the first female leader to lead a major party in Taiwan.

The first months since backed to the opposition were dominated by press coverage of the travails of [Chen Shui-bian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Shui-bian "Chen Shui-bian") and his wife [Wu Shu-jen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_Shu-jen "Wu Shu-jen"). On 15 August 2008, Chen resigned from the DPP and apologized: "Today I have to say sorry to all of the DPP members and supporters. I let everyone down, caused you humiliation and failed to meet your expectations. My acts have caused irreparable damage to the party. I love the DPP deeply and am proud of being a DPP member. To express my deepest regrets to all DPP members and supporters, I announce my withdrawal from the DPP immediately. My wife Wu Shu-jen is also withdrawing from the party." DPP Chairperson followed with a public statement on behalf of the party: "In regard to Chen and his wife's decision to withdraw from the party and his desire to shoulder responsibility for his actions as well as to undergo an investigation by the party's anti-corruption committee, we respect his decision and accept it."[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-26)

The DPP vowed to reflect on public misgivings towards the party. Chairperson Tsai insisted on the need for the party to remember its history, defend the Republic of China's sovereignty and national security, and maintain its confidence.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-27)[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-28)

The party re-emerged as a voice in Taiwan's political debate when Ma's administration reached the end of its first year in office. The DPP marked the anniversary with massive rallies in Taipei and Kaohsiung. Tsai's address to the crowd in Taipei on 17 May proclaimed a "citizens' movement to protect Republic of China" seeking to "protect our democracy and protect Republic of China."[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-29)

### 2016–2024: in majority government

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Democratic_Progressive_Party&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: 2016–2024: in majority government")]

On 16 January 2016, Taiwan held [a general election for its presidency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Taiwanese_presidential_election "2016 Taiwanese presidential election") and [for the Legislative Yuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Taiwanese_legislative_election "2016 Taiwanese legislative election"). The DPP gained the presidential seat, with the election of Tsai Ing-wen, who received 56.12% of the votes, while her opponent [Eric Chu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Chu "Eric Chu") gained 31.2%.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-30) In addition, the DPP gained a majority of the Legislative Yuan, winning 68 seats in the 113-seat legislature, up from 40 in 2012 election, thus giving them the majority for the first time in its history.[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-31)

President Tsai won reelection in the [2020 Taiwanese presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Taiwanese_presidential_election "2020 Taiwanese presidential election") on 11 January 2020, and the Democratic Progressive Party retained its legislative majority, [winning 61 seats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Taiwanese_legislative_election "2020 Taiwanese legislative election").

### 2024–present: return to minority government

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Democratic_Progressive_Party&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: 2024–present: return to minority government")]

The 13 January 2024 [presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Taiwanese_presidential_election "2024 Taiwanese presidential election") and [legislative elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Taiwanese_legislative_election "2024 Taiwanese legislative election") led to the election of [Lai Ching-te](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lai_Ching-te "Lai Ching-te") who won with 40.1% of the votes, while his opponents, [Hou Yu-ih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hou_Yu-ih "Hou Yu-ih") of the KMT had 33.5% of the votes, and [Ko Wen-je](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko_Wen-je "Ko Wen-je") of the [Taiwan People's Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_People's_Party "Taiwan People's Party") with 26.5% of the vote. In addition, this election made the DPP the first party to win three consecutive presidential terms since direct elections were introduced in [1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Taiwanese_presidential_election "1996 Taiwanese presidential election"). The DPP was unable to retain its majority in the Legislative Yuan, losing ten seats.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-32)[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-33)

## Ideology and policies

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Democratic_Progressive_Party&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Ideology and policies")]

The DPP's political stance is described as [centrist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrism "Centrism"),[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-38)[centre-left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre-left_politics "Centre-left politics"),[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-51) or [left-wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-wing_politics "Left-wing politics").[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-56) It is also sometimes described as "[right-wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-wing_politics "Right-wing politics")" by pro-China media outlets owing to its anti-[CCP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Party "Chinese Communist Party") stance,[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-63) but this has no bearing on DPP's actual ideology; _[Jacobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobin\_(magazine) "Jacobin (magazine)")_, a left-wing socialist magazine in the United States, has referred to the DPP as "centre-left" and mentioned it as part of "[Taiwanese Left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_independence_Left "Taiwan independence Left")".[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-Jacobin_magazine-48)

The DPP is generally described as [progressive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressivism "Progressivism").[[66]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-69) It has also been described as [liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism "Liberalism"),[[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-71)[social liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_liberal "Social liberal"),[[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-casey-72) as well as [social democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_democracy "Social democracy").[[72]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-75) The party takes a [Taiwanese nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwanese_nationalism "Taiwanese nationalism") position, advocating for strengthening Taiwanese identity;[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-anticom-39) the party opposes the [KMT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuomintang "Kuomintang") and mainland China's pan-[Chinese nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_nationalism "Chinese nationalism"), and criticizes China's claims of sovereignty over Taiwan as '[colonialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neocolonialism "Neocolonialism")' or '[imperialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_imperialism "Chinese imperialism")'.[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-Baogang_He-76) The party also defines its own identity as an "[anti-communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-communism#Taiwan "Anti-communism")"[[74]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-77) or "[anti-imperialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-imperialist "Anti-imperialist")"[[75]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-78)[[76]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-79) in the context of [opposition to the PRC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti%E2%80%93People's_Republic_of_China_sentiment "Anti–People's Republic of China sentiment") and [united front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_front_in_Taiwan "United front in Taiwan").

Programs supported by the party include moderate [social welfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_welfare "Social welfare") policies involving the rights of women, senior citizens, children, young people, labor, minorities, indigenous peoples, farmers, and other disadvantaged sectors of the society. Furthermore, its platform includes a legal and political order based on human rights and democracy; balanced economic and financial administration; fair and open social welfare; educational and cultural reform; and, independent defense and peaceful foreign policy with closer ties to the United States and Japan. The party is socially liberal[[67]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-SD-70)[[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-casey-72) and has a progressive stance that includes support for [gender equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_equality "Gender equality") and [same-sex marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Same-sex_marriage_in_Taiwan "Same-sex marriage in Taiwan") under Tsai's leadership, and also has a conservative base that includes support from the [Presbyterian Church in Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian_Church_in_Taiwan "Presbyterian Church in Taiwan").

### Stance on Taiwanese independence

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Democratic_Progressive_Party&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Stance on Taiwanese independence")]

The primary political axis in Taiwan involves the issue of [Taiwan independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_independence "Taiwan independence") versus [Chinese unification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_unification "Chinese unification"). Although the differences tend to be portrayed in polarized terms, both major coalitions have developed modified, nuanced and often complex positions. Though opposed in the philosophical origins, the practical differences between such positions can sometimes be subtle.

The current official position of the party is that Taiwan is an independent and sovereign country whose territory consists of Taiwan and its surrounding smaller islands and whose sovereignty derives only from the ROC citizens living in Taiwan (similar philosophy of [self-determination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-determination "Self-determination")), based on the 1999 "[Resolution on Taiwan's Future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resolution_on_Taiwan's_Future "Resolution on Taiwan's Future")". It considers Taiwan an independent nation under the name of Republic of China, making a formal declaration of independence unnecessary.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-resolution-21) Though calls for drafting a new constitution and a declaration of a Republic of Taiwan was written into the party charter in 1991,[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-1991article-20) the 1999 resolution has practically superseded the earlier charter. The DPP rejects the so-called "[One China principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_China_principle "One China principle")" [defined in 1992](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Consensus "1992 Consensus") as the basis for official diplomatic relations with the PRC and advocates a Taiwanese national identity which is separate from mainland China.[[77]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-92c-80)

By contrast, the KMT or pan-blue coalition agrees that the Republic of China is an independent and sovereign country that is not part of the PRC, but argues that a one China principle (with different definitions across the strait) can be used as the basis for talks with China. The KMT also opposes Taiwan independence and argues that efforts to establish a Taiwanese national identity separated from the Chinese national identity are unnecessary and needlessly provocative. Some KMT conservative officials have called efforts from DPP "[anti-China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Chinese_sentiment#Taiwan "Anti-Chinese sentiment")" (opposing migrants from mainland China, who DPP officials did not recognize as Taiwanese, but Chinese). At the other end of the political spectrum, the acceptance by the DPP of the symbols of the Republic of China is opposed by the [Taiwan Solidarity Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_Solidarity_Union "Taiwan Solidarity Union").

The first years of the DPP as the ruling party drew accusations from the opposition as a self-styled Taiwanese nationalist party, the DPP was itself inadequately sensitive to the ethnographic diversity of Taiwan's population. Where the KMT had been guilty of "Chinese [chauvinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chauvinism "Chauvinism")", the critics charged, the DPP might offer nothing more as a remedy than "[Hoklo chauvinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoklo_chauvinism "Hoklo chauvinism")".[[78]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-81) The DPP argues that its efforts to promote a Taiwanese national identity are merely an effort to normalize a Taiwanese identity repressed during years of authoritarian Kuomintang rule.

Since the democratization of Taiwan in the 1990s, the DPP has had its strongest performance in the [Hokkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwanese_Hokkien "Taiwanese Hokkien")-speaking counties and cities of Taiwan, compared with the predominantly [Hakka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwanese_Hakka "Taiwanese Hakka") and [Mandarin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwanese_Mandarin "Taiwanese Mandarin")-speaking counties, that tend to support the [Kuomintang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuomintang "Kuomintang").

The deep-rooted hostility between [Taiwanese aborigines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwanese_aborigines "Taiwanese aborigines") and (Taiwanese) Hoklo, and the effective KMT networks within aboriginal communities contribute to aboriginal skepticism against the DPP and the aboriginals' tendency to vote for the KMT.[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-82) Aboriginals have criticized politicians for abusing the "indigenization" movement for political gains, such as aboriginal opposition to the DPP's "rectification" by recognizing the Truku for political reasons, where the Atayal and Seediq slammed the Truku for their name rectification.[[80]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-83) In 2008, the majority of mountain townships voted for [Ma Ying-jeou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma_Ying-jeou "Ma Ying-jeou").[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-84) However, the DPP share of the aboriginal vote has been rising.[[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-85)[[83]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-86)

The DPP has been plagued by several [corruption scandals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_gold_(politics) "Black gold (politics)") which has increased feelings amongst voters that fraud is an acceptable practice within the party.[[84]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-87)[[85]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-China_Times_2023-12-23-88)[[86]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-89)

Former DPP president of Taiwan [Chen Shui-bian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Shui-bian "Chen Shui-bian") has also been involved in a raft of corruption scandals that continue to affect the party's reputation. Chen was sentenced to life imprisonment on corruption charges in 2009 that his supporters said were politically motivated. Chen was acquitted of embezzling millions of [New Taiwan Dollars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Taiwan_dollar "New Taiwan dollar") from a special presidential fund while he was in power, but was found guilty of money laundering and forging documents, and was given an additional two-year sentence.[[87]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-90) As Taiwan president [Tsai Ing-wen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsai_Ing-wen "Tsai Ing-wen") prepared to leave office in 2024, she declined to comment on whether or not she would pardon Chen of his corruption convictions.[[88]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-91) In the end, there was no presidential pardon delivered to Chen.

In 2024, the former DPP [vice premier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vice_Premier_of_the_Republic_of_China "Vice Premier of the Republic of China")[Cheng Wen-tsan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheng_Wen-tsan "Cheng Wen-tsan") was detained on corruption charges relating to his time as [Mayor of Taoyuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_Taoyuan "Mayor of Taoyuan"),[[89]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-92) former DPP [Tainan City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tainan_City_Council "Tainan City Council") speaker Lai Mei-hui received a 2-year suspended sentence for corruption,[[90]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-93) and prosecutors began an investigation into DPP Legislator [Lin I-chin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin_I-chin "Lin I-chin") for allegedly fraudulently claiming public funds.[[91]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-94)

In June 2025, [Kaohsiung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaohsiung "Kaohsiung") DPP representative [Lin Dai-hua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin_Dai-hua?action=edit&redlink=1 "Lin Dai-hua (page does not exist)") was indicted alongside nine others for embezzling nearly [NT$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Taiwan_dollar "New Taiwan dollar")15 million by misappropriating city funds designated as wages for her assistants.[[92]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-95)

[![Image 7](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a3/Huashan_Business_Building_20130118.jpg/250px-Huashan_Business_Building_20130118.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Huashan_Business_Building_20130118.jpg)

DPP headquarters at Huashan Business Building Level 10 in [Taipei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taipei "Taipei").[[93]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-96)

The DPP National Party Congress selects, for two-year terms, the 30 members of the Central Executive Committee and the 11 members of the Central Review Committee. The Central Executive Committee, in turn, chooses the 10 members of the Central Standing Committee. Since 2012, the DPP has had a [Department of China Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_China_Affairs_of_the_Democratic_Progressive_Party "Department of China Affairs of the Democratic Progressive Party") to deal with [Cross-Strait relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-Strait_relations "Cross-Strait relations"); the name caused some controversy within the party and in the Taiwan media, with critics suggesting that "Mainland Affairs Committee" or "Cross-Strait Affairs Committee" would show less of a hostile "[One Country on Each Side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Country_on_Each_Side "One Country on Each Side")" attitude.[[94]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-97)

For many years the DPP officially recognized several factions within its membership, such as the [New Tide faction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Tide_faction "New Tide faction") (新潮流系), the [Formosa faction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formosa_faction?action=edit&redlink=1 "Formosa faction (page does not exist)") (美麗島系), the [Justice Alliance faction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice_Alliance_faction "Justice Alliance faction") (正義連線系) and [Welfare State Alliance faction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_State_Alliance_faction?action=edit&redlink=1 "Welfare State Alliance faction (page does not exist)") (福利國系). Different factions endorse slightly different policies and are often generationally identifiable, representing individuals who had entered the party at different times. In 2006, the party ended recognition of factions.[[95]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-fractions-98) The factions have since stated that they will comply with the resolution. However, the factions are still referred to by name in national media.[[96]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-99)[[97]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party#cite_note-100)
