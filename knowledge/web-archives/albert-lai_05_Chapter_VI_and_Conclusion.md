B. Hope Is Adventure
In his scholarly work Homo Viator, Gabriel Marcel often speaks of hoping, the verb, and not
hope, the noun. I think this is a rst step in the study. Hoping is a process; a psychic activity of the
person. It is not just an idea but a real, live occurrence in a concrete and knowable setting. In what
kind of setting does it occur? Hoping occurs, according to Marcel, when a person is visited by a
calamity. Only when a person feels caught can he hope. If everything goes perfectly well, hoping
cannot enter the picture. In order to hope one must rst have a sense of captivity, of being caught
by the human condition, in the platonic sense of the word. The less life is felt as captivity, the less is
a person susceptible to hope.
We note the increase in despair, of un-hope, among some Formosan people in the United
States, especially after Nixon took his step on New China Policy. While working with Formosan
people in Los Angeles areas as my level IV project of San Francisco Theological Seminary, I tried to
translate the word “hopeless” which was so often on some lips. Persistently they used the word to
stand for “helplessness” on the one hand and “fearfulness” on the other. They were unable to
visualize a future in which personal identity and national independency were possible. As an
Oriental immigrant in a big city in this country, hopelessness is also illustrated in the uneasiness and
the estrangement of migrant life, which is often called “racial gap” and “cultural shock”.
Hoping does not merely deal with possibility, but also with the realization of possibilities. Roy
W. Fairchild remarks,

claim that such and such must happen. But because the hoping person sees reality as open-ended and
not xed, he assumes that reality has resources which are as yet undiscovered and untapped. This is his
risk and his adventure.”
In hoping, one lives life as an adventure indeed.

C. Hope Is Sharing
Often hope is considered an interior strength, some believe one possesses alone. But can one
hope as a lone individual? You can wish, or long, or hallucinate, or dream as an isolated person. But
hope is basically a sharing experience. It requires a responsive other if one is to discover real hope.
Marcel stresses that true hoping requires an interpersonal context. Isolation leads to
hopelessness and to disintegration of personality functioning. When communication between
persons fails, the isolated individual goes to pieces. For human beings, mutual help is part of the
nature of hope; hope is the anticipation of helpful relationship. Hope grows in a network of human
caring. Theologically speaking, hope is the Holy Spirit in the Christian Church. Without the
experience of the Spirit, there is no distinctly Christian understanding of hope; and even if Jesus
had spoken much about it, it would have signi ed little beyond what Judaism already understood.
He could tell his disciples to be of good courage, to look up and lift up their heads when
circumstances grew black and threatening; but a theology of hope must wait until the Holy Spirit
could take Christ’s teaching and expound it in the post-resurrection community. Be that as it may, it
is undeniably true that the Spirit has a great deal to do with Hope in the New Testament; and the

Hope is sharing. The language of hope does not accentuate action verbs, but verbs of

A. Hope and Christian Faith
Hope is a psychic concomitant to life and growth. When hope has gone, life has ended, actually
or potentially. Hope is an intrinsic element of the structure of life, the dynamic of man’s spirit. It is
closely linked with another element of the structure of life, Faith. Faith is not a weak form of belief
or knowledge; it is not faith in this or that; faith is the conviction of the not yet proven, the
knowledge of the real possibility, the awareness of pregnancy. Faith, like hope, is not a prediction of
the future; it is the vision of the present in a state of pregnancy.
In Hebrew the word “faith” (emunah) means certainty. Amen means certainly. It is certainty
about the reality of the possibility — but it is not certainty in the sense of the unquestionable
predictability. This is a paradox of faith: It is the certainty of the uncertain. It is certainty in terms
of man’s vision and comprehension; it is not certainty in terms of the nal outcome of reality. We
need no faith in that which is scienti cally predictable, nor can there be faith in that which is
impossible. Faith is based on our experience of living, of transforming ourselves. Faith that others
can change is the outcome of the experience that I can change. Hope is the mood that accompanies
faith. Faith cannot be sustained without the mood of hope, and hope can have no base except in
faith.
To love means to commit oneself without guarantee, to give oneself completely in the hope
that our love will produce love in the loved person. Love is act of hope and whoever has no hope
also has no love. Accordingly, hope keeps love alive. Hope opens the freedom for love to express
itself completely, to take on pain and suffering; and suffering presupposes love. He who does not
love does not suffer. One can avoid suffering by not attaching oneself to anything, by making oneself
insensitive to happiness and pain. All things and conditions and life relationships then become
replaceable and exchangeable. Only when one loves something or someone, does this thing or
person become irreplaceable. At this point, one becomes open to injury, vulnerable, and the
possibility of suffering develops.
If we attempt to overcome suffering by the creation of a culture of non-attachment, or of
attachment, which are loose and may at any time be dissolved, and create a consumer situation of
the replaceability of all things, then pain can indeed be avoided; but the frustration of life,
emptiness and boredom, ensues.

C. Hoping Community
The primary reality of human consciousness is not the individual but the community; the
individual person develops creativity only in the context of a community. Such a community, to be
healthy, must respect the uniqueness of each person. Each person, to be healthy, must respect his
brotherhood with others. Community and person are interrelated and cannot be understood apart
from one another.
For a human being, the search for identity and the search for community are one and the same.
A man discovers who he is both in solitude and in the company of others. A community is a
brotherhood, both of mutual reconciliation and mutual criticism. To love, to hope, and to faith, men
must rst nd themselves; to nd themselves, they must nd one another. Identity and community
are both found by one same decision. A decision to live in a certain way, a decision to live open to
both one’s own self and to the self of each of one’s brother.

The American Cultural Revolution
The period from 1968 to 1972 was the most tumultuous time in American history, the time of
the American cultural revolution, marked by the women's rights movement, civil rights movement,
anti-Vietnam War movement, counterculture movement, the assassinations of Martin Luther King
Jr. and Robert Kennedy, and the landing of American astronauts on the moon—all of which
sparked social movements that formed an unprecedented cultural revolution in American history.
This revolution caused a tremendous upheaval in American politics and society, challenging
traditional values with overwhelming force, epitomized by the slogan, "Never Trust Anybody Over
30."
The University of California, Berkeley, was the epicenter of this revolution, and SFTS was just
a bridge away. As a think tank for the church and a steering wheel for its direction, the seminary was
naturally swept into the tide of events. Against this tumultuous background, mainstream churches
and theologians around the world paid full attention to this revolution, with theologians proposing
concepts like "Feminist Theology," "Black Theology," "Theology of the death of God," "Theology
of Revolutionary," "Theology of Liberation," "Theology of love as suffering," and "theology of
hope." For me, a young seminarian from a traditionally Eastern cultural background, raised in a
relatively conservative Christian pastor’s family and educated under authoritarian rule and white
terror in Taiwan, it was like a wake-up call.
