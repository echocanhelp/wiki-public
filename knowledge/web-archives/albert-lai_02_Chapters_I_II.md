A. The Characteristics of This Book - A Fusion of Theology and History
"Toward a Community of Hope — A Mission to Formosan Community in Los Angeles" is the
doctoral dissertation completed by Dr. Albert S. Lai（賴信雄） in December 1971 while studying
at San Francisco Theological Seminary. The main purpose of this work is to discuss why evangelism

signi cance of establishing an ethnic church speci cally dedicated to nurturing Taiwanese people.
Fifty years later, the political landscape in Taiwan has undergone signi cant changes, transitioning
from an authoritarian regime to a fully democratic system. Moreover, national identity has shifted
from identifying as Chinese to Taiwanese, with only about 2% of the population still considering
themselves "Chinese." This difference makes it dif cult for contemporary readers to understand the
political developments in Taiwan in 1970 and the emotional journey of the Formosan community in

As we take the time to read this book, we gain insight into the concern of the Christian faith for
Taiwanese people in North America, while also entering the political context of Taiwan in 1970

As a doctoral dissertation from a theological seminary, its purpose should be to elucidate
Christian faith and doctrine. However, structurally, it dedicates half of its content to narrating

theological dissertation. The reason for this phenomenon lies in the fact that the target audience for

Under Chiang's military authoritarian regime, elites in various sectors found little hope for
personal career development unless they entered the party-state system. For intellectual elites, the
suppression of thought and culture by the party-state resembled a prison for the mind. Furthermore,
Taiwanese elites faced another form of torment: the erasure of their personal subjectivity. After the
Republic of China retreated to Taiwan, it maintained the institutional and ideological constructs of
its former mainland-style governance, imposing them upon the island of Taiwan. As a result,
although Taiwan was merely a province of the Republic of China, it accounted for over 99% of the
territory of the Republic of China. Public employees and representatives at various levels in Taiwan
were allocated positions based on the quotas set for the "occupied regions," with Taiwanese people
receiving less than half of the available positions. Additionally, if Taiwanese individuals wanted to
work in government or educational institutions, they were required to be pro cient in "Mandarin,"
while speaking their "mother tongue" was considered a violation
subject to punishment. Under this mainland-centric system, Taiwanese people had to deny
their own subjectivity to enter the so-called "upper" society.
Beyond systemic issues, Chiang and the party-state had considerable cultural biases against
Taiwanese people. Just before the Republic of China was to govern Taiwan, the "Taiwan Minsheng
Bao"（台灣民聲報）, organized by Taiwanese individuals, petitioned the government to
implement constitutional self-governance, but their request was denied by the party-state. During
the February 28 Incident, Chiang recorded in his diary on March 7, 1947, "This kind of Taiwanese
initially attached to us, having long suffered from the Japanese, forgot their homeland, thus they all

accurately referred to as a "settler state." Under the governance of a settler regime, the oppressed
Taiwanese people gradually felt the differences between themselves and the ruling group, which is
also the reason for the emergence of Taiwanese subjective consciousness.
Under this unequal rule, various degrees of resistance movements emerged among Taiwan's
political and intellectual elites, with some choosing to establish an independent nation. So how did
the Taiwanese independence movement unfold under the oppressive rule of the party-state? Indeed,
due to the omnipresent surveillance by the party-state, the independence movement within Taiwan
found it dif cult to develop, leading many activists to operate overseas. Japan and the United States
became important bases for the development of the movement. The historical connection to Japan
dates back to the Japanese colonial period, during which some Taiwanese had established networks

However, with the outbreak of the Korean War and the formation of a confrontation between
democratic and communist camps, the previously abandoned Chiang regime became America's ally
in combating communist forces, receiving substantial aid. With American support, Chiang's forces
were able to take root in Taiwan, turning a crisis into stability. Therefore, while the KMT could
control the culture and economy on the island of Taiwan, it could not sever ties with the United
States. Moreover, Chiang's authoritarian rule also had to consider the American response and still
needed to present itself as a democratic nation. It was precisely because the Chiang regime was still
constrained by the U.S. that it allowed for a narrow space for Taiwanese intellectual elites to
develop. In this narrow space, Taiwanese elites, unable to develop in Taiwan, sought opportunities
in the U.S., which re ects the reality of “goes, goes, goes, going to America.”

after being exposed to different political information. Immigrants who developed a sense of
Taiwanese subjectivity did not necessarily engage in the independence movement, but there were

E. Understanding Taiwanese Politics Through American Literature
With an understanding of the historical context, we can gain valuable insights from this book.
Firstly, it was written in November 1971, a critical moment when the Republic of China was
expelled from the United Nations. The development of the overseas Taiwanese independence
movement was in uenced by this event, and their discourse and actions were subsequently adjusted.
The author documents how Taiwanese intellectual elites articulated Taiwan's subjectivity before
1972. Interestingly, the book also explains why the Formosan community in the United States uses
"Formosa" instead of "Taiwan," primarily to distinguish themselves from the Republic of China's
"Taiwan" and to establish a new narrative of Taiwanese subjectivity. Therefore, this book offers
unique insights into Taiwanese identity, history, culture, and the post-war political system in Taiwan.
While some aspects of historical and cultural understanding may require revision based on
contemporary academic research, this text presents a rare historical narrative centered on Taiwan,

I. The Land -- Formosa
The island of Formosa, which the Chinese and the Japanese call Taiwan, is a link in a chain of
islands stretching from Kamchatka to the Malay Peninsula and sheltering the coastline of the East
Asia mainland from the open Paci c. Formosa is parallel to the mainland of China and is separated
from there by the Formosa Straits, which is 90 miles wide at the narrowest point. It extends from
north-northeast to south-southwest with an extreme length of 243 miles, a breadth generally
ranging from 60 to 80 miles, and an area of nearly 14,000 square miles - approximately equal to
that of Massachusetts, Rhode Island, and Connecticut combined. Lying between Japan and the
Philippines, the most northeasterly point of Formosa is about 350 miles west by south of Okinawa,
and its southernmost tip is 225 miles north of Luzon. Due eastward, the nearest land is Iwo Jima,
some 1,000 miles distant; and southeastward, 1,500 miles away, lies Guam. In the straits, about 25
miles from the coast of Formosa, is a cluster of low-lying, storm-swept islets, the Pescadores. These
islands historically have been a political appendage of Formosa. With an aggregate area of 25
square miles, they are important only because of their strategic position. The Japanese during their
occupation maintained a naval station there.
About two-thirds of Formosa is mountainous, and 42 per cent of the entire surface is of an
elevation exceeding 500 meters (1,640 feet). Parallel mountain ranges run longitudinally along the
eastern part of the island, reaching their highest elevations in Mt. Morrison, 13,075 feet, and Mt.
Sylvia, 12,972 feet. Seventy-seven other summits of the central range approximate or exceed a
height of 10,000 feet. The eastern part of the island is generally too rugged for extensive cultivation.
Most acres or 23 per cent of the total area, lies along the western slopes and in the coastal plain
stretching toward the straits.
Formosa has a coastline of more than 1,000 miles. Characteristic features of the west coast are
wide, dune-covered beaches and long stretches of mud ats exposed to tidal changes. The sea is so
shallow that sea-going vessels have to keep almost out of sight of land and can nd ingress only at a
few river mouths. These approaches, moreover, are often choked with sand bars on the east coast;
while depth permit ships to come in close to shore, the mountains are a barrier to penetration of the
island.
Formosa has only three natural harbors: Keelung in the north, formed by a deep indentation in
the coast and protected except from direct north winds; Suao in the northeast, which is somewhat
remote from the main centers of population; and Kaohsiung in the southwest, where the harbor is
formed by a long lagoon. The Tamsui River is navigable for small junks as far as the capital, Taipei,

12 miles inland. The rivers are generally short, swift, and subject to seasonal oods. Consequently,

they have wide beds, which make them dif cult to bridge. The con guration of the coast and the
topography of the land facilitate the defense of the island against external invasion by sea.
Formosa is bisected by the Tropic of Cancer, and the climate at sea level is tropical or
semitropical. At the higher elevation it is temperate. The climate is signi cantly affected by the
southwest monsoon, which blows from May until September; by the northeast monsoon, prevalent
from November until April; and by the Japan (“Black”) Current, which ows from the south and
divides, passing both the east and the west coasts of the island. The north has most of its sunny days
in the summer, and the south in the winter. February can be as raw in Taipei as in places several
degrees farther north on the eastern seaboard of the United States, while Tainan, only 200 miles
farther south, is at that season generally balmy. Rainfall decreases from north to south and from east
to west.
Forests cover about two-thirds of Formosa. At the higher elevations and in the north, the giant
cryptomeria, spruce, hemlock, pine, and mongolian oak are among the most valuable timber trees.
The island has been the world’s principal source of natural camphor. At the lower altitudes, the
forests assume a tropical character. Giant bamboos abound in the foothills, and groves of them are
found beside nearly every village. The western coastal plain and the piedmont region are well suited
for diversi ed agriculture. The slopes yield oolong tea and pineapples. With an abundance of water,
the lowlands are adapted to the growing of rice and sugar as well as of ordinary vegetables and
tropical fruits. The seas around the island teem with edible sh and shell sh. So far, proven mineral
resources are not abundant. The most valuable are coal and gold. A more thorough exploration of
the wild eastern section may yield further discoveries. Another resource probably capable of
substantial further development is water power.
Ethnographically, Formosa is roughly divided into longitudinal strips corresponding to the
topographical divisions. A major lowland strip capping the head of the island in the north and
following the line of the foothills to the southern tip, together with a narrow lowland strip paralleling
the east coast between the central massif and the coast range, are densely populated by people of
Chinese origin. A major highland strip along the central massif and a minor highland strip along
the east coast range are sparsely inhabited by primitive aborigines.

II. Brief History of Formosan
While it is uncertain when and whence the rst inhabitants came to Formosa, it is known that
the Chinese began to migrate in substantial numbers to Formosa in the late fteenth and early
sixteenth centuries.
The Chinese settlers have come for the most part from the neighboring mainland provinces of
Fukien and Kwangtung.
The Portuguese were the rst Europeans to reach the island, and Portuguese navigators early in
the sixteenth century called the island “Ilha Formosa,” “beautiful island.” During the seventeenth

In 1895 Formosa and Pescadores were ceded to Japan by the Treaty of Shimonoseki as a
consequence of the Sino-Japanese War. After suppressing resistance by Formosan elites, Japan
nally incorporated Formosa under the tent of its national administration.
In the latter part of World War II, Formosa became an important topic at some of the major
conferences. In the Cairo Declaration of 1943, President Roosevelt, Prime Minister Churchill, and
Generalissimo Chiang Kai-Shek proclaimed that it was their purpose that “All the territories Japan
has stolen from the Chinese, such as Manchuria, Formosa, and the Pescadores, shall be restored to
the Republic of China.” In the Potsdam Declaration of July 26, 1945, the three major powers—the
United States, the United Kingdom, and the Soviet Union—undertook to carry out the terms of the
Cairo Declaration. Subsequently, by the Instrument of Surrender, Japan accepted the terms of the
Potsdam Declaration and hence the obligation was embodied in the Cairo Declaration.
Collectively, the island has been governed since 1945 by the Republic of China and since 1949
as the one Chinese area free of Communist control. Of the 13.5 million inhabitants recorded by a
1968 newspaper census, over 11 million are native to Formosa and 2 million are post-1945
Nationalist settlers from the Chinese mainland. There are also approximately 170,000 aboriginal
tribesmen in the island.
