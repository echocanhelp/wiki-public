CHAPTER V
 INTEGRATION, INDEPENDENCE AND
 IDENTITY OF FORMOSAN COMMUNITY

 Up to the present point in our study, attention has been focused primarily upon the empirical characteristics of the Formosan community and the church in the community (viewed as a social institution). The rst two chapters traced the historical background of the community and its people. The third chapter illustrated the growth of a new community in this country, sketched its present psychological state and the social characteristics of its people. Chapter four brought attention to the goodwill of a group of Christians in the community developing a community church for the Formosans.
 In contrast to the preceding portion of this study, which has been primarily descriptive in the nature of the community and current states of the church, we turn now to an analysis of the church’s understanding of itself as a community of hope. In this chapter we will call attention to some selected (since it is impossible to cover all in this paper) problems facing this community and will be seeking some theological implications to ful ll the needs.
 Finally, in chapter six, the last chapter of this study, we shall consider some implications of a
Biblical-Theological understanding of the nature and mission of the church as a community of hope to new immigrants in this country in the present day and, particularly, a discovery of relevance of Christian hope to their contemporary life.

 I. Search For A Cultural Integration In The United States

 A. Historical Shadows

 Napoleon once mused that China was a “sleeping giant” and advised Europe to let the giant sleep on. He was among the rst, but by no means the last, to warn of the much exaggerated danger that has long haunted the Western people — the “Yellow Peril.” Fear of the Oriental peoples has grown stronger in the 20th Century. The fear is derived from racism, from memories of invasions from Asia, and from terror of the unknown. The fear is largely unfounded; but it is a signi cant force.
 In the meantime, historically immigration of Oriental people into the U.S.A. began as
“contract labor” (Chinese during gold-rush era and Japanese in the early part of this century), and as such they were not expected to be integrated into American society. It is quite accurate to say that they were expected to remain “alien” to American society “forever and ever.” This stand of the U.S.
public towards Oriental peoples was legalized under the Oriental Exclusion Act of 1924 and remained unchanged until it was rescinded by the Walter-McCarran Act of 1952. This means that fi fi

 historically public racial discrimination against Orientals as well as Negroes and American Indians existed and probably to some degree the discrimination still exists today.
 Although, as we have mentioned, there were practically no Formosans in the United States before World War II and furthermore they have nothing to do with “Pearl Harbor,” “Yellow Peril,”
 “Japanese economic invasion” or “Chinese Communist’s threat,” they are still classi ed as Oriental
 -- and indeed they are Oriental. It is inevitable that they have to share all kinds of problems and dif culties of integrating themselves into the American culture. In the meantime, we must realize that there are a great deal of differences among Orientals in their stance on the issue of integration.
 We cannot be of real service to the Formosans without recognizing their unusual background.

 B. Reference Groups and Their Americanization

 The term immigrant, which Americans use in a more or less blanket-like fashion, falls in at least four different categories. The four categories are as follows:

 1. Emigrant is he who on his own decides to leave his country and who goes to another with the intention of becoming its citizen.
 2. Migrant is one who temporarily leaves his country to go abroad but is planning to return to his own country when he achieves his objective of going abroad.
 3. Refugee is he who seeks asylum outside of his own country in order to escape whatever predicament (usually political in nature, but occasionally economic, religious or even occupational) he could not escape at home. He has every intention of returning to his own country as soon as the situation permits and has no intention, at the outset at any rate, to becoming a citizen, which offers him an asylum.
 4. Displaced person is he who by circumstance has been forced out of his country and invited by another country as its guest.

 All of them, irrespective of their original intention, inevitably undergo the process of acclimatization, acculturation and indigenization however much they may be discriminated against.
 But the emigrant is more likely to make deliberate efforts to accelerate this process while the other three may resist it as long as possible. To put it bluntly, although they may all be looked upon as
 “immigrants,” the emigrant is the only one to whom the U.S.A. is the sociologist’s so-called
 “reference group” from the beginning. To the other three, the reference group remains their old country.
 The immigrant from Formosa is a refugee in the proper sense of the term who has left Formosa and crossed the Paci c Ocean in order to escape not only because of the political panic situation but also because of the economic condition. Most Formosan refugees are highly trained in one professional or technical eld or another and nd it extremely dif cult to nd satisfactory fi fi fi employment in Formosa. Due to the long-time disappointment, they nally decide to go to the U.S.
This certainly is a motivation for emigration.
 Compared with early immigrants from China, Japan and other countries, present day
Formosan immigrants feel much closer to the United States. Some of the basic reasons can easily be discerned. Formosan immigrants come to this country with a B.A. or B.S. degree. The United States itself has functioned inside Formosa in the form of foreign aid to military, culture (music, lms, customs, etc.) and education. The modern means of communication and transportation have all but obliterated the geographical distance and to a great extent even the socio-cultural distance between the two countries. Thus, Formosans come to this country with a better command of the language, a more accurate knowledge of the U.S.A., with greater ease to adapt in a shorter space of time, and above all else with their minds determined to be more americanized. In the meantime, the attitude of a majority of Americans toward Orientals and Oriental culture has radically changed in recent years so that Formosan immigrants nd themselves more readily accepted by Americans.
 Before going further, the distinction between “accepted by Americans” and “to become
American” should not be confused. The former means to be accepted by Americans as a Formosan, while the latter means to no longer be a Formosan. Formosans in this country at the present stage want to be treated like an American but not to live culturally in American style. It is impossible for them to live in American style since their traditional Formosan language, customs, food, friendships and ways of life are so much in contrast with the American ways. Even though intellectually they have already been americanized, mentally and culturally they are Formosan.
 Due to the hassle of cultural adjustment and struggles in searching for identity, the Formosans in U.S. are even more Formosan than Formosans in Formosa.
 Formosans are more like refugees since their “reference group” is and remains Formosan.
Therefore, they are expatriates no matter how long they reside in the U.S.A. Permanent residency or even U.S. citizenship for many a Formosan only means “I am no longer a national of the Republic of China” rather than “I am an American.”
 This does not mean that the Formosans in the United States should be counted together with the “long-term seasonal workers” or “temporary immigrants” who have plans of staying for a few years until they have saved enough money to be able to establish themselves in their own country.
Formosans come prepared to stay for a considerable time because they know the problem of the
“future of Formosa” is not an easy knot in the hands of world politics. As the years pass by, it seems that most of the Formosans would be forced to stay in this country forever. A great number of them are already obliged to stay here for a long time, because their family, career, property, and children’s education have been established in this land. Therefore, gradually they become americanized.

 C. From The “Melting Pot” TheoryTo A Pluralistic Society

 “As each new wave of immigration has reached American, it has been faced with problems, not only the problems that come with making new home and learning new jobs, but, more important, the problems of getting along with people of di erent background and habits.
 Each new group was met by the group was met by the groups already in America, and adjustment was often di cult and painful. The early English settlers had to get along with Indians; the Irish that followed were met by these ‘Yankees,’ and so it has gone down to the recent group of Cuban and
 Hungarian refugees. Somehow, the di culty of adjustments are made and people get down to the tasks of earning a living, raising a family, living with their neighbors and in the process, building a nation.”

 The late President Kennedy stated, “Somehow, the dif cult adjustments are made.” But this
 “somehow” should be examined critically, because historically Oriental immigrants in this country were a different “kettle of sh” or were even excluded from American dignity.
 Being an Oriental, Formosans naturally learned from the integration experiences of their
 Japanese and Chinese counterpart. What has happened to the U.S. society over the years since the last century in the face of the emerging Japanese community and Chinese community? First, it rejected them though it needed them and kept them segregated, causing the development of
 Chinese and Japanese ghettos. This resulted in the U.S. society becoming a racially closed society and Oriental people being forced to remain foreigners no matter how long they lived here.
 As the years went by and Oriental people continued to stay Oriental (that is, un-americanized),
 Americans came to think that it constituted a fair enough reason to keep them segregated, which in turn intensi ed the ghettoizing tendency on their part. Thus, the vicious cycle kept on going from bad to worse, and the social distance increased from year to year. U.S. society ended up being a polarized society. The Oriental Exclusion Act of 1924 was a legislative action to nationalize and legitimatize this.
 With immigrants from Europe, on the other hand, U.S. society took a totally different position.
 It insisted that they should become amalgamated or assimilated into U.S. society just as fast as humanly possible by “becoming like us.” With this attitude prevailing in the United States, no immigrant community was given encouragement to assert its group identity or cultural integrity.
 Everybody was made to feel that he had to cease being whatever he had been in order to become an
 American. The melting-pot theory was an attempt to rationalize this with a cultural homogenization as its goal.
 Neither a racially divided and polarized America nor a culturally homogenized America is, in my opinion, truly desirable. A true great and wholesome America is one in which every ethnic group is accepted for what it is and encouraged to become an integral part of U.S. society while maintaining its own group identity -- a pluralistic U.S.A.

 D. Internal Integration And Cultural Harmony

 In the process of cultural harmony, one should not ignore or dispute the value of differentiation of each culture. Without differentiation, there is no integration. Modern psychologists have concluded that individuality is the result of differentiation. Jung’s
“individuation,” Maslow’s “self-actualization,” Allport’s “functional autonomy” and the emergence of the ego in Freud’s personality theory are concepts of describing the process of differentiation in human growth. They have the idea of individuality in common. This concept not only explains human growth but cultural integration as well. To be integrated is to become an independent, autonomous culture. Erickson’s “ego-identity” and “integrity” have a strong cultural, individualistic connotation also. Erich Fromm’s positive view of self-love is signi cant in the understanding of individuality as a necessity of integration. This individuality is well expressed in Paul Tillich’s
“courage to be oneself.” It is embodied in the Oriental philosophy in the Confucian “ethical individual” through self-cultivation, in the Hinayana Buddhist arhatship, and in the basis for the
Zen Enlightenment.
 However, cultural differentiation needs to be accompanied by integration. From the dynamic, functional point of view, “integrity” signi es the degree of integration. So often, “integrity” is understood as consistency of values in one’s moral behavior in a static sense. If one replaces his old values with the unconventional, he is regarded as losing his integrity. This understanding of integrity is likely to regard integration as a state, rather than as a process.
 In this study of Oriental immigrants in an Occidental culture, “integrity” should be seen from the functional, process-oriented point of view. This process-oriented view of “cultural integrity” is congenial to the Freudian harmony between the ego, the superego, and the id in the normal, healthy person. It means without integrity there is no individuality in the true sense, for such selfhood is divided.
 This integration of the individual person is better understood as the internal harmony because the word “Harmony” does not have the implication of rigidity. A mature person is not rigid though he is internally integrated around a culture. He is exible to make necessary adjustments to his environment. Although he wants to maintain his identity which has come from his past experiences, he is capable of accepting new experiences, digesting and absorbing them, and integrating them into a new identity. This capacity for exibility is the strength of the new immigrant in his struggle for survival in a foreign land.
 It takes real courage and stamina for a young person to leave the safety and support of his family and culture to go to a strange and distant country to study with an intention of staying there for a long period. When he studies abroad, he must necessarily compete with his host country’s nationals playing the academic game on their own ground and by their own rules. He must adapt himself to their educational pattern, work in their language, live on their kind of food, conform to their social customs, live in their kind of housing, and pay their price for the things he needs. If he fl has a rigid personality, he cannot succeed; and he obviously will not be happy in his new life. To be happy in a foreign land requires exibility and openness to a new culture.
 The internal integration is the source of strength for a new immigrant to cope with tension and frustration without losing his integrity. With the internal integration, one can be calm and serene even though under tremendous external pressures. Without this internal integration, one will be corrupted when confronted with dif culties, hardships, or cultural shocks. Paul Tillich’s “the courage to be oneself ” certainly implies this point. He who has “the courage to be oneself ” cannot simply be smashed to pieces or get completely lost in a strange culture of a foreign society.
 The source of one’s internal integration and external harmony is love which includes all its aspects, self-love, love-for-other, agape-eros- lial-together, human-heartedness, feeling of commiseration, compassion, altruism, productive love and so on. Man’s basic need is to be loved and to love others, to receive love and to give love, to care and to share. Without love, there is no sense of security. Without a sense of security, there is no trust. Without trust, there is no peace of heart. Therefore, without love, there is no internal integration, no external harmony. This shows us how signi cant the formula “God is love” is.
 With love, the internal wounds are healed and con icts resolved; and then the disintegrated person becomes whole again. With love, he nds inner peace and external harmony. With inner peace, he does not need the emotional support which comes from racial prejudice, social status, power, prestige, and material wealth. He is no longer like a small child whose sense of family superiority is often expressed to his playmates by boast that his father is greater than their former selves. He is now humble, spontaneous, and relaxed. His internal integration brings him external harmony and vice versa.
 From this, we can see the ideal personality for immigrants in a foreign land is the one which is internally integrated himself and externally in harmony with people in the new land. However, the polarity of the self is not enough; the polarity of the people of the new land has to be taken into consideration in order that the immigrants mature and live with other people. One’s internal integration needs to be accompanied by external harmony. It is integration and harmony between the two polarities, “one-in-two” and “two-in-one.” When this is seen from the immigration point of view, the American society should always be in the process of integration and harmony.
 The characteristics of American society should be expressed by the unity-in-diversity and diversity-in-unity of all its people.

 II. Search for Formosan Independence.

 The immigrants from Formosa are refugees in the proper sense of the term, as discussed in prior chapters. The “reference group” of a refugee is and remains his home country; and due to the dif culties of cultural adjustment and struggle in searching for an identity, the Formosans in the U.S.
 are even more Formosan than Formosans in Formosa. For instance, most of the Formosans in the
 U.S. begin to appreciate their Formosan folk music after they stay in the U.S. for a period. When fi fi they were in college in Formosa, they regarded folk music as “already out of current” or “second- rated music”; and they considered western music as the music (from Beethoven to the Beatles). Of course, that is due to the fact that we are living in an age in which traditional values have been challenged. They thus despise their traditional folk music as well as their other cultural heritages.
 On the other hand, it is also understandable that after they arrive in the U.S., they have to search for an identity; and from native folk music, they nd a great part of themselves.
 Not only from folk music do they try to nd their identity by no means. A good number of
 Formosans in the U.S. still have their home-town daily newspaper sent by air mail from Formosa.
 They always want to know what is happening back in Formosa, although they might never be able to return.

 A. National Independence -- Political

 It also is true on the matter of political independence movement that not all Formosan refugees are activists, but that most Formosans agree with the ideology of independence, and that they are always eager to work for it. A brief description of the Formosan Independence movement has been given in Chapter III of this study. Fundamentally, the Formosan Independence movement is a self- preservation movement of the island’s 14 million people who are willing neither to be ruled by the
 Chinese Communists nor to be destroyed by the Chinese Nationalist regime.
 To summarize the attitude of 10,000 or more Formosans in the U.S. concerning the movement, the vast majority (although 100 percent of Formosans are all in agreement with the movement) hope to remain abroad until their home island is a democratic republic safe from either Nationalist or Communist rule. Most would prefer to avoid direct confrontation with the Nationalists, because they value their own life or the security of their parents back in Formosa.
 The in ux of more young Formosans to American campuses, however, probably presages a more militant generation not so likely to be satis ed with the “politics-no-touch” attitude of their parents and school teachers. Debate over “freedom and self-determination” aspects of American policy in Vietnam and the Black power movement inside the U.S. have combined to persuade many newcomers of the importance of Formosan efforts to secure their own goals.
 Nixon’s ping-pong diplomacy and his announcement of willingness to visit Peking brings
 Formosans a mixed feeling. On one hand, Formosans are happy about it for they see this as a sign that the U.S. is willing to change its attitude on the issue of Formosa and its relationship with
 Chiang’s regime. On the other hand, Formosans are worrying; they feel that they might be “sold out” to the Communists by the U.S., since Communist China never accepted the idea of an independent Formosa. Particularly, after the United Nations admitted China and expelled Chiang’s regime, the anxiety among Formosans is greater than ever before. Formosans realize that, from now on, their enemy is not only Chiang’s regime but Communist China in addition; and the latter is almost impossible to deal with.
 fi
 Of course, predictions are always risky in the eld of international politics, perhaps especially so in the case of Formosa, where external factors are more likely than domestic developments to decide its fate. One future possibility is the conquest of Formosa by mainland China either through armed force or by peaceful merger. Another is maintenance of the status quo under Nationalist rule, with perhaps greater political freedom for the majority but no genuine Formosan independence. A third would be foreign intervention by the United Nations or a group of powers to force the
 Nationalists to hand over political control to the Formosans. We should also consider the prospect for a Formosan uprising against the mainlanders. elite, whether initiated by local group or precipitated by some external attempt to change the island’s status.
 From the United Nations’ decision this year on the so-called “Albania Solution” which ignored the 14 million Formosan people who wished for political independence, Formosans have learned an important lesson: It is unlikely that the United Nations will take any action on the future of
 Formosa. They realize that the Formosan Independence Movement will have to be exactly that — a purely native Formosan revolt against all outside domination. The task toward an independent
 Formosa ought to be carried out by Formosans themselves — through revolution than through international intervention. Self-help is the best help.
 The self-help of the Formosan people, their willingness and capacity to take destiny in their own hands, is the key to any hopeful future change. Only when the Formosan people can demonstrate to the world that Formosa’s independence is neither a personal affair of Chiang’s nor an arti cial outcome of U.S. strategy will more elements of the world community be favorably disposed to acknowledge Formosa’s new status in the world arena.
 Self-help is the best method for any national liberation movement. The real test of nationalism is a people’s willingness to ght for it. Symbolic acts to arouse the attention of the more apathetic islanders and the outside world would be a useful rst step. A demand for a freely conducted referendum on the future status of the island, such as what the British offered the people of
 Gibraltar and the U.S. held in Puerto Rico, would seem the best to use.
 It is important to reiterate that the people and territory of Formosa are fully capable of supporting a viable status. Strategically, Formosa is far more defensible than any other small nation adjoining a hostile power, such as South Vietnam, Cambodia or Laos. Politically, the 15 million natives are more educated, socially uni ed, and emotionally committed to defend their island against external threat than most of the newly-independent nations. Judged on any basis, Formosans are far better equipped to manage their own affairs than almost any group which has attained independence since 1945.

 B. Personal Independence -- Psychological

 “Independence” has been the common goal of Formosans for the past three decades. Today, it has brought them more attention than ever before.
 fi fi
 Yukichi Fukuzawa, one of the most outstanding intellectual leaders in modern Japan, said,
“When a person is independent, a nation is independent.” For Fukuzawa, an independent person and an independent nation are indivisible and interdependent. In other words, when a person can secure his own independence, a nation can secure its independence from outsider’s rule. To be independent is to govern oneself and not to be dependent upon others. “Independence” in the sense of self-support, self-government, self-determination and self-respect has two connections: On the one hand, the spiritual and intellectual independence of dehumanized Formosan men and women; on the other, a more ideological meaning — the social, political, and economic independence of individuals from the traditional hierarchical and semi-feudalistic social system and also the freedom of nation from foreign rule. For Fukuzawa, the spiritual and mental independence of man and the political independence of the nation are one, for only when independent men participate in nationbuilding can the nation itself attain independence.
 Jawaharlal Nehru, in his autobiography, quotes C.F. Andrews as an echo of the cry of the
Indian people:

 “The only way of self-recovery was through some vital upheaval from within. The explosive force needed for such an upheaval must generate within the soul of India itself. It could not come through loans and gifts and grants and concessions and proclamations from without... Therefore, it was with the intense joy of mental and spiritual deliverance from an intolerable burden, that I watched the actual occurrence when Mahatma Gandhi spoke to the heart of India the mantra ‘Be free, Be slaves no more!’ and the heart of India responded. In sudden movement her fetters began to be loosened, and the pathway of freedom was opened.”

 Lu Hsün, an outstanding modern Chinese intellectual of whom Mao Tse-tung has written,

 “Lu Hsün is the greatest saint of New China,” resisted western and all other outside in uences and sought for a truly independent spirit in the slavish and stagnant mind of the Chinese people. He said, “Only the independent spirit of the people is respectable and only when it is exalted will there be true progress in China.”

 The government of Chiang’s regime in Formosa connoted the family-state ideology of the emperor system of Old China, in which the unity of the independent person and the independent nation was lost — the independent nation with wealth and power was exalted at the expense of the independent person. A government independent from independence-minded people is totalitarian!
In one word, the goal of Formosan national independence movement cannot be achieved until or unless the fundamental personal independence has been taken to the ground.
 C. Ethnos As A Vital Dimension Of Formosan Existence

 Throughout the history of mankind the individual has always found himself a member of an ethnocultural group. He has seldom questioned the basis of the ethnos in which he was born and to which he was proud to belong. It provided him with “the homeland of his soul,” the vantage point from which he saw his universe, the perspective in which he saw other people; and the basis for his sense of importance and self-respect.
 In the more primitive eras, ethnos was basically a kinship group: The extended family, clan, tribe and the like. In the course of history such groups were consolidated to form a nation in the modern sense of the term. The growth of the nation led to the emergence of a common ethos, mores and culture among all its subgroups. Those who did not share the same culture were strangers. This cultural con ict has been described by a scholar as “xenoi over against ethnoi.”
 The problems of identity have been the subject of considerable popular interest and lately have received scienti c attention; there is a surprising dearth of social-psychological studies dealing with
“ethnic identity”; but a special study focused on contemporary Formosan ethnic has, however, still never been undertaken.
 Here we are using the term “ethnic” in a broad sense similar to that employed by Klineberg in the following de nition:

 “An ethnic group may be de ned... as one which is set o from others by inherited physical type
(or race), by religion, language, or national origin, or any combination of these.”

 It cannot be disregarded that every ethnic group has its own cultural peculiarities so that they build up their own unique identity. Formosan study should be separated from Chinese study; and their own should be established, since modern Formosan is very different from Chinese in terms of cultural experience and political interests.
 Of course, here we are not saying that Formosan study has nothing to do with Chinese (we recognize that it will be impractical to study Formosan without knowing their relationship with
Chinese), but are emphasizing that Formosan peculiarities should not be disregarded. Unless
Formosan study has gained independency, the Formosan people will always remain “lost” in that they will not recognize their own ethos and others will not identify Formosan as Formosan.
 In discussing Formosan identity, as done in preceding chapters, we cannot divide personal growth and communal change, nor can we separate the identity crisis in individual life and contemporary crisis in historical development because the two help to de ne each other and are truly relative to each other. In fact, the whole interplay between the psychological and social, the developmental and historical, for which identity formation is of prototype signi cance, could be conceptualized only as a kind of psychological relativity. In other words, identity is dynamic, has never been “established” as an “achievement” in the form of a personality armor, or anything static and unchangeable.
