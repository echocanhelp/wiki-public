CHAPTER IV
 FORMOSAN CHRISTIAN CHURCH IN LOS ANGELES

 This chapter is a presentation of the background, brief history, current status and prospect of a newly organized church in Los Angeles -- Formosan Christian Church in Los Angeles, California -- and the characteristics of the people in the community. I have spent my level IV year of San
Francisco Theological Seminary to work with this project in developing a Formosan community church in Los Angeles.

 I. Background

 A. Christianity and Formosans

 It was only six years ago that the Protestant churches in Formosa celebrated their centenary, which marked the beginning of a second century of a rapidly progressing movement in Christianity on the island of Formosa.
 Dr. James L. Maxwell of the Presbyterian Church of England commenced the rst mission on the island in 1865. This was followed by a continuous ow of missionaries from the Western World, such as England, Canada, and the United States. Their sacri ce and tireless effort made the spreading of the Gospel possible among the people who have had their rigid tradition, selfcontained culture, and conservative religious views for so many thousands of years. Now there are about 400,000 members in the Protestant churches in Formosa, which constitute about 3 percent of the island population. Although it is still a minority, the in uence of the church on the better quality of education, medical care, social welfare on the island are evident.
 Since there were practically no other Christian churches working among the islanders besides the Presbyterian before the Nationalist Chinese were defeated by the Communists and their withdrawal to the island in 1949, the Presbyterian church is the largest and oldest Christian church in Formosa. It is estimated that three-fourths of Protestant Christians on the island now are
Presbyterians.
 During World War II, all missionaries in Formosa were expelled by the Japanese government and went back to their home countries. All nancial aids from churches in Western countries naturally were completely cut off. This was a bitter and disappointing experience, but it taught them an important lesson: Formosan churches could no longer depend on Western missionaries. If they wanted to survive, they had to carry out the mission themselves. In other words, they had to be independent from foreign missionaries; and indeed, they had been.
 fl fi

 Today, the Formosan Presbyterian Church is the only nancially and administratively independent denomination on the island, although about 50 other denominations entered the island in the last 25 years.

 B. Formosans in Los Angeles

 As mentioned previously, there were practically no Formosan immigrants in the United States before World War II. Because of political panic, the number of Formosan college graduates entering the States for advanced study have been increasing in recent years. There are about 10,000 students, engineers, doctors and other professionals in the United States presently. A majority of them choose to stay in this country because of the abundant opportunities available and the democratic freedom they enjoy so much.
 It is estimated that there are about 1,000 Formosans scattered in Metropolitan Los Angeles.
This is an estimated 300 percent increase over the past ve years and is expected to double in the next three years. They are young (average age is below thirty- ve), well educated (most of them have or are working on their masters or Ph.D. education), highly motivated and extremely talented. Due to the political situation, most of them are permanent residents with the intention of becoming citizens, for they see no hope in returning. The reasons for such a Formosan community church are as follows:

 1. Sociologically.
 Although all Formosans arrive with Chinese passports, they stick together according to their dialects and differentiate themselves against either the Cantonese-speaking people or the Mandarin-speaking people. On the other hand, they feel that assimilation with the
 American culture is not possible with their cultural and language barriers and tend to be socially introverted. Under such a condition, therefore, there is expectation that the church will provide the only social activity center.

 2. Politically.
 As mentioned before, because of the differences in the political interests of the ruling and the ruled, the opportunities in Formosa are very limited for Formosans after studying in the United States to contribute their skills and knowledge. The reason is more political than any other that there are more Formosans than any other nationalities who elect to stay in this country in recent years.

 3. Theologically.
 The Presbyterian Church of Formosa is experiencing a severe brain drain, for they keep sending their well-trained, young college graduates to the United States and seldom see them return to their home churches. While on this side of the Paci c, there is practically no ethnic church prepared to edify and nurture them. There are several sectarian churches which fi fi

 provide them with the emotional needs but little with the theological nourishment relevant to their more advanced technological know-how and scienti c skills. It is ironical but it is a reality that although they may be highly intellectual in their own eld, they very often realize that they are not culturally and linguistically ready to merge into the Anglo-American churches.

 II. Organizing The Formosan Community

 A. Spirits Of Mission

 The church is an organization in which a group of people can touch one another’s heart and feel that they are one in Holy Spirit. The church is a living body in which people can be touched by the proclamation of the Gospel and be sent to serve the world. In this spirit the Formosan church in
Los Angeles is organized to carry out the following missions:

 To regroup the Formosan Christians who have been drifting away from the church and
Christian faith simply because there is no church paying due attention to them and answering their special needs;
 To function as a socio-cultural center for the people coming from the Orient and to provide services which are pertinent to their needs, such as immigration, employment, health consultation, recreational activities, etc.;
 To witness the Christian faith among fellow non-believer Formosans, who are still the great majority. The challenge of distributing the Gospel of Faith, Hope and Love to more Formosans seems more urgent in this land and at this hour. An ethnic church can approach these people more effectively and answer their needs more appropriately.
 The Formosan Christian Church in L.A. is primarily concerned with people and not real estates or facilities. The role of the minister and the church will be taken as mainly to serve these socially and politically insecure people. Imagine that to these Formosans this time in history is comparable to the pilgrims of the pioneer era. To the newcomer, direction in orientation, information, introduction, comfort, and encouragement are seriously needed in order to live and prosper in this racially and culturally alien society.

 B. Steps Towards A Church

 1. Family Prayer Meetings
 A group of 20 to 30 were already meeting every other Saturday night rotating among the
 Formosan Christian families in early 1970’s. This circle was formed out of spontaneous auspices.
 Although some of the attendants had been associated with a Chinese church -- First Evangelical
 Church -- they had intended to form their own separate church. The group chose to meet on
 Saturday nights in order to allow those associated with other churches to attend on Sundays. This prayer meeting circle made plans and held discussions before the actual organization of the church.

 2. One-Month Experimental Church.
 In August 1970, Reverend Jonah Chang of the United Methodist Church in Fresno, California, a former minister of Presbyterian Church of Formosa, started a one-month experimental church to test the feasibility of such a church group in the Formosan community. At the end of August, when
 Rev. Chang returned to Fresno and the experiment was concluded, the majority of the congregation envisioned the importance of carrying on such a fellowship among the Formosan people and decided to organize a church despite the fact that they had no pastor or that no outside nancial support was available. Through the assistance of Centenary United Methodist Church and its minister, Rev. Peter Chen, they used the facilities of the Wesley Hall of the Centenary United
 Methodist Church until they moved to Wilshire Crest Presbyterian Church in July 1971.
 3. Contacts With Presbytery and Synod.
 The Presbyterian Church is the largest and the only nancially independent denomination on the island of Formosa. Most of the members of this newly-born church were members of the
 Presbyterian Church of Formosa and are very proud of their Presbyterian heritage. Denominational loyalties among these Christians are very strong by American standards. Right from the beginning in forming this church, they seriously considered association with the United Presbyterian Church in the United States of America.
 Through a careful and continuous contact with various departments and staff at the Synod
 Of ce of Southern California and Presbytery of Los Angeles Southwest, in February, 1971, the
 Formosan Christian Church decided to join U.P.C.A. (United Presbyterian Church in United States of America) and asked their nancial and moral support for a period of time. The Presbytery and
 Congregational Development Committee of the Presbytery of Los Angeles Southwest gradually became interested in this request.
 fi fi

 III. Associate With United Presbyterian Church In U.S.A.

 The church has been in communication with the Presbytery since late fall of 1970 about becoming part of the United Presbyterian Church. The Presbytery Committee voted on March 27,
 1971, to work with the leadership of the church to try to develop a workable plan for developing within a three to ve year period a self-supporting United Presbyterian congregation, if and when such a plan can be developed, to recommend to Presbytery formal action toward organization of a
 United Presbyterian Church.
 The Request for Funding of April 14, 1971, from the Committee to the Department of
 Presbytery and Congregational Development of the Synod of Southern California states:

 “The present request is for support of what the Committee feels to be already a highly signi cant ministry and mission that o ers promise of becoming a successful model by a somewhat di erent form of church development with ethnic minorities within our Synod. The intent is to enable the present church to maintain its momentum while at the same time allowing the church and the Presbytery time to make a truly sound judgment regarding its organization as a Presbyterian congregation.”

 Although the church was using facilities of Centenary Methodist Church at that time without any problems or obligations, the leadership of the church was not willing to discuss seriously af liation with the Methodist church, because of deeply based ties in Formosan culture with
 Presbyterianism, and considered that a permanently ecumenical approach involved too much insecurity for the new congregation. The fundamental direction of the church is to develop full acceptance as a United Presbyterian congregation.
 Towards this end, the Presbytery Committee agreed to the Formosan Church’s request to try to secure an invitation from a Presbyterian congregation to share facilities, which will become more than a space-sharing arrangement.
 Through a careful review of locations, facilities and training, the Formosan Church decided to accept an invitation from the Wilshire Crest Presbyterian Church of Los Angeles. On July 27, 1971,
 Formosan Christian Church moved to share Wilshire Crest’s facilities located near the intersection of Olympic and La Brea in Los Angeles.
 The proposed organization of Formosan Presbyterian Church and request of funding presented by the Presbytery and Congregational Development Committee was approved by the
 Presbytery of Los Angeles Southwest on September 28, 1971, at a meeting held at Brentwood
 Presbyterian Church, Los Angeles.
 In the presentation to the Presbytery, the Committee concluded:

 ff
 “The Committee has studied this project for more than six months and feels that immediate progressive action is needed to sustain the momentum of this vital worshiping group. It is the consensus of the Committee that this group has a valid and viable mission.”

 With nancial assistance from the Synod Of ce of Southern California and National Missions,
 Reverend Dr. Charles C.L. Kao was called in September 1971 by the Presbytery of Los Angeles
 Southwest in behalf of Formosan congregation as the organizing minister. Dr. Kao, former
 Assistant Professor at Tainan Theological College, Formosa, is also a native Formosan. Before he accepted this new position, he was a Visiting Scholar at Harvard University, Massachusetts.

 IV. Strategy Consideration -- From Presbytery’s Point of View

 Two areas of Presbytery strategy have compelled the Presbytery and Congregational
 Development Committee to seriously consider the request of the Formosan group to assist them in developing their ministry. One is the growing emphasis in Presbytery to use the church’s resources toward efforts where there already exists a signi cant spark of life in the people to be served. The
 Committee could not overlook the initiative and commitment of these people and believes it feasible, in spite of the relatively small population to be served, that this ministry will attain self- support within a relatively short time. The other is the intent and practice of Presbytery to be responsive to the changing urban community in which it nds itself. Their aid is consistent with this commitment to seek to organize a new ministry in order to deal with particular new urban phenomenon, the advent of these newcomers, even though the Presbytery faces dif culties in sustaining its other congregations.
 The design of the proposed new congregation is consistent with Synod and General Assembly guidelines in the following ways.

 1. It proposes to serve a very clearly de ned and strongly-felt community and to deal particularly with needs peculiar to this community -- the Formosan immigrants.
 2. The life-style of the group already includes a strong corporate ministry, as well as concern for individuals, not only in a rich fellowship that is inclusive of both Christians and non-
 Christians but in dealing with the problems of relating these people to Anglo culture and institutions.
 3. It is the intent of this involvement to keep open options for change in the style of ministry and to help the congregation avoid being encumbered in the near future with concerns for building a facility.
 4. The involvement emphasizes interdependence with other congregations and programs of the Synod and General Assembly in order to attain proposed mission goals.
 fi fi fi

 The Presbytery Committee has considered on the one hand the genuine vitality of this new congregation, its obvious capacity to function within Presbyterian denominations as well as its strongly-expressed desire to do so, and its genuine present mission of evangelism and service, and on the other hand the relatively small number of Formosans in the Los Angeles basin and the dif culties experienced by others of ethnically-oriented congregations when social conditions change.
 The Presbytery Committee on Presbytery and Congregational Development will continue to oversee and evaluate this project in consultation with the Synod Department of Presbytery and
 Congregational Development.
 The developing congregation is now, and will continue for the foreseeable future, to share facilities of the Wilshire Crest United Presbyterian Church with whom it is sharing some programs, including monthly joint-worship service. It is the intent of the Committee, consistent with
 Presbytery strategy regarding buildings, that Presbytery continue to nd among its existing facilities truly adequate space for this work. It is important that the Formosan congregation continue to maintain the needed amount of control and responsibility for the facility so that they are never just
 “tenants” or “squatters” and that this congregation not divert its resources from the mission by undertaking a building campaign.
 The Presbytery Committee struggled with their long-standing Presbyterian church strategy of promoting integration of congregations, but they concluded that a ministry to Formosan
 Presbyterians and the rich opportunity to reach non-Christian Formosans with the Gospel cannot be realized apart from the envisioned Formosan church. Furthermore, it concluded that effective unity of the church can be enhanced as these new United Presbyterians are enabled to actively participate in the life of the Presbyterian denomination.

 V. The Current State Of Formosan Christian Church

 On the rst experimental Sunday worship held on August 9, 1970, by Rev. Jonah Chang, 22 adults and 11 children attended. The worship was fully enjoyed. The following four Sundays of the experimental church was a tremendous experience for them. It was a good start and inspired the development of the Formosan Christian Church.
 The members of the congregation have a strong sense of being “the light of the world” and
 “the salt of the earth.” The Christians in Formosa are usually “minority-minded.” They have experienced the feeling of being the one out of every fty Formosans; they are used to the spirit of
 “sharing” this News to the unchurched.
 During the three months of October, November and December, the church did not have a minister; but lay members were able to take turns preaching, working on membership drive, and doing janitorial services such as cleaning sanctuary and church school rooms. Everybody got the feeling of “this is our own church” -- an extremely strong sense of “family gathering.” fi fi

 Under a scholarship from Carson Ministerial Education Fund of Laguna Beach, California, I was able to ful ll my academic requirements at San Francisco Theological Seminary and serve as the volunteer organizing minister of this new-born church for eight months.
 The rst eight months is the decisive period of time to a new-born church. It involved a series of decision-making, policy-setups, and a great many not-yet-solved problems. It has been a bitter- sweet experience to all who were involved in this project. However, the church is progressing and growing gradually in its organization and ministry.

 In the Formosan Church, the activities are designed and carried based upon the fundamental needs of the community and individuals. The following is a brief description of some activities in the church at this time:

1. Sunday morning worship service in Formosan language.
 The attendants presently average between 70 and 90 (including 15-20 children) -- a great increase from the initial 20 of last September. Half of them are non-Christians who are searching for fellowship and the enlightenment of Christian faith. The church is attracting new attendants at a rate of 2-5 persons weekly.

2. A joint worship with Wilshire Crest United Presbyterian Church on the last Sunday of every month has been one of the most meaningful and inspirational to both congregations. The worship service is cooperated by pastors of both congregations.

3. Agape Meal
 After worship service each Sunday morning, the church has an Agape Meal consisting of potluck lunch of native food. Through this ancient Christian tradition of sharing love and care, the congregation and their friends greatly enrich their community life.

4. Sunday School
 There are about 15 to 20 Sunday school students in three different classes ranging from pre- school to the sixth grade. The volunteer director of the program is a seminarian from School of
 Theology, Claremont, California, majoring in Christian Education.

5. Choir
 There are about 15 members in the choir. Under direction, the choir practices hymns, classics, and Formosan folk songs as well. The choir has helped the service a great deal.

6. Social function
 Every Saturday afternoon the church is open to the entire Formosan community in Los Angeles as a social center. The church provides facilities for table tennis, volleyball, games, music as well as folk dancing.

7. Family prayer meeting
 There is a prayer meeting every other Friday night at homes of different members. This circle was where all the plans and discussions had been located before the actual organization of the church.
 At this bi-weekly gathering, they study the Bible, theological textbooks and discuss the way of
 Christian living. Through study, discussion and praying together, the church carries on important adult Christian education in a very fruitful way.

8. Lectures on special interests
 Many members of this congregation are experts in one eld or another, but their knowledge is quite limited beyond their own eld. In order to share the knowledge and experience, the church sponsors a series of lectures held once a month on various interesting topics as
 "Computer and Modern Man," "How to Find a Doctor If You Are Sick," "Income Tax and
 You," open to the entire community.

9. Retreat
 The church had a very successful retreat at Lazy-W Ranch, a United Methodist's retreat ground, during the last Christmas holidays. Seventy adults and children gathered to enjoy the happiness of Christian fellowship. They vividly demonstrated to those non-Christians who were among them the joy and exciting ful llment of being a Christian. They are planning to have another one this coming Christmas.

 VI. Preliminary Mission Design

 The experience of nine months’ association with this particular congregation certainly is long enough to formulate a list of suggestions regarding the future direction and strategy for this newborn church. Based upon the nine-month actual involvement with the church as well as in discussions with groups and interviews with a number of individuals of the entire Formosan community, I shall summarize a guideline for this project; of course, it is based only upon limited observation and contact.

 A. Style of Ministry.

 The style of ministry of this new congregation should re ect the immigrant status of the people to be served, the relatively small number in Los Angeles area, the culture native to them, their relative sophistication, and their commitment to the Presbyterian church.
 The outreach should be metropolitan and extensive, seeking to touch all Formosans in the area.
It should have particular emphasis on university centers. Strong emphasis should be given to fellowship and the attempt to deal with the needs of the whole man. Included in this invitation to fellowship and assistance should be both Christians and non-Christians. This congregation should be an instrument for the self-development of this particular people. At the same time, there should be emphasis on pastoral care and nurture of the congregation, particularly of new members and those inquiring into the faith.
 The denominational identity should be Presbyterian. The leaders of the initiating group are
Presbyterian by heritage with strong tie to Presbyterian families in Formosa. The language of worship should be Formosan. However, the Christian education program should use the English fi language. Part of the need being met is relief from the frustration of communication for adults in an adopted language. Participation in Presbytery and Synod committees should be emphasized far beyond what might be expected of a small, new congregation. The church should also be encouraged to participate in other levels of Presbytery and Synod life in order to facilitate a ministry of interpretation of Formosan and Asian cultures that this group is unusually well-equipped and committed to undertake among Presbyterian churches.

 B. Concerns and Issues.

 The shared facilities and shared life with the congregation of Wilshire Crest United
Presbyterian Church will immeasurably help the realization of the following concerns. The speci c concerns that this church expects to focus on are two-fold:
1. Dealing with needs of the new Los Angeles Formosan community, including:
 a. Adjustment to an entirely foreign culture and coping with sometimes severe dif culties of communication (i.e., as in describing symptom to an Anglo physician).
 b. Psychic and psychological insecurity (i.e., there is an indicated need for marriage counseling).
 c. The problem of identity, as faced by other minorities in America.
 d. Renewal of the spirit, which the church is uniquely equipped to deal with.
 e. Planning for the inevitable change that come to the situation and needs of an immigrant people as the second generation emerges.

2. The other concerns extend beyond this group in Los Angeles and include:
 a. Dealing with the place of Asians in American society and of Asian churches within
 Presbytery and Synod.
 b. Strategies of ministries to Formosans in other areas of the nation.
 c. Relations with the Presbyterian Church in Formosa in order to facilitate informal contact in thinking about ministry to immigrants from Formosa to this country.
 d. It is the intent also to develop strategy with other denominations in the Los Angeles area regarding work with Formosans and to encourage continued development of fraternal relations with the only other Formosan group in the metropolitan area, which is approximately 30 miles from this church.
 e. It is a strongly conservative, evangelical-oriented church.

 The Presbytery anticipates that the church will reach self-support in ve years. Aid will be required until that time on a diminishing basis. The professional nature of the membership would indicate the probability of achievement of this schedule. The congregation is already giving some funds.  C. Prospect of the Mission.

 Since the in ux of the Formosan people into Los Angeles area will increase signi cantly in the coming year, the church has tremendous opportunity to proclaim the Gospel among those people.
This is a new horizon of evangelism -- a foreign mission within the national boundary. The church certainly cannot afford to lose the chance to relate herself to this group of young intellectuals and industrious new immigrants. I believe that the prospect is very bright for this church.
 As the church grows and its members establish themselves in their professions, the church will in time become nancially independent. A church consisting entirely of college graduates cannot stand to be on the receiving end for too long. The enthusiasm and potential of her members is the best guarantee of the future success. Under these conditions and circumstances, it is rmly believed this church will grow physically and spiritually.
