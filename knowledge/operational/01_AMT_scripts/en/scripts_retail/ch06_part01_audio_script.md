CHAPTER VI
 TOWARD A COMMUNITY OF HOPE

 A man living in a period of transition searches for knowledge of the future. He realizes that the foundations of the past have been shaken and that his present existence is dissatisfying and ambiguous. He acts when he discovers something to hope for in the future. Man is a historical being living not only in the memory of the past but in anticipation of the future. A man in the 1970’s is not likely to think of the future in a rosy, idealistic way, as did the utopian thinkers of the eighteenth century; he is much more of a realist, if not a pessimist.
 Among Formosan men and women in the U.S., as they “drop out”, “get away” from political and economic hopelessness, they look forward with hope. One who drops out from something drops into something else.
 Now that “drop out” or “get away” has been attained, however, apathy and uncertainty about the future have crept in. Being in a foreign land and a strange culture, Formosans are confronted with the dif cult and painful process of cultural adjustment. More important, Formosans are confronted with the identity crisis. There is a temptation to fall into social amnesia, to forget their social responsibility and to be concerned with immediate private gain and pleasure, since they feel they do not belong to the U.S. and are too far from Formosa, which means they are neither
 American nor Formosan.
 Under such a circumstance and critical situation, Formosan Christian Church was founded in order to carry out the mission of identity and to provide a foundation of hope for these “lost”
 Formosans. Even though it is too early to give an evaluation of the mission, it is clear that the experience of the last 12 months’ involvement has strengthened and encouraged leaders of the church to carry on the mission toward a community of hope.
 For most Formosans in the U.S., when they were still in Formosa they had a hope of “getting away” or “escaping” from political misery. The rst moment they set foot on American soil, their hope had been achieved. Eventually, they realized that they had to search for a new hope since the old hope had been achieved.
 Without hope people perish. This is a study about people, not about books or disembodied ideas. We have been concerned about how various approaches of the Formosan people to the future affect their life style and attitudes when they live in a new land. In this chapter we are going to discuss, in light of their psychological attitudes and sociological experiences, hope as a norm of life orientation for new immigrants and a “Christian church as a community of hope for Formosans in the U.S.”

 I. Colonialism and Alienation According To Formosan Experience fi

 Colonialism is not a new situation. It is indeed very old. In the past, colonialism was so successful that it was able not only to maintain an oppressed man but an oppressed consciousness as well. An oppressed consciousness is that which is domesticated by the situation in which it nds itself. It is a consciousness which really becomes re exive, unable to be the subject, deprived of a sense of direction and of historical vocation. Dominated by re exiveness, this consciousness could not speak. Unable to enter in critical dialogue with their environment, as a consequence of the relationship of domination which oppressed them, these societies became “mute”.
 Fortunately, however, a remarkable change occurred in the Formosan people’s selfunderstanding. Like the self-awareness of the Third World and the Black man in the United States of America, Formosan became aware that although to live without-freedom was terribly painful that was not the most basic of their problems. They came to realize that they were not simply withoutfreedom. They were made without-freedom. Their without-freedom was the consequence of a colonial relationship in which the powerful dominated and controlled the lives of the weak and powerless. To Formosans, colonialism is not just a situation of the past. It is intrinsic to the relations between ruler, Chiang, and ruled, Formosans, and indeed the cause of without-freedom of the
Formosans. But more than that, colonialism came to be seen as a relationship in which those who are dominated are not allowed to become the creator of their own history. They are reduced to a situation of re exiveness. This means that the lives of the colonial groups are not planned according to their needs, but according to the needs of the politics of the ruling group, the KMT. Thus, the lives of Formosans became, then, sub-systems which always reacted according to the stimuli of the masters without ever being able to change the relationship of domination under which they were.
Without-freedom accordingly began to be understood not in temporal but in relational terms, as without-freedom, as the result of a relationship whereby the powerful and free dominate the powerless and without-freedom.
 By Formosan experience, their past was the history of their impotence under the domination of foreign powers. They could not remember when they had been the subjects of their own history.
The only thing that their memory contained was the presence of foreign powers which forced them to the situation of re exiveness. As far as they could remember, their future had been imposed and not freely chosen. They had never been the creator of their own history but rather the object of someone else’s history.
 More important, due to the cultural differences and experience of dif culty to merge into the
American “melting pot”, Formosan students once again experienced the “alienation”. In the United
States, they are put in a university which is a factory for the production of knowledge and technicians to service American society’s many bureaucracies. For the Formosan students in the
U.S., it means that they are being prepared to t in and serve a system given by a stranger. They have thus to learn how to be “in a game” in which all the rules have been made up and which they cannot amend at all. For the sake of the given system and as a prior condition for being admitted into it, they must suppress the most creative impulses that they have. Here they once again experienced the “not allowed to be the creator of their own history.” fl fl fi

 Before they came to the U.S., in Formosa they already experienced themselves as victims of a
KMT conspiracy that creates educational institutions which are really “factories” which turn out people with all the sharp edges off, the well-rounded person. They consider themselves, therefore, as an exploited class, since their labor is not to be used for the building of their own Formosan society but rather for the preservation of KMT’s interests. The government rationalizes: “the youth is good... but must be oriented.” Formosan is inexperienced on matters of politics; we have to teach them how to ght Communists...However, the goal of this cynical and hypocritical attitude is not to make Formosan the cause of history but rather to make them its subject.
 The process of decolonization, which began at the end of the Second World War with the granting of independence to India and Pakistan, gained momentum in Asia, Africa and Caribbean, with the result that one new country after another was added to the list of free, sovereign and independent states qualifying for membership in the United Nations. It was no longer possible to hold back the forces of liberation or to prevent the revolutionary effects of what former British
Prime Minister Harold Macmillan has aptly described as “wind of change.”
 In his book A Theology of Human Hope, Ruben A. Alves remarks:

 “Today, however, something has changed; the man still oppressed is speaking a di erent language.
It is a language of his own, which indicates that he has emerged into history. He sees the situation of oppression that dominates him. But his consciousness is no longer domesticated. He is determined to liberate himself historically. In the past, the future was closed for him and his consciousness was closed to the future. Today, although the future still remains closed, his consciousness is open for the future.
He inserts himself into his historical present as a contradiction to it, as a negation which presses toward a new tomorrow. He has become a historical subject with a de nite sense of vocation. The new language announces that a new man is born into history.”

 It is the “wind of change” and “the consciousness of to be the creator of their own history” that makes possible the Formosan Independence Movement. They are united in a common experience of not being allowed to be the creator of their own history. They are sharing a worldwide phenomena: Countries want independence, nations want liberation, and peoples want revolution.

 II. The Wait-and-See Attitude and Future of Formosa

 Some people sympathetic to the Formosan struggle for independence frequently say a plebiscite or armed revolution in Formosa would be too swift and radical a step to take for the solution of the
Formosa problem. To them a peaceful change and gradual transfer of power from the mainland
Chinese to the Formosan is more feasible as an alternative solution to the problem. Misled by the fi

 ff

 same thought, a tiny minority of Formosans have cooperated with Chiang, helping him in his despotic rule over Formosa. It is essential that these people be awakened from their anesthetic delusions.
 Those Formosans serving the Chiang regime often say; “I cooperate with Chiang in order to bring about reform from within and gradual transfer of power to the Formosan. Rather than staying outside of the ruling circle, it is better to join, make oneself felt and thus contribute to the transformation.” Under such pretexts quite a few opportunistic Formosans have become the willing tools of Chiang. Such assertions are untenable in the light of reality, for once one cooperates with a corrupt regime, one has to violate his conscience and betray the Formosan interests.
 The futility of trying to improve the community’s common interests by participating in a fundamentally corrupt government system is too risky to one’s integrity. Unless people work together, there is no individual who can ght alone with an evil system. The following story, well circulated in Formosan community, illustrates this futility: A recent law graduate, upon passing his civil service exams and completing his apprenticeship with outstanding merit, was appointed to a judgeship in the Taipei District Court. Young, enthusiastic, and idealistic, this newly appointed judge refused to accept bribes and to accede to the KMT’s interference in the administration of justice. Two years later, this judge was demoted to a rural district because of his “lack of communication with those above and below him.” Initially puzzled, he came to understand the reason later—he did not accept bribes nor did he bribe his superiors. After some hesitation, he decided to do what the Romans do—betray his conscience and administer justice according to the
 “policy” of the party. He began to give and receive bribes and thus excelled in “communication.”
 One year later, he was given an excellent rating and was promoted to head a district court. He is now being considered for a Supreme Court seat.
 This story tells us of the illusion of hoping to join the KMT to effect a gradual reform from within, even in the area of the judiciary whose independence is guaranteed by the “Constitution.”
 In the other departments of the government, corruption is even more deep-rooted. The truth of the matter is that by joining the KMT, one can only aggravate corruption and bring more miseries to his people.
 Why is it impossible to reform the KMT from within? In order to answer this question one must rst analyze the vicious methods the KMT uses to rule and anesthetize the Formosan people.
 First of all, the Formosans are permitted only to stay at the margins of power and are excluded from important decision-making processes. In today’s Formosa, all the power of the KMT, from top to the bottom, is monopolized by the Chinese mainlanders. The very few Formosans who are given high posts in the government have neither real power nor responsibility. In the eyes of the KMT rulers, the Formosans are only t to play the role of bribe-giving servants. The “Banana Corruption
 Case” is a good illustration of this point. No doubt there are some Formosans who are willing to be a pawn for some totalitarian regime and are happy with whatever table scraps their master has left for him. It has never occurred to these people that if they were the masters of their own house they would not have to be contented with the table scraps. Their uncle—Tomism only enables the KMT to permanently keep the Formosans at the margins of power.
 fi
 The second tactic the KMT uses is this: In order to undercut the rapid-growing strength of the
Independence Movement, the KMT promises to give high, but powerless, positions to Formosans abroad willing to return to serve the regime. This helps to create a wait-and-see attitude among
Formosans who, as a result, are afraid to be involved in the independence movement lest they should risk their “future” with the KMT. Those who have been induced into collaboration with the regime are mostly not of rst-rate talents and often are men of no principle and integrity. Instead of being grateful to the Independence Movement for their newly acquired status, they out-do the KMT in their condemnation of the Formosan people’s wish for independence. To them the Formosan people are but ladders by which they can climb to their “success.” Their real masters are the KMT, and its boss, Chiang.
 There are other Formosans who are fence-riders. Intoxicated by the “great Chinese
Chauvinism,” these people are still burdened with their ethnocentric hang-ups. Like so many overseas Chinese, they have the fantasy of sharing one day the grandeur of the mother country— the People’s Republic of China. Fearful of infuriating the Communist Chinese, they oppose the
Formosan Independence Movement. But not being able to endure the frustrations of an exile’s life, as so many Chinese exiles are able to do, and not being able to withstand the temptation of reward from Chiang’s regime, they have gone back to serve the regime. I do not know how these people can rationalize their choice. Can it be that they are no longer fearful of the Chinese Communists? Or are they guarding Formosa against independence in the hope that one day they can help to turn
Formosa over to the Communist Chinese regime?
 The third tactic, the KMT, is not interested in the transfer of power to the Formosans. If the
KMT is sincere about gradual, peaceful change, it should relax, if not abolish completely, the martial law; it should release all political prisoners and dismantle security agencies such as the
Taiwan Garrison Command, the Investigation Bureau (of the Department of Justice) and all the security bureaus attached to every governmental of ce. But everybody knows that the KMT is both fearful and unwilling to take such steps.
 For more than 20 years, Formosa has been virtually an independent state devoid of Chinese
Communist domination. However, because of the hot-and-cold-war politics, Chiang’s regime had been allowed to “represent” the 750 million Chinese people in the United Nations and effectively deny the right of the 14 million Formosans to determine their own fate. In spite of being kicked out from the United Nations this year, Chiang still claims he is the government of China. The continuing existence of the Chiang regime, be it under the name of “the Republic of China,” would only perpetuate instabilities in Formosa and prolong and worsen the dangers of peace and security in East Asia. For as long as the Chiang regime is allowed to persist in Formosa, its provocative if impotent threat of “recapturing mainland China” will continue; and the counterthreat of the Chinese Communists to “liberate Taiwan” will not die. Only by removing the Chiang regime, and hence its dangerous and futile provocations to China, can the source of instability in
East Asia be uprooted.
 In short, wait-and-see attitude is an opium likely to foster escapism and utopianism. Time will not allow Formosans to continue being anesthetized. To escape ruin, the only answer is an fi

 independent nationhood for Formosa in reality as well as in name. Formosa is Formosa and is neither China nor part of China. With the change of status of Formosa, China will have no excuse or necessity to “liberate” Formosa.
 On discussion of the wait-and-see attitude of some Formosans, we have to make it clear that there are two kinds of waiting which must be carefully distinguished. One waits because there is nothing else to do. The other, which goes with hope, is positive and creative, waits because it knows what it wishes and wants. This kind of positive and creative waiting we will discuss more intensively in the next part of this chapter.

 III. The U.N.’s Expulsion of Nationalist Government and Formosa’s Future

 It is regrettable that the expulsion of the regime of Chiang Kai-shek has frequently been identi ed with the expulsion of Formosa. The truth of the matter is that Formosa has never been represented by any government in the United Nations. What was expelled by the majority of the
 United Nations was the regime falsely claiming to be the government of China. The defeat of the
 U.S. and Japanese policy was due precisely to their failing to distinguish the regime of Chiang from the people of Formosa. The majority of the U.N. members felt justi ed in opposing the U.N.
 proposal (endorsed by Japan), because as long as there was another government of China in the
 United Nations, there would be no representation from the true China. Therefore, it seems only reasonable that the government of China should be given its proper place in the world organization.
 On the other hand, it is also true that as long as the regime of Chiang retains its seat, there would be no true representation of Formosa in the United Nations. Thus it seems equally reasonable that the government of Chiang pretending to be the government of China was nally expelled.
 Ironically, the issue has not been the expulsion of Formosa or the representation of China. On one hand, the expulsion of Chiang’s regime from the U.N. does not solve the problem of Formosa.
 On the other hand, the loss of U.N. seat does not necessarily mean that Formosa will cease to be an independent political entity. The crux of the matter is the future of the 14 million people on the island.
 The United Nations’ decision in seating Peking and expelling Chiang’s regime—President
 Nixon’s proposed visit to the mainland—underscores the tangle of the Formosan issue. And now as world leaders nally admit the absurdities of nationalistic Chinese fantasies, a new myth emerges:
 That Formosa has been and must be an integral part of China to achieve full sovereignty, that she must annex Formosa.
 In reality, Formosa’s connection with China before 1895 was so loose as to be almost nominal and relations were tumultuous. In the last 75 years the island has fewer than four years of union with China, 1945-1949, and those were by no means the happiest in Formosan history. In 1947 an uprising occurred which resulted in the massacre of over 20,000 Formosan leaders. Native
 Formosans are of Chinese extraction, but have shared centuries of experience unique to them and have developed their own identity. They were joined after the Second World War by over two fi fi

million continental Chinese, who now are also cut off for more than 20 years from the experience of the new China. All the people in Formosa now have no more connection with China than
Americans have with Britain. Moreover, Formosa’s legal status has been undetermined since 1945.
 Furthermore, China’s emotional claims to Formosa have nothing to do with its sovereignty and territorial integrity. Early in this century, when China was striving to attain full sovereignty, Formosa was never an issue. Mao Tse-tung himself, drawing an analogy with Korea, expressed his readiness to support an independent Formosa. It was only after 1949 when the exiled Nationalist used the island as a base for aggression against China that Peking’s attitude toward Formosa became belligerent.
 Many Chinese in the U.S. still insist that Formosa must be a part of China, which in fact means rule either by the Peking or the Taipei Government. Yet they themselves have chosen to live under neither Chinese regimes and have never considered Formosa their homeland.
 The Chinese must learn to distinguish ethnic origin and culture from politics and law and to discard their archaic obsession to claim anyone of Chinese ancestry as legally Chinese, however far removed from China. They must stop vilifying as traitors those who desire self-determination and understand that one can be proud of his Chinese heritage and still choose not to be subject to government by China.
 The real issue is not independence for Formosa but self-determination for the people there.
And the Formosans want to live in the most friendly association with the Chinese people and would spare no effort to establish the closest economic, commercial, cultural and even political ties with
China.
 Any settlement of the sovereignty over Formosa must come through peaceful negotiation in which the people of Formosa are fully and effectively expressed. No one from outside may dictate to
Formosans to whom they should belong. Neither Peking nor Washington can speak for them. The present regime in Taipei never has spoken legitimately for the majority on Formosa. It cannot do so unless it undergoes a basic structural change and the Formosan majority is represented effectively at every level.
 The jolt of being expelled from the United Nations has touched off a round of calls for political and social reforms to strengthen Formosa. But it also has emphasized the heavy hand of the past on Nationalist China. Lots of people in Formosa, mainlanders as well as native Formosans, remained skeptical about the sincerity of high-level calls for reform. Giving Formosan a larger voice in national government would broaden Chiang Kai-shek regime’s base of support and strengthen it against Kekiz. But the regime is stuck with the system and structure it had created. If they change it and give the Formosan a full voice, it won’t be a Nationalist Chinese government anymore. It will be a Formosan government.
 Political and psychological problems growing out of the United Nations ouster could be at least as serious. Peking’s long-dormant challenge to Chiang’s control of Formosa now is likely to grow.
However, the U.N. ouster is just the start of Chiang’s problems. Meantime, it is a crisis and challenge to Formosa.

 IV. Search For The Ground Of Hope

 In the period of environmental adjustment from an old to a new and from an east to a west, most Formosan immigrants in the United States are facing radical changes to their value system and lifestyle. Since their traditional norm is not applicable in this new society, Formosan people become uncertain as to the foundation of their life. Before they left Formosa, their home land, the Confucian teaching and the Buddhistic attitude to life provided them the basic framework of society. But today, in a new society, there is widespread ambiguity in de ning the center of life. In this period of transition, confusion and uncertainty prevail. To some, dissatis ed and frustrated in a new environment, their reactions are negative, and they do not play their part in developing a common framework of realistic participation in the new society and the new life.
 In a new environment, people are concerned with immediate, individual interests and tend to forget their common social responsibilities. People easily forget their common humanity and put their individual, private interests rst. A student, let us say, in a highly competitive system of examination forgets to think of the ultimate concern of the meaning of life; he is interested only in getting a better grade than his classmates in the next examination in order to obtain a scholarship.
 Or a churchman is occupied with installing a new piano suitably but forgets his role in the social problem of his own community.
 Certainly, we are living in an era of change, and in the process of change people very easily get lost. It is particularly true of those new immigrants in this country. Formosans in the United States are fascinated by American science and technology and are attracted by the abundant material wealth and worldly pleasures of life. They come to this country in search of HAPPINESS, but nobody has ever found happiness existing in material wealth. For happiness is not an entity, happiness is never an end in itself but rather a side effect of devotion to a more important and unsel sh goal. Formosans come to this country to search for FREEDOM but gradually discover that in the highly mechanized American society nothing is free and nobody is free. To many a Formosan, due to their past, miserable experience in Formosa, freedom means only “escape”. Freedom, however, is always associated with responsibility, according to Viktor E. Frankl’s thought: Each person, each existing being, is responsible for attaining the utmost meaning that can be ful lled in his life, in the course of which he is also responsible for realizing speci c values.
 Due to their frustration of being without freedom in the United States as well as in Formosa, which was described in the earlier part of this chapter, they soon become aware that they are just in
 “existential vacuum,” which according to Frankl is as follows:

 “This existential vacuum seems to me to issue from the two-fold fact that, unlike an animal, man is not told by drives and instincts what he must do; nor is he, like man in former times, told by tradition and values what he should do. Often he does not even know what he basically wishes to do.
 Instead, he wishes to do what other people do—which is conformism. Or he does what other people wish him to do—which is totalitarianism.” fi fi fi

 To Formosans in this complex jungle of a strange society and the “existential vacuum” life style, there is a tendency toward apathy and resignation. It is not the heroic revolutionaries with idealistic visions who make sweeping changes needed but a vital spiritual force to awaken men and women from their social amnesia. They need the power of anamnesis, a reawareness of selfhood in their lives and the recognition of responsibility in the society.
 This vital spiritual force or the power of anamnesis, in Paul Tillich terms “the courage to be”, is possible only when people have the sense of the possible. This “sense of the possible” is the ultimate concern of human hope and that is what Christian faith is all about.

 A. Hope Is Possible

 We human beings, who need hope more than anything else in life, have written little about it.
 Dr. Karl Menninger, one of the most distinguished psychologists, notes humorously that even in the
 Encyclopedia Britannica there are many columns on love, and still more on faith, but not even a list on hope. As a matter of fact, not only do psychologists seldom touch on the subject, theologians have also stayed away from “Hope” until the recently begun Hope Movement.
 Hope has, apparently, a bad reputation; few people want to have anything consciously to do with it, much less write about it. In the book Image of Hope, William Lynch suggests that there is a supreme irony going on around about it. He says:

 “… for many people hope really means despair. As we use language, when we say that a man has hope, we mean that he is in serious trouble. When we say that someone has hope, we usually imply that he has nothing else, and that he is close to despair.”

 Hoping is a process, both emotional and intellectual, of image, of a future in which our possibilities might come to fruition. William Lynch provides a very suggestive de nition on hope:

 “I de ne hope… as the fundamental knowledge and feeling that there is a way out of di culty, that things can work out, that we as human persons can somehow handle and manage internal and external reality, that there are solutions in the most ordinary biological and physiological sense of that word, that, above all, there are ways out of illness.”
