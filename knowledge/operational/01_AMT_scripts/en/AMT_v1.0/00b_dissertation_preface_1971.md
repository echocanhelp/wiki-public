# Dissertation Preface (1971)

PREFACE

 For various reasons, mainly political, every year there are nearly two thousand Formosan students who come to the United States for graduate study, and only less than 5 percent of them return to Formosa on completing their graduate study. This is a great brain drain to a developing country such as Formosa. These young Formosans realize the problem; but unfortunately, there is no other alternative for them. They are forced to come and to stay here for one reason or another -- a modern style of exodus.
 Although all Formosans arrive in this country with Chinese passports (Republic of China), they hardly get along with Chinese. They stick together according to their dialect and differentiate themselves from either the Cantonese-speaking people or the Mandarin-speaking people. On the other hand, they feel that assimilation with the American culture is not possible with their cultural and language barriers. They tend to be socially introverted, and there is an expectation that the church will provide the only social activity center -- a home away from home.
 In order to study the needs and to work out the problems in the Formosan community in the
United States, I proposed the project-dissertation of my study at Level IV Program of San Francisco
Theological Seminary on an intensive study regarding this particular group of new immigrants in
Los Angeles by establishing an experimental church for Formosans in the area.
 “Toward a Community of Hope” is the way of my project, and also the title of my research dissertation, for the purpose of nding the meaning of theology of hope to this spiritually depressed group of new immigrants. Every one of them is struggling with the problem of hope and hopelessness in their everyday life. If the church can be de ned as a community of hope, then that is what hope is all about to these silenced people in psychological, sociological, political and theological terms.
 The reader is entitled to know what to expect in this thesis. It is my contention that the
Christian church is essentially a community of hope. The function of the church is to provide the meaning of hope for the community so that they can nd out that their struggle for political, social and economic justice is consistent with the Gospel of hope. Any message that is not related to the hope of human being is not a Christian message.
 The purpose of this study is to describe and explain the Formosan view of their hope -- their past, their present and their future -- in order to search for a usable future for the Formosan community. The principle focus of this study is on Formosans’ political, social attitudes toward their future, but evidence is also drawn from psychological and theological perspectives.
 A perspective permits something to be seen. It brings a matter into the present circle of history.
It presents an opinion and opens up a view. It is based on the present and points out the special aspects of a concern. At the same time, a perspective is closely related to practice. It reaches out into the possibilities of the future, searches for goals and develops directives for action.
 Furthermore, to be a Christian is to be a member of historical people. As a community of hope, we live in the present, in view of the past, in face of the future. Life is a pilgrimage and we are fi always on the way. In order to help people on their journey, of course, we ought to know the people rst. And in order to get to know the people on the journey, we must have the answers to these questions: “Where did they come from?”, “What are they doing?”, “Where are they going?” Then we can ask, “How can they get there?”
 On the matter of mission to the Formosan community in Los Angeles, keep this in mind.
 Therefore, in the rst two chapters, I try to explain the rst question, “Where did they come from?”, politically, ethnically, psychologically and culturally.
 In the third and fourth chapters, I attempt to describe the answer to the question “What are they doing?” Then I try to deal with the question “Where are they going?” in terms of searching for integration, independence and identity. Finally, in the last chapter,
 I suggest the formula of “toward a community of hope” as the way of “how to get there” in terms of Biblical-theological, social-psychological and economic-political perspectives.
 Under these presuppositions in this study, I concern myself with the concepts and phenomena of hope and correlative attitudes toward the future of the Formosan communities in the United
 States at large and attempt to discern how theology of hope can be mobilized in the groups and individuals with whom I worked as a religious leader.

 A. L.
