 Half a Century of Faith and Mission
 National Taiwanese Presbyterian Council, Presbyterian Church (U.S.A.)

 In 1971, Dr. Albert S. Lai（賴信雄） published Toward A Community Of Hope —A Mission to Formosan Community in Los Angeles, a work both theological and prophetic in nature. At a time when Taiwanese immigrant churches in North America were still in their infancy—especially the
Formosan Presbyterian Church in Los Angeles (FPCLA), the rst of its kind in the continent—Dr.
Lai offered not merely a historical review, but a spiritual vision. He called the church to transcend being merely a haven for immigrants and instead become a missional community, a beacon of hope and a witness to the gospel in the larger society.

 A Spiritual Call Amid Cultural Transition
 In the early 1970s, most Taiwanese in North America arrived with hopes for education, professional advancement, or temporary settlement. They faced linguistic barriers, cultural alienation, and identity uncertainty. Churches quickly became one of the few familiar places in a foreign land—a spiritual anchor amid transition. Yet Dr. Lai pushed the church to think beyond comfort. He boldly asserted that if the church is indeed called by God, then it must live as a sent people—not merely surviving, but witnessing to and transforming its surrounding society.

 The Los Angeles Calling: From Ethnic Church to Missional Community
 The publication of this book is inextricably linked to the establishment of the Formosan
Presbyterian Church in Los Angeles (FPCLA). As the rst Taiwanese church in North America,
FPCLA did not exist merely to bridge linguistic or cultural gaps. From its inception, it sought to become a “mission base” in the local community. Dr. Lai documented both the struggles and aspirations of the early church—how believers found spiritual meaning beyond work and family, how they supported one another in a foreign land, and how that internal community gave birth to an external calling to serve and transform.

 The Theological Vision of a “Hopeful Community”
 The idea of a “hopeful community” carries rich theological weight. It is not a slogan born of optimism or human willpower, but a lived imagination rooted in biblical truth. Drawing from Acts and Pauline letters, Dr. Lai described the church not as a safe retreat for insiders, but as a dynamic body for the hope of the world. A hopeful community is one that chooses love in adversity, chooses service in marginality, and chooses witness in brokenness.

 Continuing the Un nished Mission
 Over ve decades later, Taiwanese churches in Los Angeles and across North America have undergone multiple transitions—from immigrant churches to intergenerational, multicultural communities; from language-based fellowships to agents of justice and public engagement. Yet, the core challenge of this book remains timeless: Are we still a hopeful community? Are we still bearers of the gospel light for Los Angeles and beyond? Are we still faithful to the vision rst cast in 1971?

 Honoring the Past, Journeying into the Future
 We give heartfelt thanks to Dr. Albert Lai for his vision and voice, and we honor the pioneers who laid the spiritual foundation of FPCLA. Their faith, courage, and servant hearts gave rise not only to a book or a church, but to a movement that has shaped the spiritual heritage of Taiwanese
 Christianity in North America. May this commemorative reprint not merely mark a retrospective moment, but sound a fresh call to mission—inviting a new generation to rise and respond.
 Let us once again embrace the weight of this phrase—Toward A Community Of Hope —A
 Mission to Formosan Community in Los Angeles—not merely as a book title, but as a journey still unfolding.

 To God be the glory!

 Tainan Theological College and Seminary(TTCS)（台南神學院） from 1932 to 1936. In the academic year of 1935-1936, which was his nal year at the TTCS, he was elected as the president of the student council. That year, Dr. Barclay passed away on October 5, 1935, at the Sin-lâu
 Hospital（新樓醫院）. At the funeral, my father and his classmates represented the student council and carried the cof n to be buried in the Christian cemetery.

From London to Amoy to Taiwan, his contributions spanned every corner of Taiwan, from urban areas to remote mountain villages, making him a classic model for the Taiwanese people and the Taiwanese church across centuries.

 Subtle Similarities
 In the early 1970s, I attempted to engage in trade with the Communist Party and worked in advertising, translation, printing, and publishing in Los Angeles. In the 1980s, I had the opportunity to work in publishing, training, and international resource integration in China. In the 1990s, I was engaged in training government of cials and leaders of state-owned enterprises, English training, and industries related to the internet and e-commerce in China. After 2000, I transitioned into private English education and university education in China, until my retirement in 2020.
 However, when I revisited the life work of Dr. Barclay in Taiwan; re ected on my father's lifelong ministry; and also re-examined my own experiences over the past 50 years, I unexpectedly discovered that my father and I, two generations, shared similar backgrounds in terms of birth, upbringing, education, and work environments after graduation. The timeline spans nearly a century, covering the era of the Japanese Empire, the White Terror under Chiang Kai-shek in the
Republic of China, the democratic transition in Taiwan, the United States, and the People's
Republic of China under the Communist Party, enduring a century and countless rulers. Different eras, different social conditions, different political climates, and differing needs of the people, yet I found that I was profoundly in uenced by Dr. Barclay in many ways, subtly and imperceptibly."

This gradually developed into a center for Sunday worship and weekly activities for faculty, staff, and students. Over the years, this church surprisingly evolved into a new seven-story large Western-style cathedral, with a team of 40 clergy, staff, and workers, serving as the only community church within dozens of kilometers, providing services for nearby Christians and residents.
 Fast forward to four or ve years ago, the COVID-19 pandemic left me at home in Southern
 California, concluding my work in China and allowing me time to re ect on and review my career.

 The Generational Legacy of Faith
 After a series of re ections, comparisons, and repeated contemplations, I was amazed to discover the profound in uence passed down from my father from his mentor, Dr. Barclay. More astonishingly, I realized that I have also been deeply in uenced by my father, inescapably and inevitably. This must be a marvelous arrangement of God's grace, passing through Dr. Barclay to my father and continuing down the line. On examining this wonderful legacy, I humbly re ect and feel quite inadequate, as I truly cannot compare to my ancestors even in a fraction; they have left behind the genes of faith, which have been passed down and witnessed throughout my life.
 Therefore, I am grateful that this gene of faith has made my work more meaningful and my life more abundant. I pray to honor God and bene t others, as a self-motivation.

 A. The Characteristics of This Book - A Fusion of Theology and History

 "Toward a Community of Hope — A Mission to Formosan Community in Los Angeles" is the doctoral dissertation completed by Dr. Albert S. Lai（賴信雄） in December 1971 while studying at San Francisco Theological Seminary. The main purpose of this work is to discuss why evangelism is necessary for Taiwanese immigrants in Los Angeles and the importance and theological signi cance of establishing an ethnic church speci cally dedicated to nurturing Taiwanese people.
 Fifty years later, the political landscape in Taiwan has undergone signi cant changes, transitioning from an authoritarian regime to a fully democratic system. Moreover, national identity has shifted from identifying as Chinese to Taiwanese, with only about 2% of the population still considering themselves "Chinese." This difference makes it dif cult for contemporary readers to understand the political developments in Taiwan in 1970 and the emotional journey of the Formosan community in
 North America.
 As we take the time to read this book, we gain insight into the concern of the Christian faith for
 Taiwanese people in North America, while also entering the political context of Taiwan in 1970 through the author's words.
 As a doctoral dissertation from a theological seminary, its purpose should be to elucidate
 Christian faith and doctrine. However, structurally, it dedicates half of its content to narrating
 "Taiwan's" own history and politics—topics that were serious taboos at the time, making it a unique theological dissertation. The reason for this phenomenon lies in the fact that the target audience for evangelism in the text is "Taiwanese" (Formosan) in Los Angeles. In 1970, the American church community was quite unfamiliar with "Taiwanese," so the author needed to provide background knowledge on Taiwan's geography, historical culture, political development, and the psychological and physical conditions of Taiwanese immigrants. Moreover, the concern for immigrants extends beyond the physical realm to deeper spiritual and emotional needs. Without addressing a group's history and culture, holistic care cannot be achieved. This book, as a dialogue between history and theology, also asserts that the Christian faith cannot be separated from history and culture, and this aspect of theological exploration is deeply discussed in this text.
 However, in 1970, the national identity of the people living in Taiwan was "Chinese," and their understanding of Taiwan was that it was a province of the Republic of China; identifying as fi fi

 "Taiwanese" was unacceptable to the ruling authorities. Therefore, from the current atmosphere of national identity, it is challenging to understand why the author proposed evangelism to
 "Taiwanese" in 1970, as it was seen as defying the ruling authorities of the Republic of China and violating political taboos. Additionally, before the maturation of national consciousness in Taiwan, it is intriguing to explore why immigrants in North America began to develop a sense of Taiwanese national identity. Furthermore, how did these intellectual elites, who fostered a sense of Taiwanese national identity, interpret Taiwan's history and culture? What visions did they have for Taiwan's future? What factors led them to believe in the necessity of "independently establishing a nation"? These issues continue to in uence our social and political development today, highlighting the importance of understanding the relevant historical topics. Therefore, further understanding the "Taiwanese perspective" presented in this book will help us connect more closely with that era.

 B. The North American Formosan Community in Current Academic Research and Literature

 Since the lifting of martial law and the political party transition in the Republic of China, research on the Taiwanese independence movement has no longer been a political taboo. The contemporary academic community has conducted a series of oral history interviews with participants involved in the movement and has begun preliminary explorations into overseas leadership groups and the development of the overseas Taiwanese independence movement, yielding some initial results. However, research on the history of Taiwanese churches in the United
 States is still in its nascent stages. Related studies can be categorized into three areas based on the volume of research ndings: the Taiwanese independence movement, post-war Taiwanese church history, and the development of Taiwanese churches in the United States. This paper will sequentially introduce the key points and characteristics of the relevant research.
 Research on the Taiwanese independence movement has produced a wealth of scholarly books, among which Chiahong's Chen "History of the Taiwanese Independence Movement"（陳佳宏
 《台灣獨 運動史》） stands out as a representative work in this eld. This book categorizes the process of "Taiwanese independence" into ve major stages—objective, substantial, subjective, institutional, and legal—based on the characteristics of post-war political development. It also explores the consolidation of "Taiwanese independence" forces both domestically and abroad, as well as the challenges and transformations faced by the movement. Regarding the intersection of the Christian church and the independence movement, a notable work is Rongfang
 Tsai's "From Religion to Politics: The Practice of Dr. Shoki Coe's Ecumenical Theology."（蔡
 榮芳《從宗教到政治： 彰輝牧師普世神學的實踐》） This book examines how Pastor Coe's religious and political thoughts were formed and their in uence on the independence movement, while also discussing the impact of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan system on the movement.

 C. The Gap Between Taiwanese Society and the Formosan community in North America

 With the democratization of Taiwan, Taiwanese civil society has become accustomed to the freedom of speech and the freedom to travel in and out of the country. "Taiwan independence" can now be publicly discussed and is no longer a taboo topic; there has even been academic research on the subject. The United States is also an important country for Taiwanese people traveling abroad for tourism and work, with the number of people entering the U.S. reaching 600,000 in 2019.
However, despite these freedoms, there remains an invisible gap in civil society regarding the
Formosan community in North America.
 Firstly, civil society is quite unfamiliar with the knowledge elites overseas who support Taiwan's
"nation-building." What is often recognized are the opinions expressed by certain independence advocates or their contributions to Taiwan's democracy noted in the news upon their passing, but these "contributions" are largely disconnected from the lived experiences of the general public.
Secondly, for the majority of Taiwanese people, becoming an "American" is seen as a signi cant marker of success in life. There has been a saying: “Comes, comes, comes, coming to National
Taiwan University; goes, goes, goes, going to America," which seems to imply that the ultimate goal in life is to become an "American." Furthermore, if friends or family members obtain a U.S. "Green
Card" or even become American citizens, it is often viewed as a joyous occasion. Therefore, in this societal atmosphere, the U.S. is perceived as a kind of "paradise," and living in America seems to free one from the "ghost island" of Taiwan. This leads Taiwanese society to think: if people living in the U.S. are enjoying such a ful lling life, why should they care about Taiwan's affairs? Or it may be hard to understand why, despite living in "paradise," one could feel so miserable.
 Lastly, beyond the Taiwanese public's "American" sentiment, changes in the political environment have further widened this gap. Under the governance of the Democratic Progressive
Party (DPP), many who once promoted the Taiwanese independence movement have transitioned from being marginalized in society to becoming politically "esteemed" gures. In summary, the reasons for the gap between Taiwanese civil society and the Formosan community immigrating to the United States can be discussed in two points: rst, civil society is largely unfamiliar with their life experiences, often believing that immigration to the U.S. signi es success and a life of comfort; second, while the overseas independence activists faced signi cant hardships, after the DPP came to power, these activists gained considerable political standing.
 This lack of understanding in Taiwanese society regarding the lives of American immigrants, coupled with the reversal of political status and the rapid changes in national consciousness, has created multiple layers of fog around the historical research of the Formosan community in North
America. This situation prevents Taiwanese civil society from grasping the life experiences of the
Formosan community overseas, leading to a lack of resonance between their experiences. Without resonance, it becomes dif cult for Taiwanese society to empathize with the political and cultural discussions of the Formosan community in North America regarding "Taiwan." This book offers a perspective on the struggles of the Formosan community in America and their longing for their fi fi fi homeland through the lens of Christian faith, fostering empathy for their experiences. Only through
 "empathy" can this invisible gap gradually be bridged, promoting dialogue.

 D. Authoritarian Politics in Post-War Taiwan and the Formation of Taiwanese Consciousness

 Recently, a former mayor of Taipei has been detained and barred from contact due to allegations of bene ting a consortium in a development project. In response to this political controversy, a legislator with a Ph.D. in law from McGill University publicly declared it to be "green terror," urging supporters to resist the current authoritarian regime. With the encouragement of this law scholar and the promotion by supporters, terms like "green terror" and "authoritarian regime" have circulated widely online and in the media, suggesting that the current political environment is akin to that of the 1970s. This phenomenon indicates that many people in contemporary Taiwanese society have no concept of the political system of the 1970s, allowing for the revival of the story of
 "pointing at a deer and calling it a horse.”
 To understand the life experiences of the Formosan community in North America today, it is essential to return to the context of 1971 and understand the political environment at that time.
 This section will explain the situation of authoritarianism after the war, the special relationship between the Republic of China and the United States, and further elaborate on how Taiwanese consciousness emerged within the North American Formosan community.
 "Authoritarianism," as de ned by political scholar Juan Linz, is characterized as follows:
 A political system that is a limited pluralism, but not responsible politics.
 Lacking a comprehensive and coherent ideology, it is primarily based on the will of the ruler.
 There is a lack of deep and sustained political mobilization, and it rejects spontaneous grassroots political mobilization.
 Political life is dominated by leader-centric political groups, where the exercise of power is not xed but primarily aimed at enforcing the will of the leader.
 When the Kuomintang (KMT) ruled mainland China, the military strongman Kaishek Chiang
 （蔣中正） led the KMT to eliminate various political forces after controlling the central government of the Republic of China. After retreating to Taiwan, they deepened military rule through martial law. This political model is described as "authoritarian militarism," which translates to "military authoritarianism" or "militarism." One criticism at the time was the failure to promulgate a constitution to establish the rule of law; the KMT often acted in disregard of legal norms. After Chiang retreated to Taiwan, this authoritarian political power was further strengthened. On May 10, 1948, as Chiang faced continuous defeats in the Chinese Civil War, he promulgated the "Temporary Provisions Effective During the Period of National Mobilization for
 Suppression of the Communist Rebellion," freezing parts of the Constitution. As Chiang's rule deepened, more provisions of the Constitution were frozen until they were repealed on May 1, fi
1991, under President Tenghui Lee（李登輝）. Thus, for 43 years, the KMT government governed with "incomplete" adherence to constitutional norms, and as the Constitution became non-functional, the ve-powers system gradually deteriorated into an executing unit of Chiang's will, further undermining its autonomy. For example, in the judicial system, recent literature has revealed many instances of Chiang directly revising judgments and even ordering the replacement of judges.
 In addition to undermining the ve-powers system, Chiang further established numerous intelligence and security agencies—also known as secret police—that were not bound by law and were directly commanded by him. The most notable example is the operation of the Taiwan
Garrison Command（臺灣警備總司令部）, whose commander is subordinate to the Ministry of
National Defense, but in practice, the Minister of National Defense could not restrain the Garrison
Command's activities. Moreover, the Garrison Command, due to its connections with various administrative agencies and police institutions, could not legally order the operations of these agencies, yet in practice, they were all directed by the Garrison Command. Although there was a legal framework, intelligence units were unbound by it, extending the mentality of a military strongman into the daily lives of Taiwanese society. This control over various aspects of life, including publishing, speech, and assembly, characterized the political atmosphere in Taiwan at that time.
 Under Chiang's military authoritarian regime, elites in various sectors found little hope for personal career development unless they entered the party-state system. For intellectual elites, the suppression of thought and culture by the party-state resembled a prison for the mind. Furthermore,
Taiwanese elites faced another form of torment: the erasure of their personal subjectivity. After the
Republic of China retreated to Taiwan, it maintained the institutional and ideological constructs of its former mainland-style governance, imposing them upon the island of Taiwan. As a result, although Taiwan was merely a province of the Republic of China, it accounted for over 99% of the territory of the Republic of China. Public employees and representatives at various levels in Taiwan were allocated positions based on the quotas set for the "occupied regions," with Taiwanese people receiving less than half of the available positions. Additionally, if Taiwanese individuals wanted to work in government or educational institutions, they were required to be pro cient in "Mandarin," while speaking their "mother tongue" was considered a violation subject to punishment. Under this mainland-centric system, Taiwanese people had to deny their own subjectivity to enter the so-called "upper" society.
 Beyond systemic issues, Chiang and the party-state had considerable cultural biases against
Taiwanese people. Just before the Republic of China was to govern Taiwan, the "Taiwan Minsheng
Bao"（台灣民聲報）, organized by Taiwanese individuals, petitioned the government to implement constitutional self-governance, but their request was denied by the party-state. During the February 28 Incident, Chiang recorded in his diary on March 7, 1947, "This kind of Taiwanese initially attached to us, having long suffered from the Japanese, forgot their homeland, thus they all fear the powerful and do not cherish virtue," revealing his distrust and disdain for the Taiwanese fi people. After retreating to Taiwan, Chiang employed intelligence agencies to conduct strict loyalty investigations and assessments of Taiwanese intellectual elites. For example, in 1952, the
 Investigation Bureau conducted reports on the ideological and political tendencies of important local faction leaders in Taiwan. On June 2, 1950, Chiang noted in his diary that Yang Zhao Jia（楊
 肇嘉）, the head of the provincial government's Civil Affairs Of ce, was a central gure in the
 Taiwan Liberation Movement. Thus, the above-mentioned literature con rms the party-state's contempt and distrust toward Taiwanese people, resulting in the comprehensive suppression of local elites in political, military, cultural, and educational aspects. This political structure has been described as a form of "colonialism," but since the Republic of China lost its homeland, it is more accurately referred to as a "settler state." Under the governance of a settler regime, the oppressed
 Taiwanese people gradually felt the differences between themselves and the ruling group, which is also the reason for the emergence of Taiwanese subjective consciousness.
 Under this unequal rule, various degrees of resistance movements emerged among Taiwan's political and intellectual elites, with some choosing to establish an independent nation. So how did the Taiwanese independence movement unfold under the oppressive rule of the party-state? Indeed, due to the omnipresent surveillance by the party-state, the independence movement within Taiwan found it dif cult to develop, leading many activists to operate overseas. Japan and the United States became important bases for the development of the movement. The historical connection to Japan dates back to the Japanese colonial period, during which some Taiwanese had established networks in Japan. Thus, intellectual and social elites like Wang Yu Te（王育德） and Lin Hsien Tang（林
 獻堂） moved to Japan to escape political persecution. In the case of the United States, it was intertwined with the international political landscape of the Cold War. During the Chinese Civil
 War, as Chiang's forces began to collapse, the U.S. also ceased aid to the Republic of China.
 However, with the outbreak of the Korean War and the formation of a confrontation between democratic and communist camps, the previously abandoned Chiang regime became America's ally in combating communist forces, receiving substantial aid. With American support, Chiang's forces were able to take root in Taiwan, turning a crisis into stability. Therefore, while the KMT could control the culture and economy on the island of Taiwan, it could not sever ties with the United
 States. Moreover, Chiang's authoritarian rule also had to consider the American response and still needed to present itself as a democratic nation. It was precisely because the Chiang regime was still constrained by the U.S. that it allowed for a narrow space for Taiwanese intellectual elites to develop. In this narrow space, Taiwanese elites, unable to develop in Taiwan, sought opportunities in the U.S., which re ects the reality of “goes, goes, goes, going to America.”
 Once in America, Taiwanese elites gradually developed an awareness of Taiwanese subjectivity after being exposed to different political information. Immigrants who developed a sense of
 Taiwanese subjectivity did not necessarily engage in the independence movement, but there were still KMT intelligence personnel in the U.S. who reported on the situation of Taiwanese people overseas. In response, the government intensi ed monitoring or harassed the relatives of immigrants in Taiwan, which further accelerated the development of immigrants' Taiwanese consciousness.
 fi fi

 Consequently, beginning in the 1960s, Taiwanese students in the U.S. formed various independence groups, and some even began to lobby U.S. Congress members. For the U.S., the government did not view the Republic of China as a democratic nation, which con icted with the spirit of
 American democracy and made the question of whether to aid the Republic of China a dilemma for the government. In the 1960s, as the People's Republic of China and the Soviet Union became estranged, U.S. foreign policy shifted to engage the People's Republic of China to counter the Soviet
 Union. This also laid the groundwork for the passing of United Nations Resolution 2758 in
 December 1971, which expelled Chiang's regime as China's representative and recognized the
 People's Republic of China as the representative of China. The Republic of China was expelled, yet
 Taiwan became the territory of China under the "One China" principle. Although the Republic of
 China suffered diplomatic defeat, the Taiwanese independence movement found a new adversary in the form of the People's Republic of China. The question then arose: where would the path of
 Taiwanese independence lead? This re ects the sentiments of the Formosan community in North
 America at that time.

 E. Understanding Taiwanese Politics Through American Literature

 With an understanding of the historical context, we can gain valuable insights from this book.
 Firstly, it was written in November 1971, a critical moment when the Republic of China was expelled from the United Nations. The development of the overseas Taiwanese independence movement was in uenced by this event, and their discourse and actions were subsequently adjusted.
 The author documents how Taiwanese intellectual elites articulated Taiwan's subjectivity before
 1972. Interestingly, the book also explains why the Formosan community in the United States uses
 "Formosa" instead of "Taiwan," primarily to distinguish themselves from the Republic of China's
 "Taiwan" and to establish a new narrative of Taiwanese subjectivity. Therefore, this book offers unique insights into Taiwanese identity, history, culture, and the post-war political system in Taiwan.
 While some aspects of historical and cultural understanding may require revision based on contemporary academic research, this text presents a rare historical narrative centered on Taiwan, especially in the absence of "Taiwanese historiography" within Taiwan itself.
 Notably, the author's discussions of Taiwanese history reveal that the sources cited are predominantly foreign works. This suggests that the historical narrative is shaped by the author's perspective of Taiwanese subjectivity, informed by external literature. This re ects the American
 Taiwanese intellectual elite's understanding of their homeland's history and contributes to the broader process of expanding Taiwanese historiography.
 Secondly, nding uncensored articles within Taiwan under an authoritarian regime is exceedingly dif cult. For researchers studying post-war Taiwanese history, it is clear that relevant of cial documents have been censored and are often propaganda. Without a profound understanding of authoritarian systems and a careful analysis of the ideological context of the literature, one may easily be swayed by of cial narratives, leading to a perception of that era as a fi fl fl

golden age. For the author, gaining an in-depth understanding of Taiwan's political situation at that time necessitated referencing foreign literature. In discussing post-war Taiwanese politics, the author utilized reports from American congressional investigations, which directly described Taiwan's political issues and recorded instructions from the Chiang family. Therefore, further research into
American investigative reports on Taiwan at that time, compared to contemporary of cial documents, would aid scholars in analyzing post-war Taiwanese politics and society from diverse perspectives. Currently, the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) has archival materials from Taiwan dating back to 1970, which, when compared with documents from the
Taiwanese independence movement in the U.S., will likely enhance our understanding of Taiwan's authoritarian regime.
 Thirdly, current understanding of the Formosan community in North America largely focuses on literature related to the independence movement. However, these documents concentrate on political discussions and do not deeply explore the daily lives and emotional states of the North
American Formosan community. This book, from the perspective of Taiwanese Christian missions in North America, describes the mental and physical conditions of the Formosan community and records the early situations of the American Taiwanese churches—speci cally the Presbyterian
Church and the Taiwan Gospel Church—while arguing from a Christian faith standpoint about how to confront the challenges of the independence movement. Such discourse is also bene cial for contemporary churches to re ect on how to balance political engagement with spiritual concerns.
 Finally, research on the Formosan community in North America heavily relies on oral histories from those involved. However, with the signi cant changes in the political environment of the
Republic of China, individuals may reinterpret past experiences in uenced by current circumstances, making it dif cult to understand their genuine feelings during those events. This book can be seen as a semi-autobiography of the author, providing insights into how he perceived the challenges faced by Taiwanese people. When the author emphasizes the need to build a community of hope, he also acknowledges that the emphasis on hope re ects the "despair" of the current reality. A close reading of the text allows readers to enter the author's life journey and resonate with a young person living abroad, observing compatriots in America feeling lost, with the political situation in their homeland becoming increasingly oppressive and seemingly devoid of a future. In such a context of uncertainty, how can one establish a community of hope grounded in the spirit of Christian faith? This mission may also represent the ultimate concern for future intellectuals caring for the land of Taiwan.
 By empathizing with the life experiences and emotions of North American Taiwanese individuals, we can understand their struggles in a foreign land and their concerns for Taiwan.
Different groups have varied life histories; if we show genuine concern for the suffering of souls, we may set aside national and political prejudices and collectively contemplate how to foster a community of hope.
 May God guide us. Amen.
