# Chapter VI — Toward a Community of Hope (Part 2)

“What we are already saying is that hope is, in its most general terms, a sense of the possible, that what we really need is possible, though di cult, while hopelessness means to be ruled by the sense of ffi ffi

 the impossible. Hope therefore involves three basic ideas that could not be simpler: What I hope for I do not yet have or see; it may be di cult, but I can have it — it is possible.”

 Indeed, hope is paradoxical. It is neither passive waiting nor is it unrealistic forcing of circumstances that cannot occur. To hope means to be ready at every moment for that which is not yet born, and yet not become desperate if there is no birth in our lifetime. There is no sense in hoping for that which already exists or for that which cannot be.

 B. Hope Is Adventure

 In his scholarly work Homo Viator, Gabriel Marcel often speaks of hoping, the verb, and not hope, the noun. I think this is a rst step in the study. Hoping is a process; a psychic activity of the person. It is not just an idea but a real, live occurrence in a concrete and knowable setting. In what kind of setting does it occur? Hoping occurs, according to Marcel, when a person is visited by a calamity. Only when a person feels caught can he hope. If everything goes perfectly well, hoping cannot enter the picture. In order to hope one must rst have a sense of captivity, of being caught by the human condition, in the platonic sense of the word. The less life is felt as captivity, the less is a person susceptible to hope.
 We note the increase in despair, of un-hope, among some Formosan people in the United
 States, especially after Nixon took his step on New China Policy. While working with Formosan people in Los Angeles areas as my level IV project of San Francisco Theological Seminary, I tried to translate the word “hopeless” which was so often on some lips. Persistently they used the word to stand for “helplessness” on the one hand and “fearfulness” on the other. They were unable to visualize a future in which personal identity and national independency were possible. As an
 Oriental immigrant in a big city in this country, hopelessness is also illustrated in the uneasiness and the estrangement of migrant life, which is often called “racial gap” and “cultural shock”.
 Hoping does not merely deal with possibility, but also with the realization of possibilities. Roy
 W. Fairchild remarks,

 “Hope allows one to live in uncertainty. Hope cannot predict that such and such will happen, nor claim that such and such must happen. But because the hoping person sees reality as open-ended and not xed, he assumes that reality has resources which are as yet undiscovered and untapped. This is his risk and his adventure.”

 In hoping, one lives life as an adventure indeed.
 Therefore, some element of uncertainty, then, is always present in hoping. Marcel has pointed out that hoping is not a listless waiting, but that it is a psychic activity. Hoping includes reality testing, perhaps with some sadness over the harshness of reality. Tension between the actual and the possible is the climate in which hoping can thrive. From the point of view of knowledge and fi

 ffi judgment, this tension is one between certainty and uncertainty. Both must be present, in a dialectic relation, for hoping to occur. In the third book of his Ethics, Spinoza describes hoping as: “A certain
(unsteady) joy which has been elicited in us by the representation of a future or past happening (event) of whose course we are still uncertain.” And he adds in the discussion: “As long as he hopes he also fears that the event will not take a favorable course.” In terms of Menninger’s “consciousness of the realizable wish,” this means that there is in hoping also a consciousness of the unrealizability of the wish.

 C. Hope Is Sharing

 Often hope is considered an interior strength, some believe one possesses alone. But can one hope as a lone individual? You can wish, or long, or hallucinate, or dream as an isolated person. But hope is basically a sharing experience. It requires a responsive other if one is to discover real hope.
 Marcel stresses that true hoping requires an interpersonal context. Isolation leads to hopelessness and to disintegration of personality functioning. When communication between persons fails, the isolated individual goes to pieces. For human beings, mutual help is part of the nature of hope; hope is the anticipation of helpful relationship. Hope grows in a network of human caring. Theologically speaking, hope is the Holy Spirit in the Christian Church. Without the experience of the Spirit, there is no distinctly Christian understanding of hope; and even if Jesus had spoken much about it, it would have signi ed little beyond what Judaism already understood.
He could tell his disciples to be of good courage, to look up and lift up their heads when circumstances grew black and threatening; but a theology of hope must wait until the Holy Spirit could take Christ’s teaching and expound it in the post-resurrection community. Be that as it may, it is undeniably true that the Spirit has a great deal to do with Hope in the New Testament; and the
Holy Spirit is the very life of hope, of the Christian Church, therefore. Fundamentally speaking,
Christian Church is a Community of Hope. Without hope, there will never have been such a thing as “Christian Church.”
 Hope is sharing. The language of hope does not accentuate action verbs, but verbs of relationships and receptivity. A hope is found, it is giving, it is receiving. One hopes with, though sometimes for someone else. Hoping is basically a shared experience. What William James said about courage and faith is pertinent here: “Just as our courage is so often a re ex of another’s courage, so our faith is apt to be ... a faith in someone else’s faith.”
 So it is with hoping.
 Here we have some profound implications for the nature of the minister-churchmember relation. Hoping, is of its essence. Who gives hope to whom is an irrelevant question; the point is that hoping is generated in the relation. In addition to the processes of transference and countertransference, which are products of derailed wishing, there is the process of hoping, shared by churchmember and minister.
 V. Christian Church as Community of Hope

 A. Hope and Christian Faith

 Hope is a psychic concomitant to life and growth. When hope has gone, life has ended, actually or potentially. Hope is an intrinsic element of the structure of life, the dynamic of man’s spirit. It is closely linked with another element of the structure of life, Faith. Faith is not a weak form of belief or knowledge; it is not faith in this or that; faith is the conviction of the not yet proven, the knowledge of the real possibility, the awareness of pregnancy. Faith, like hope, is not a prediction of the future; it is the vision of the present in a state of pregnancy.
 In Hebrew the word “faith” (emunah) means certainty. Amen means certainly. It is certainty about the reality of the possibility — but it is not certainty in the sense of the unquestionable predictability. This is a paradox of faith: It is the certainty of the uncertain. It is certainty in terms of man’s vision and comprehension; it is not certainty in terms of the nal outcome of reality. We need no faith in that which is scienti cally predictable, nor can there be faith in that which is impossible. Faith is based on our experience of living, of transforming ourselves. Faith that others can change is the outcome of the experience that I can change. Hope is the mood that accompanies faith. Faith cannot be sustained without the mood of hope, and hope can have no base except in faith.
 To love means to commit oneself without guarantee, to give oneself completely in the hope that our love will produce love in the loved person. Love is act of hope and whoever has no hope also has no love. Accordingly, hope keeps love alive. Hope opens the freedom for love to express itself completely, to take on pain and suffering; and suffering presupposes love. He who does not love does not suffer. One can avoid suffering by not attaching oneself to anything, by making oneself insensitive to happiness and pain. All things and conditions and life relationships then become replaceable and exchangeable. Only when one loves something or someone, does this thing or person become irreplaceable. At this point, one becomes open to injury, vulnerable, and the possibility of suffering develops.
 If we attempt to overcome suffering by the creation of a culture of non-attachment, or of attachment, which are loose and may at any time be dissolved, and create a consumer situation of the replaceability of all things, then pain can indeed be avoided; but the frustration of life, emptiness and boredom, ensues.
 Dietrich Bonhoeffer once wrote:

 “To be a Christian does not mean to be religious in a particular way ... But to be a Christian is to be a man. It is not some religious act that makes a Christian what he is, but participation in the su ering of God in the life of world ... One must abandon every attempt to make something of oneself, ... But rather, to take life in one’s stride and helplessness.” ff fi

 B. God of Promise

 For the Israelite and Christian spirit, truth is found in the advent of that new thing which God has promised. Their relationship to truth is, therefore, one of hope. Change in history and changes of history acquire meaning when the promised new thing must be expected in history. The inconceivable, which is called fate or chance, becomes secondary for Christian hope, for it is not directed toward a God of fate or chance but rather toward the God of promise.
 A promise is no Cassandra-like prophecy, but requires hope and a new mode of action. Only when Christian hope recalls its own unique basis — the God of promise, the God of exodus in the
 Old Testament and the God of the resurrection in the New Testament — can it become a critical and active partner of the present mentality of planning and the model of the world of tomorrow.
 If we read the Old Testament, paying attention to the history of early fathers of Israel, and then look back to the contemporary Formosan experience, we come to a very interesting view and nd a close similarity between these two peoples. Both Israelite and Formosan left their homeland as a wandering people looking forward to the day of returning and dreaming of a country of their own, but in the process losing themselves in a foreign land, being confused by foreign value systems, and so forth. Like the Israelites of old, they search for the meaning of life. Probably this is why among Formosans, according to percentage, there are more Christians committed to the Formosan
 Independence Movement than non-Christians — since the phenomenon of the Formosan
 Independence Movement logically is a Hope Movement. Formosan Christians learned from the
 Bible and from history that fundamentally Christianity is a community of searching. This
 “searching” means, rather, breaking out from what is given toward the future, which is promised but not yet visible, in contrast to “having.”
 In this way, this searching of contemporary Formosans parallels the exodus of Abraham and
 Israel and other exodus stories in the Bible toward the future of the promise. This is a community of hope. Its God is the “God of hope” (Rom. 15:13), the “exodus God”, in E. Bloch’s terms He who is powerful and meaningful in history. The dynamics of hope then can be realized in the sacri ce in love.
 Only hope has sustaining and enduring patience; only searching leads to continuance. For
 Christians, continuing in hope, however, is continuing in the body of Christ, in his word, in love.
 Hope for the Christian is bound up with the call “go forth from the camp” and bear abuse for
 Christ. The going forth in hope is bound up here also with the active acceptance of suffering.
 Christian hope, therefore, is not empty promises for another world; rather, it places these concerns here and now in the front of the apostolic events in the midst of the pain of love.
 Hope leads forth from the camp into abuse. The expectation of the divine future prepares one to take upon oneself the “cross of the future” in Hegel’s terms. In Letters and Papers from Prison,
 Dietrich Bonhoeffer called “this worldliness of Christianity living in the midst of the multitude of tasks, questions, successes and failures, and taking seriously not man’s personal su ering, but rather the su ering of God in the world.” Suffering is found only where one loves. He who loves, suffers and ff

 ff endures. Therefore, the church itself is not the home of salvation, where all contradictions are overcome and that which is torn is healed.

 C. Hoping Community

 The primary reality of human consciousness is not the individual but the community; the individual person develops creativity only in the context of a community. Such a community, to be healthy, must respect the uniqueness of each person. Each person, to be healthy, must respect his brotherhood with others. Community and person are interrelated and cannot be understood apart from one another.
 For a human being, the search for identity and the search for community are one and the same.
 A man discovers who he is both in solitude and in the company of others. A community is a brotherhood, both of mutual reconciliation and mutual criticism. To love, to hope, and to faith, men must rst nd themselves; to nd themselves, they must nd one another. Identity and community are both found by one same decision. A decision to live in a certain way, a decision to live open to both one’s own self and to the self of each of one’s brother.
 Keep in mind this understanding of person and his relationship to community; then we can go forward to view the church as a community of hope. William Lynch says: “Hope is an act of City of
 Man, ... Hope must be some way or other be an act of a community, ... people develop hope in each other.”
 In his study of the Epistle to the Ephesians (Commenting on Ephesians 2:14), Professor Markus
 Barth says that the metaphor of the broken wall points to the true meaning of the work of Christ who has broken down every division between men. And he added: “To say Christ means to say community, coexistence, a new life, peace.” And Dietrich Bonhoeffer has written: “There is in fact only one religion from which the concept of community is essentially inseparable, and that is the
 Christian religion.”
 Indeed, Christian Church is a community, but it is not a Community of “Past”; it is not a
 Community of “Now”; and it is not even a Community of “Future.” For hope is historical. Michael
 Novak is right when he says, “To be a Christian is to be a member of historical people.” So accordingly, hope is not to be mistaken for a futurism that ees from the burden and the joys of the present. It is not an escapist trick; it is not creating a new “God of the gaps” so that we merely slip into the future the problems we cannot solve in the present. If anything, it calls us to the front line of world history as the missionary people of God. As a community of hope, we live in the present, in view of the past, in face of the future.
 Not only are we Christians, all human beings are called to hope, to keep on hoping. The
 Christian Gospel enforces this attitude toward life. The Gospel communicates the reality of all three arrows of time, but that is by opening the speci c reality of the future that the Gospel grants also the past and the present.
 fi fi
 Church as a community of hope means that church is a “moving church” — moving toward the future, the eschatological “Kingdom of God”. In the moving community, people pray together, worship together, share together and hope together. Church is not dogma. Dogma is strict, narrow and exclusive; only those who seek security in conformity are provided for. Over-protection and the demand of blind obedience, however, prevent growth into interpersonal responsibility. We should not expect that everyone will enter the church through the same door and proceed toward his salvation on the one common road. Church must have many doors letting everyone in.
 Church is not the Kingdom of God but a community of hope. Religious teaching such as
“theology of hope” cannot be much more than a pointer, a road sign. Whether in what manner and how far the “faithful” will venture on the path of personal experience will be largely a matter of individual disposition and level of maturity and possibly the wisdom of one’s spiritual guide. All too often, however, we nd the situation which Alan W. Watts formulates so delightfully in The Wisdom of Insecurity:

 “The common error of customary religious practice is that one mistakes the symbol for reality, that one only looks at the nger which points out the way and then prefers to suck it comfortably, instead of following the direction it indicates.”

 D. “Out” of Hope

 The term “ekklesia” which one nds in certain passages (for example, Matthew 16:18 and
18:17), even more so if taken in connection with verses like Matthew 24:31 or John 15:19, indicates that the “chosen” are in no way to be called into or stay in the shelter of any organized community, but rather out of their customary attachment and security, out of the world.
 To follow Christ is to be venturing (hope is adventure, we noted in early part of this chapter) out of tradition, out of “consensus of opinion”, out of any comfortable settling down in the shelter of the anonymous mass.

 “to be called out, to have to leave one’s attachments, one’s securities, for something as vague and intangible as ‘spirit’, ‘truth’, ‘light’ is very threatening; most people hate to be ‘out of things’, ‘out of contact with events’, ‘out of date’, ‘out of fashion’, ‘out of a group’, ‘out of job’; one cannot bear to be suspended in the uncertain, unlimited and formless, but hastens to precipitate oneself into some concrete structure, whatever it may be. If nothing else is o ered as a shelter, the ‘structure’ may be a neurotic defense. Instead of being ‘out of everything’, one may choose to be at least ‘in trouble’. This human tendency to seek security in the concrete and variable is something that we have to keep in mind in the development of the Christian Church out of what is given in the Gospel.” fi ff

 In other words, a truly Christlike life would consist in having the same courage to discover and ful ll one’s own mission, even if it carries one against the stream of fashion and public opinion and perhaps into a situation “whither we would not” (John 21:18). It is paradoxical to Christian life, but it is what hope really means.

 Robert Lee in his last page of Strange in the Land concludes:

 “In a world of accelerated change — where revolutionary technological pattern of life have silently crept in — only the Christian who sees himself as the stranger in the land can truly feel ‘at home’. The Christian is armed with hope and con dence, born of realism and the realization that we are temporary and fallen residents of this earth. Men of faith are enabled to see beyond their present existence to heavenly city ... and so, in his dual citizenship, the Christian always has one eye on the
 ‘City of Man’ and one on the ‘City of God’. Christians everywhere are all strangers in the land. But we are not stranger to God. We too are known to God. We look to him as the author, nisher and perfecter of our faith, as we run the good race and ght the good ght, hoping and trusting that His righteousness will reign, and that all men will dwell in His Kingdom, which is Kingdom without end.”

 Marxists have charged religion as being “the opiate of the people.” We should not ignore or cover up this charge. In history certain religions have quieted the conscience of the oppressed, but it is unfair to historic reality if we do not point out that this is only half the picture. There have been a number of occasions when religion was not an opiate but the driving force of a new civilization.
 The Christian Gospel does not individualize man; it stimulates him to see his personal responsibility toward his neighbors in the community, not because he has something to give them, but because he has encountered the eternal God who becomes man to share the burden of all men. The Christian faith does not lead man to retire behind an individual’s religious wall, but sends him out into society.
 The Gospel proclaims that God becomes man to overcome man’s sin and death by participating in man’s suffering and struggle. When the people of God accept this vision of the Gospel, they become the creative and driving force in history, functioning as the salt of the earth, the mustard seed in the eld and the leaven in the bread. Man has irreplaceable and irreducible hope in Jesus Christ. It is the hope with which one can continually present a Christian witness in the midst of our struggling world. This hope is based on the historic event: God became man in order that man may become true man.

 CONCLUSION

 It would be super uous and unduly repetitious to summarize the entire discussion at this point.
 Adequate resumes occur at various signi cant junctures of this study. The point of these nal pages is simply to bring together in synthetic form the various critical conclusions that have been reached, if only tentatively, throughout this project.
 First of all, historically, Formosans have been struggling for their self-determination, Formosan nationalism, for almost thirty years but not yet gained. In the process of the struggling, people live in misery and panic. They call themselves “the silent majority”. The future of Formosan independence lies mainly in the hand of the majority itself. The real test of nationalism is a people’s willingness to ght for. In other words, when a person is independent, a nation is independent. Realistic and dynamic hope is the key to the problems of independence, personal and national as well. To achieve a goal, it is not enough just to do something. One must also know what to do. To be of any use at all, efforts to achieve a goal must be guided by practical understanding of the problem to be solved, by some kind of plan for achieving one’s purpose. Efforts to achieve a goal are efforts to execute a plan. Hope tends to achieve a plan.
 Secondly, to those new immigrants from Formosa we must not see and help them as regular immigrants. They have their particular problems which regular immigrants do not have. As a
 Christian concern, we should welcome them and assist them in every sense, which Presbyterian
 Church has already shown in terms of helping them develop a Formosan ethnic church. It is too early — since it is just 12 months old — to evaluate their achievement to Christian ministry.
 However, the evaluation of a church should not be based on their membership drive or nancial support, which we used to do. The goal of a church is to carry out Christian mission, not to self- support. This is especially true to a new ethnic church. Ministry concerns primarily people (person) not head-counting or real estates. The church is where a group of people can touch one another’s heart and feel they are one in the Holy Spirit. The church is where people can be touched by the
 Gospel.
 The Formosan group is just an instance of the new phase of immigrants of the Asian nationalities, to which the Christian Gospel should try to reach. This is a grand chance of foreign mission made possible within the national boundary. This migration phenomenon is, therefore, challenging the church at every level of its life. As Cesar and Ramalho have appropriately said: “To escape the migrant problem is to escape Christ.”
 Thirdly, in principle, it seems good to attend more closely to our actual experience than to theories, formulas or doctrine which purports to articulate and to direct that experience. For experience is in principle richer, more complex, and more full of compass. Fresh attention to experience is a major source of newer and more accurate formulas. Of course, the relationship between experience and theory is reciprocal; theory points us in a direction, helps us to escape from unobservant and con ned habits, often awaken us to the world around us. Theory, however, is for fi fi

 the sake of life, and sometimes the lure of life exceeds what present theories can handle. Therefore, on this study I tried to keep one eye on the academic Hope Movement of today’s theological concerns, and the other eye on the practical Hope Movement, the attitudes toward the future of the
 Formosan communities in the United States at large. In the actual involvement, I attempted to discern how theology of hope could be mobilized in the groups and individuals with whom I worked as a religious leader. My experience tells me that since Hope is the best language and subject to communicate with these Formosans since as new immigrants, as a minority, and as a silenced people their ultimate concern is toward a hopeful life.
 Fourthly, from my personal experience and the 12 months of investigation on the matter of dynamics of hope among Formosans. My attention was attracted by the fact that according to percentage there are much more Christians than non-Christians involved in the Formosan Hope
 Movement -- Formosan Independence Movement. Gradually, I came to the conclusion that the phenomenon was from a Biblical Hope Movement. Those Formosan activists seem to have learned from the Biblical Hope Movement a great deal. They seem to identify contemporary Formosan history with Israelite Exodus of Biblical time. Furthermore, they seem to identify themselves with those Exodus characters such as Abraham, Israel, Moses, Joshua, and Jesus according to their own interpretation in one way or another.
 Ideology of people strengthens the courage to solve problems. Theology is one of the most important guidelines to Christian life, sacred and secular as well. If word is power, the Greatest
 Power for human beings is the Word of God. Jesus event is, therefore, the historical “Power to
 People”, because he brings man The Hope. Creative action is possible only in the context of hope and power when man envisages a future and nds himself powerful enough to master his environment, through his action, in order to achieve his goals. No hope, no future. The oppressed consciousness, however, is deprived of both these elements. It has no future. The future belongs to the master.
 From Christian view of history, because the present is not seen as nal, it becomes possible and necessary to search for those possibilities that are absent from the present. The consciousness then projects itself in the direction of the future, giving birth to hope. Paul Freire remarks:

 “This climate of hope is born at the exact moment when a given society begins to turn upon itself and nds out that it is not nished, that there is an in nite number of tasks to be accomplished. The hopelessness and pessimism which existed before about its present and future, as well as the naïve optimism, given place to a critical optimism, to hope.”

One discovers then that “history has not ended, that a better society is possible, and that it is worth dying for.” Only this consciousness is able to shape the new tomorrow according to its hopes.
 Hope is the opposite of fatalism and decisionism.

 For it is when we hope in hopeless, or impossible solution, that the feeling of hopelessness and despair take over and we feel that everything is hopeless and that there is no possible solution. In other words, it is when we mix hope with what is hopeless that we become lled with hopelessness.
 It is the recognition that some ways are hopeless that open up for us the way to nd hope. The important thing is not to think that all things can be hoped for but to keep the hopeless out of hope, to separate the impossible from the possible.
