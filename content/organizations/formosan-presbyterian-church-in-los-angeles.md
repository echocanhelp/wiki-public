# Formosan Presbyterian Church in Los Angeles (FPCLA)

**Formosan Presbyterian Church in Los Angeles (FPCLA)** was the first Taiwanese Presbyterian church established in North America, founded in 1970 in Los Angeles, California. The church was established under the United Presbyterian Church in the USA (UPCUSA, now PC(USA)) system and served as a spiritual anchor for Taiwanese immigrants in Southern California.

## Identity Snapshot

- **Chinese name:** 洛杉磯台美人基督教會 (literal); 福爾摩沙長老教會 (Formosan Presbyterian Church)
- **Founded:** 1970
- **Location:** Los Angeles, California
- **Denomination:** United Presbyterian Church in the USA (UPCUSA/PC(USA))
- **Type:** Taiwanese-language Presbyterian congregation
- **Status:** Historical founding church; FPCLA lineage continued through subsequent Taiwanese Presbyterian churches

## Founding History

### Predecessor: Taiyou Church (太陽教會)

Before FPCLA, an experimental church called "Taiyou Church" (太陽教會, "Sun Church") was established in 1967 by Rev. Jonah Chang (張瑞雄) at a Japanese Methodist church facility. This experiment laid the groundwork for a separate Taiwanese congregation.

### Founding Process (1970)

FPCLA was founded through the collaboration of:

- **Taiwanese believers:** A group of Taiwanese immigrants including Dr. & Mrs. Daniel Kao (高光民), Dr. & Mrs. C. C. Chen (陳昭俊), Mr. & Mrs. Norman Hsu (許彬), Dr. & Mrs. Isiah Lee (李宗派), Dr. & Mrs. Joseph T. C. Lu (盧俊哲), Mr. & Mrs. Morris Chen (陳茂雄), Mr. & Mrs. George Chen (陳進琛), Dr. & Mrs. Sze-Ya Yeh (葉思雅), Mr. & Mrs. Wang Chun Hsiung (王春雄), Dr. & Mrs. Jang Yan Wu (吳政彥), Dr. Wen-Cho Yang (楊哲), Dr. & Mrs. Justin Chen (陳有義), Meiling Lai (賴美玲), Michael Chen (陳明章), and others
- **UPCUSA support:** Pastors and elders from the United Presbyterian Church system, including San Francisco Theological Seminary, the General Assembly of the United Presbyterian Church, the Southern California/Hawaii Presbytery, and the Pacific Presbytery
- **Organizing Pastor:** Dr. Chaolong Kao (高昭龍), born 1932, was called as the church's first organizing pastor. He graduated from Taiwan Theological College and Seminary (TTCS), was sent to study in the US by President Shoki Coe (鄺輝迎), returned to teach at TTCS where Dr. Albert S. Lai was a student, and later accepted the call to FPCLA

### Theological Vision

Dr. Albert S. Lai, who participated in the founding and later wrote his doctoral dissertation on the church, articulated a vision that transcended the church being merely a haven for immigrants. FPCLA was intended to become a "mission base" in the local community — a missional community that would witness to the gospel in the larger society.

## Founding Process: A Detailed Account

### The First Experimental Worship (August 1970)

On August 9, 1970, Reverend Jonah Chang (張瑞雄), a former minister of the Presbyterian Church of Formosa then serving at the United Methodist Church in Fresno, California, led the first experimental Sunday worship service for Formosan Christians in Los Angeles. Twenty-two adults and eleven children attended — a turnout that exceeded expectations and confirmed the viability of a dedicated Formosan congregation. Over the following four weeks, the experimental church drew growing numbers, culminating in a strong conviction among the majority that a permanent church fellowship was essential.

### From Prayer Circle to Congregation

Before the church was formally organized, a spontaneous circle of 20 to 30 Formosan Christians had been meeting every other Saturday night in rotating homes throughout early 1970. This prayer meeting circle — some members previously associated with the First Evangelical Chinese Church — chose Saturday nights specifically so that those already affiliated with Sunday churches could also participate. The group made plans, held discussions, and laid the groundwork for the church's organization.

In August 1970, Rev. Chang's one-month experimental church served as the catalyst. When the experiment concluded at the end of August and Rev. Chang returned to Fresno, the congregation envisioned the importance of carrying on the fellowship and decided to organize a church — despite having no pastor and no outside financial support. With the assistance of Centenary United Methodist Church and its minister, Rev. Peter Chen, they used the Wesley Hall facilities of Centenary United Methodist Church until they moved to Wilshire Crest Presbyterian Church in July 1971.

### UPCUSA Connection and Presbytery Approval

The Presbyterian Church of Formosa was the largest and only financially independent denomination on the island, and most members of the new church were proud Presbyterians. From the beginning, they seriously considered association with the United Presbyterian Church in the USA (UPCUSA). Through continuous contact with the Synod Office of Southern California and the Presbytery of Los Angeles Southwest, the Formosan Christian Church decided in February 1971 to join the UPCUSA and requested financial and moral support.

The Presbytery Committee voted on March 27, 1971, to work with the church leadership to develop a plan for a self-supporting United Presbyterian congregation within three to five years. A formal funding request submitted on April 14, 1971, stated: "The present request is for support of what the Committee feels to be already a highly significant ministry and mission that offers promise of becoming a successful model by a somewhat different form of church development with ethnic minorities within our Synod."

The Presbytery of Los Angeles Southwest approved the proposed organization of the Formosan Presbyterian Church on September 28, 1971, at a meeting held at Brentwood Presbyterian Church. The Presbytery Committee concluded that "this group has a valid and viable mission." Reverend Dr. Charles C.L. Kao (高光民), a native Formosan and former Assistant Professor at Tainan Theological College (then a Visiting Scholar at Harvard University), was called as the organizing minister in September 1971.

### The Presbytery's Strategic Rationale

The Presbytery Committee identified two areas compelling their support: (1) a growing emphasis on investing church resources where there already existed a significant "spark of life" in the people to be served, and (2) the Presbytery's intent to be responsive to changing urban demographics. The proposed congregation was consistent with Synod and General Assembly guidelines because it:

- Served a clearly defined community with needs peculiar to Formosan immigrants
- Included a strong corporate ministry encompassing both Christians and non-Christians
- Kept open options for changing ministry styles without burdening the congregation with building concerns
- Emphasized interdependence with other congregations and programs of the Synod and General Assembly

The Presbytery also recognized that a ministry to Formosan Presbyterians and the opportunity to reach non-Christian Formosans with the Gospel could not be realized apart from a Formosan church, and that effective unity of the church could be enhanced as new United Presbyterians actively participated in denominational life.

### Proposed Budget (1971–1974)

The church's leadership developed a four-year budget projection reflecting the expectation of gradual self-support:

| 1971 | $3,750 | $2,800 | $550 | $240 | $7,340 |

Total projected expenses (including ministerial support, Christian education, housing, car allowance, pension, administration, utilities, activities, and general mission) matched income projections, with the Presbytery and Synod of Southern California expected to cover the gap until self-support was achieved.

The occupational profile of members and non-members reflected the highly educated nature of the community: 19 engineers, 17 students, 10 laboratory researchers, 8 in business, 6 in computer science, 5 medical doctors, 5 social workers, and 10 in other professions.

Sunday morning worship attendance averaged between 70 and 90 (including 15–20 children), with half being non-Christians searching for fellowship and the enlightenment of Christian faith. The church was attracting new attendees at a rate of 2–5 persons weekly.

## Mission and Community Role

In early Taiwanese immigrant life, FPCLA functioned as:

- **Worship community in the mother tongue:** Taiwanese-language worship for immigrants, with Sunday morning services in Formosan language
- **Social support network:** Assistance for new arrivals navigating life in America, including immigration, employment, and health consultation
- **Space for identity negotiation:** A place where Taiwanese immigrants could negotiate faith, identity, and public belonging
- **Missional community:** A beacon of hope and witness to the gospel in the larger society
- **Spiritual anchor:** A familiar place amid cultural transition, linguistic barriers, and identity uncertainty
- **Cultural center:** Saturday afternoon open church providing facilities for table tennis, volleyball, games, music, and folk dancing

### Church Activities

The church designed its activities around the fundamental needs of the community and individuals:

- **Sunday worship service** in Formosan language, with 70–90 average attendance
- **Joint worship with Wilshire Crest United Presbyterian Church** on the last Sunday of every month, cooperated by pastors of both congregations
- **Agape Meal** potluck lunch after each Sunday service, featuring native food
- **Sunday School** with 15–20 students in three classes (preschool through sixth grade)
- **Choir** of about 15 members, practicing hymns, classics, and Formosan folk songs
- **Saturday afternoon open church** as a social center for the entire Formosan community
- **Family prayer meetings** every other Friday night at members' homes, with Bible study, theological textbook study, and discussion of Christian living
- **Monthly lectures** on topics such as "Computer and Modern Man," "How to Find a Doctor If You Are Sick," and "Income Tax and You," open to the entire community
- **Retreats**, including a successful Christmas retreat at Lazy-W Ranch with 70 adults and children

### Theological Vision

Dr. Albert S. Lai, who participated in the founding and later wrote his doctoral dissertation on the church, articulated a vision that transcended the church being merely a haven for immigrants. FPCLA was intended to become a "mission base" in the local community — a missional community that would witness to the gospel in the larger society. The church was seen as a place where people could "touch one another's heart and feel that they are one in Holy Spirit," and where people could be "touched by the proclamation of the Gospel and be sent to serve the world."

## Historical Significance

### First Taiwanese Church in North America

FPCLA was the first Taiwanese Presbyterian church established in North America, setting a precedent for subsequent Taiwanese Presbyterian churches across the continent. Over 40 Taiwanese Presbyterian churches would be established in North America in the decades following FPCLA's founding.

### Foundation for PC(USA) Connection

By establishing FPCLA under the UPCUSA/PC(USA) system, the founders created a connection with Presbyterian churches around the world, maximizing strength to care for the future of Taiwanese people. This connection resonated with church headquarters in New York, including the UPCUSA, National Council of Churches (NCC), and World Council of Churches (WCC) in Geneva.

### Dissertation and Legacy

FPCLA was the central case study in Dr. Albert S. Lai's 1971 doctoral dissertation *Toward A Community of Hope — A Mission to Formosan Community in Los Angeles*, which documented the church's struggles, aspirations, and theological vision. The dissertation was reprinted in 2025 with a 50th anniversary foreword.

## Theological Framework

### "Internal Integration and External Harmony"

Dr. Albert S. Lai's dissertation articulated a theological vision centered on the concept of "internal integration and external harmony" — a framework for understanding how the church could serve Formosan immigrants while maintaining their cultural identity.

**Internal Integration** refers to the degree to which an individual has achieved psychological coherence and stability. For Formosan immigrants, this meant:
- Maintaining cultural identity while adapting to American society
- Finding spiritual meaning beyond work and family
- Developing a sense of belonging in a foreign land
- Choosing love in adversity, service in marginality, and witness in brokenness

**External Harmony** refers to the relationship between the individual and the broader society. This meant:
- Engaging with American society while maintaining distinctiveness
- Building bridges between Formosan culture and American culture
- Serving as a missional community in the local context
- Contributing to the broader society while preserving unique identity

### The Church as "Community of Hope"

The vision for FPCLA was not merely to be a haven for immigrants, but to become a **dynamic body for the hope of the world**. This vision was rooted in:

- **Biblical truth:** Drawing from Acts and Pauline letters, the church as a dynamic body for the hope of the world
- **Missional community:** Choosing to serve in marginality and witness in brokenness
- **Cultural integrity:** Maintaining Formosan identity while engaging with American society
- **Political consciousness:** Understanding the Formosan independence movement as a self-preservation movement of the island's people

### Formosan Identity and Independence

The dissertation addressed the complex political identity of Formosans in the United States:

- The vast majority of Formosans in the US supported the independence movement
- Most hoped to remain abroad until their home island became a democratic republic safe from both Nationalist and Communist rule
- Younger Formosans, influenced by the civil rights movement and Vietnam War debates, were more likely to adopt militant positions
- The church served as a space for navigating these political tensions while maintaining spiritual focus
