# John Chiang (江俊輝)

**John Chiang** (江俊輝, born 1962) is a Taiwanese American politician who serves as **California State Controller**, the state's chief financial officer. He was elected to the office in 2006 at age 45 and has served since. He previously worked as a prosecutor for 12 years.

- **Siblings:** 江宜玲 (Joyce Chiang, 1965–1999) — immigration lawyer, Clinton administration; 江俊德 (Robert Chiang) — physician; 江俊立 (Roger Chiang) — MBA, producer on *American Most Wanted*

- **Geography:** California (Sacramento / Los Angeles / Washington DC)

## Early Life and Education

John Chiang was born in New York City in 1962 to Taiwanese immigrant parents. His father, 江牧東 (Mudong Chiang), was a Taipei-born chemical engineer who graduated from National Taiwan University in the 1950s and earned a PhD in chemical engineering from Cleveland Institute of Technology. His mother, 沈吟香 (Yinhsiang Shen), was from Tainan, studied in Japan, and met his father at the University of Notre Dame. His maternal grandfather, 沈榮 (Shen Rong), was a highly respected lawyer in Tainan who helped the poor — a visit to him left a lasting impression on young John, who decided then he wanted to be a lawyer.

The family moved to a Chicago suburb (about 30 minutes from Chicago) where they were the first Asian American family in the neighborhood. Young John faced racial discrimination: other children excluded him and his siblings, threw stones at their house, wrote insults on walls, and destroyed their mailboxes. When his younger brother Roger was learning to ride a bicycle, older kids beat him up, so John — three or four years older — fought back to protect his siblings, getting labeled a troublemaker by the school. The family also faced cultural isolation: the children's Taiwanese bento boxes (rice, fish or beef, vegetables) contrasted sharply with other kids' sandwiches. His mother, a devout Catholic, began attending church regularly hoping to find acceptance and bridge cultural differences. She also actively participated in community activities, earning the respect of local elected officials who would advocate on the family's behalf.

The family raised four children with strict traditional Chinese values: treat people with sincerity, no hypocrisy, no vanity, no jealousy. The children's names were chosen so that two start with J (John, Joyce) and two with R (Robert, Roger).

After high school, John earned a full accounting scholarship, attended DePaul University, then transferred to the University of South Florida where he graduated with a finance degree. He then attended **Georgetown University Law Center**, earning his J.D.

## Political Career

### Board of Equalization

In 1987, John moved to Southern California to find new opportunities. The IRS had an opening, and he applied and was hired as a tax law specialist for the IRS Los Angeles office. Through his sister Joyce's internship with Congressman Howard Berman's Los Angeles office, John met Berman's staff who recognized his political acumen and encouraged him to consider public service.

John subsequently worked in the offices of several California political leaders — including then-State Controller Gray Davis, Senator Barbara Boxer, and Congressman Mel Levine — as a legal advisor. He eventually became Chief of Staff for California Board of Equalization Los Angeles district commissioner Brad Sherman. When Sherman was elected to Congress in 1997, John was appointed to fill the vacancy.

The California Board of Equalization is the only democratically elected tax agency in the United States. It collects approximately $40–43 billion in annual taxes, accounting for one-third of the state government's funding. In 1998, John ran for the first time and won with nearly 800,000 votes, becoming the highest-ranking Asian American elected official in California's executive branch.

### State Controller

On January 3, 1999, at age 36, John was sworn in as a member of the California Board of Equalization, representing the 4th district which included Greater Los Angeles and 73 cities.

In November 2006, at age 45, Chiang was elected California State Controller, defeating Republican Tony Strickland with 3,338,081 votes (51%). He became the highest-ranking Taiwanese American in elected executive office in American history. As State Controller, he supervised California's $1 trillion budget and served as the state's chief financial officer.

In 2008, Chiang's political reputation was cemented when he resisted then-Governor Arnold Schwarzenegger's executive order to cut public employee salaries to reduce the deficit. Schwarzenegger sued, but a California judge rejected the governor's request for a temporary restraining order. The case was ultimately won by Chiang in California Superior Court. The *Los Angeles Times* published a special report noting that Chiang's ability to successfully challenge the governor was due not only to his tax expertise but also to his long-standing involvement with labor organizations.

During his tenure, Chiang also refused to pay legislators' salaries when the state budget could not be balanced on time — a move that earned widespread praise from constituents.

In 2010, he was re-elected as State Controller. In 2014, his second term ended and he could not run for re-election due to term limits, so he ran for California State Treasurer, winning in 2014 and taking office in 2015.

### Community Engagement

Throughout his political career, Chiang has consistently given back to the Taiwanese American community. He served as president of the Taiwanese American Lawyers Association and as a board member of the Los Angeles Taiwan Cultural Center. In 2013, Taiwanese community organizations held a fundraiser in Monterey Park supporting his campaign for State Treasurer. World Federation of Taiwanese Congress President 楊信 (Yang Hsin) praised Chiang's sincerity and dedication, urging full community support. TACL President 田詒鴻 (Alan Thian), Taiwan Chamber of Commerce President 陳柏宇, and Taiwan Cultural Association Chairman 王梅鳳 all publicly endorsed him.

Chiang also established a political internship program through TACL, opening his office to young Taiwanese Americans for civic engagement experience.

## Tragedy: Loss of Sister Joyce

On January 9, 1999 — just one week after John was sworn in as a Board of Equalization member — John's sister 江宜玲 (Joyce Chiang, born 1965) went missing. Joyce was an immigration lawyer working for the Clinton administration Immigration and Naturalization Service in Washington, DC, living with her younger brother Roger.

John immediately contacted authorities and coordinated the search. The next day, Joyce's green leather jacket and government work ID were found near Anacostia Park. Four months later, her body was discovered 19 miles from where she disappeared, in the Potomac River. The cause of death could not be determined and no suspects were identified. The case was listed as cold.

In May 2011, after 12 years, Washington Police Chief Cathy Lanier announced the case was solved: Joyce had been murdered. Two suspects were identified but there was insufficient evidence to prosecute. Chief Lanier apologized for investigative errors. John's brother Roger, who had been searching for answers, had connected with the TV show *American Most Wanted* host John Walsh and joined the production team to help solve the case.

Joyce had graduated from Smith College (where she was student body president) and Georgetown University Law School, specializing in immigration law. She was known for her vitality, generosity, and commitment to public service. John said his sister was given to him by God, and though she was gone, he would continue the good work she had started.

## Political Philosophy

Chiang's political image was built on a step-by-step effort process and his commitment to the Taiwanese American community. He is known for his fiscal conservatism, transparency, and dedication to serving the public interest. His supporters come from diverse ethnic constituencies — Asian Americans, Latinos, women, Jewish Americans, African Americans — and he has always maintained ties to the Taiwanese American community.

His childhood experience with racial discrimination shaped his worldview. He witnessed how the absence of diverse voices in government led to outdated practices and unjust laws. Inspired by Japanese American congressmen who started from political interns and expanded their influence to broader civil rights issues, he believed that every field — government agencies, industry organizations — needs diverse talent to improve systems.
