# Dr. Albert S. Lai (賴信雄)

**Dr. Albert S. Lai (賴信雄, Lai Sin-hiong)** is a Taiwanese American pastor, theologian, and institution-builder who helped establish the first Taiwanese Presbyterian Church in North America and authored the landmark 1971 dissertation [[sources/toward-a-community-of-hope|Toward A Community of Hope — A Mission to Formosan Community in Los Angeles]], reprinted in 2025 with a 50th anniversary foreword.

## Identity Snapshot

- **Full name:** Albert S. Lai (賴信雄, Lai Sin-hiong)
- **Era:** 1960s–2020s (active 1960s–1970s; 2025 republication at age 84+)
- **Geography:** Taiwan → San Francisco → Los Angeles, California
- **Education:** San Francisco Theological Seminary (doctoral dissertation); Taiwan Theological College and Seminary (TTCS)
- **Core roles:** Pastor, theologian, seminary educator, FPCLA founding member, dissertation author

## Historical Context

Dr. Lai's life and work sit at the intersection of migration-era pastoral care and institution formation. In 1970, he participated in the founding of the [[organizations/formosan-presbyterian-church-in-los-angeles|Formosan Presbyterian Church in Los Angeles (FPCLA)]], the first Taiwanese church in North America. In 1971, he produced a doctoral dissertation that connected historical memory, migration pressure, identity tension, and communal mission. His work remains a foundational reference for Taiwanese American church history.

## FPCLA Founding (1970)

Dr. Lai was among the enthusiastic believers who co-founded [[organizations/formosan-presbyterian-church-in-los-angeles|FPCLA]] under the theology and system of the United Presbyterian Church (UPCUSA, now PC(USA)). The founding involved:

- **Co-founders:** [[people/daniel-kao|Dr. & Mrs. Daniel Kao (高光民)]], [[people/c-c-chen|Dr. & Mrs. C. C. Chen (陳昭俊)]], Mr. & Mrs. Norman Hsu (許彬), Dr. & Mrs. Isiah Lee (李宗派), Dr. & Mrs. Joseph T. C. Lu (盧俊哲), Mr. & Mrs. Morris Chen (陳茂雄), Mr. & Mrs. George Chen (陳進琛), Dr. & Mrs. Sze-Ya Yeh (葉思雅), Mr. & Mrs. Wang Chun Hsiung (王春雄), Dr. & Mrs. Jang Yan Wu (吳政彥), Dr. Wen-Cho Yang (楊哲), Dr. & Mrs. Justin Chen (陳有義), Meiling Lai (賴美玲), Michael Chen (陳明章), and others
- **Supporting pastors and elders:** From the UPCUSA system including San Francisco Theological Seminary, the General Assembly of the United Presbyterian Church, the Southern California/Hawaii Presbytery, and the Pacific Presbytery
- **Organizing Pastor:** [[people/chaolong-kao|Dr. Charles C.L. Kao / Chaolong Kao (高昭龍)]] — former TTCS faculty / Harvard Visiting Scholar; called by Presbytery of Los Angeles Southwest (Sep 1971). Distinct from co-founder Daniel Kao.
- **Predecessor experiment:** "Taiyou Church" (太陽教會) and the Aug 1970 experimental worship led by [[people/jonah-chang|Rev. Jonah Chang (張瑞雄)]]

## Dissertation: *Toward A Community of Hope* (1971)

> **Primary work page:** [[sources/toward-a-community-of-hope|Toward A Community of Hope (work & sources)]]  
> **Full text (GitHub archive):** [complete extract](https://github.com/echocanhelp/wiki-public/blob/gh-pages/knowledge/web-archives/albert-lai-toward-community-of-hope.md) · [fact sheet](https://github.com/echocanhelp/wiki-public/blob/gh-pages/knowledge/research/albert-lai-facts.md)

Dr. Lai's doctoral dissertation at San Francisco Theological Seminary was titled *Toward A Community of Hope — A Mission to Formosan Community in Los Angeles*. It was:

- **Theological and prophetic:** Called the church to transcend being merely a haven for immigrants and become a missional community, a beacon of hope and witness to the gospel in the larger society
- **Six chapters:** Formosan in Formosa Yesterday; Formosan in Formosa Today; Formosan in The United States; Formosan Christian Church in Los Angeles; Integration, Independence and Identity of Formosan Community; Toward a Community
- **Vision:** The church as a "hopeful community" — not a safe retreat for insiders, but a dynamic body for the hope of the world, choosing love in adversity, service in marginality, and witness in brokenness
- **Significance:** The first Taiwanese church in North America; FPCLA did not exist merely to bridge linguistic or cultural gaps but sought to become a "mission base" in the local community

See also the [[organizations/formosan-presbyterian-church-in-los-angeles|FPCLA]] page for Chapter IV founding timeline, budget, membership, and activities.

## 50th Anniversary Republication (2025)

In 2025, the dissertation was reprinted with a 50th anniversary foreword by [[people/yunching-yeh-davis|Dr. Yunching Yeh Davis]] (NTPC Moderator), along with forewords from the PCT ([[people/en-sheng-pan|Rev. En Sheng Pan]]), [[organizations/national-taiwanese-presbyterian-council|NTPC]] Historical Committee ([[people/ching-fang-chen|Rev. Ching Fang Chen]]), [[people/mingyuan-hsu|Rev. Mingyuan Hsu]] (Good Shepherd TPC), and [[people/pinghsi-liu|Rev. Pinghsi Liu]] (Tainan East Gate Barclay Memorial Church). The republication was published by Taiwan Church Press and the Incorporated Association of Tainan East Gate Barclay History Education Promotion.

### Self-Preface (May 2025)

In his Self-Preface, Dr. Lai reflected:

> "When my esteemed Rev. Mingyuan Hsu of the Los Angeles Good Shepherd Presbyterian Church in Taiwan informed me that Rev. Pinghsi Liu and his team from the Tainan East Gate Barclay Memorial Church were interested in my dissertation and wanted to translate it into Chinese for publication, I was filled with gratitude."

He noted that existing writings about Taiwanese churches in North America all start from the 1980s, while his dissertation reflects the lives of Taiwanese and Taiwanese Christians in Southern California from the 1960s to the early 1970s — a period when Taiwan was under the Kuomintang's White Terror, and thousands of Taiwanese students came to the US each year.

## Recall the Story Behind the Dissertation

In his extended postscript written over 50 years later, Dr. Lai narrated the remarkable story of how his 1971 dissertation was rediscovered, translated, and published — a journey he could never have imagined while writing it.

### Rediscovery and Publication

When the dissertation was presented to readers in 2025, Dr. Lai noted that the intended audience at the time was only about 10 people: his two thesis advisors, one project field consultant, and the seminary faculty thesis review committee. In the information-scarce 1960s, these professors had very limited understanding of Taiwan and had never heard of the Taiwanese people's desire to "rise up" or the persecution faced by the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan.

The dissertation, presented in Chinese to Taiwanese on the island and in America more than 50 years later, was something Dr. Lai could never have imagined. It was Rev. Mingyuan Hsu of the Los Angeles Good Shepherd Presbyterian Church in Taiwan who first approached Dr. Lai, informing him that Rev. Pinghsi Liu and his team from the Tainan East Gate Barclay Memorial Church were interested in the dissertation and wanted to translate it into Chinese for publication. Dr. Lai was filled with gratitude.

The publication journey spanned from 2023 to 2025, culminating in the 50th anniversary republication by Taiwan Church Press and the Incorporated Association of Tainan East Gate Barclay History Education Promotion, with forewords from Dr. Yunching Yeh Davis (NTPC Moderator), the PCT, NTPC Historical Committee, Rev. Mingyuan Hsu, and Rev. Pinghsi Liu.

### Historical Context of the Writing

Dr. Lai emphasized that the dissertation was written during one of the most tumultuous periods in modern history. As a student at San Francisco Theological Seminary from 1968 to 1972, he was immersed in the American cultural revolution — the women's rights movement, civil rights movement, anti-Vietnam War movement, counterculture movement, the assassinations of Martin Luther King Jr. and Robert Kennedy, and the moon landing. Theology students at the time were exposed to "Feminist Theology," "Black Theology," "Theology of the Death of God," "Theology of Liberation," and "Theology of Hope."

Simultaneously, dramatic events unfolded related to Taiwan: the expulsion of the Republic of China from the United Nations, and the April 24, 1970, incident when Peter Huang (黃文雄), a Cornell doctoral candidate, shot at Chingkuo Chiang (蔣經國) at the New York Plaza Hotel. In 1971, the ROC was expelled from the UN, and Taiwan became a "global orphan." Nixon's official visit to China in February 1972 further worsened Taiwan's political situation. Dr. Lai graduated from SFTS in May 1972, during this period of profound disorientation.

## Chapter VI: Toward a Community of Hope

Chapter VI of Dr. Lai's dissertation developed the theological framework that gave the project its name. The chapter explored hope not as passive waiting but as active, shared venture — and the church as the community where hope is generated and sustained.

### Hope Is Possibility

Dr. Lai defined hope as the recognition that what one hopes for is not yet possessed but is possible. He drew on Gabriel Marcel's distinction between "hope" (the noun) and "hoping" (the verb), arguing that hoping is a psychic activity that occurs only when a person feels "caught" by the human condition — in captivity. Only when everything does not go perfectly well can hoping enter the picture.

He noted the increase in despair and "un-hope" among Formosan people in the United States, especially after Nixon's shift on China policy. The word "hopeless" was often used by Formosans to stand for "helplessness" on one hand and "fearfulness" on the other — an inability to visualize a future in which personal identity and national independence were possible.

### Hope Is Adventure

Citing Gabriel Marcel's *Homo Viator*, Dr. Lai argued that hoping is a process — a psychic activity of the person, not just an idea. He quoted Roy W. Fairchild: "Hope allows one to live in uncertainty. Hope cannot predict that such and such will happen, nor claim that such and such must happen. But because the hoping person sees reality as open-ended and not fixed, he assumes that reality has resources which are as yet undiscovered and untapped. This is his risk and his adventure."

### Hope Is Sharing

A central thesis of Chapter VI was that hope is fundamentally a sharing experience. Dr. Lai wrote: "Often hope is considered an interior strength, some believe one possesses alone. But can one hope as a lone individual? ... Hope is basically a sharing experience. It requires a responsive other if one is to discover real hope."

He cited Marcel's insight that true hoping requires an interpersonal context, and isolation leads to hopelessness and disintegration. "For human beings, mutual help is part of the nature of hope; hope is the anticipation of helpful relationship. Hope grows in a network of human caring. Theologically speaking, hope is the Holy Spirit in the Christian Church. Without the experience of the Spirit, there is no distinctly Christian understanding of hope."

### The Church as Community of Hope

Dr. Lai's ecclesiology placed the church squarely as a "community of hope" — not a Community of the Past, not merely a Community of the Now, and not even a Community of the Future. "For hope is historical," he wrote. "Michael Novak is right when he says, 'To be a Christian is to be a member of historical people.' So accordingly, hope is not to be mistaken for a futurism that flees from the burden and the joys of the present. It is not an escapist trick; it is not creating a new 'God of the gaps' so that we merely slip into the future the problems we cannot solve in the present. If anything, it calls us to the front line of world history as the missionary people of God."

He drew on Dietrich Bonhoeffer's vision of Christian life as participation in the suffering of God in the life of the world, and on Robert Lee's conclusion that "the Christian is armed with hope and confidence, born of realism and the realization that we are temporary and fallen residents of this earth."

Dr. Lai also drew a powerful parallel between the Formosan experience and the Israelite Exodus: "Like the Israelites of old, they search for the meaning of life. Probably this is why among Formosans, according to percentage, there are more Christians committed to the Formosan Independence Movement than non-Christians — since the phenomenon of the Formosan Independence Movement logically is a Hope Movement."

### The "Out" of Hope

Dr. Lai explored the radical implication of *ekklesia* — the "called-out" nature of the Christian community. To follow Christ is to venture "out of tradition, out of 'consensus of opinion,' out of any comfortable settling down in the shelter of the anonymous mass." He warned against the common error of mistaking the symbol for reality — "one only looks at the finger which points out the way and then prefers to suck it comfortably, instead of following the direction it indicates."

## Conclusion: Lessons from History

Dr. Lai's conclusion synthesized the dissertation's critical findings across multiple dimensions:

### Formosan Nationalism and Hope

Dr. Lai observed that historically, Formosans had been struggling for self-determination and national independence for almost thirty years without success. "The future of Formosan independence lies mainly in the hand of the majority itself. The real test of nationalism is a people's willingness to fight for it. In other words, when a person is independent, a nation is independent. Realistic and dynamic hope is the key to the problems of independence, personal and national as well."

### Ethnic Church Mission

He argued that new immigrants from Formosa should not be treated as regular immigrants but as people with particular problems requiring specific pastoral care. "The goal of a church is to carry out Christian mission, not to self-support. This is especially true to a new ethnic church. Ministry concerns primarily people (person) not head-counting or real estates. The church is where a group of people can touch one another's heart and feel they are one in the Holy Spirit."

### Experience Over Theory

Dr. Lai emphasized attending to actual experience over abstract theory: "In principle, it seems good to attend more closely to our actual experience than to theories, formulas or doctrine which purports to articulate and to direct that experience. For experience is in principle richer, more complex, and more full of compass."

### The Biblical Hope Movement

A striking conclusion was his observation that "according to percentage there are much more Christians than non-Christians involved in the Formosan Hope Movement — Formosan Independence Movement." He concluded that this phenomenon stemmed from a Biblical Hope Movement, with Formosan activists identifying contemporary history with the Israelite Exodus and identifying themselves with Abraham, Moses, Joshua, and Jesus.

### Hope vs. Hopelessness

Dr. Lai articulated a crucial distinction: "It is when we mix hope with what is hopeless that we become filled with hopelessness. It is the recognition that some ways are hopeless that open up for us the way to find hope. The important thing is not to think that all things can be hoped for but to keep the hopeless out of hope, to separate the impossible from the possible."

He quoted Paul Freire: "This climate of hope is born at the exact moment when a given society begins to turn upon itself and finds out that it is not finished, that there is an infinite number of tasks to be accomplished. The hopelessness and pessimism which existed before about its present and future, as well as the naïve optimism, give place to a critical optimism, to hope."

## The Missing 50 Years: Dr. Lai's Personal Journey

The "Missing 50 Years" section traces Dr. Lai's extraordinary life after completing his doctorate in May 1972 — a period during which he largely disappeared from public Taiwanese church circles.

### Graduation and Disappearance

Dr. Lai completed his doctoral degree in May 1972. Pastor Chaolong Kao specially flew to San Francisco Theological Seminary to attend the graduation ceremony. In July 1972, Dr. Lai's fiancée, Jane Hatsuko Ueno (a third-generation Japanese-American from Hawaii), and he held their wedding ceremony at FPCLA, officiated by Pastor Kao — the first wedding held after the establishment of the Los Angeles Presbyterian Church.

After that, Dr. Lai disappeared from the Taiwanese church community. He explained that theological education taught him that after a pastor hands over pastoral work to a successor, it is best not to continue attending that church, as it increases pressure on the successor. He also cited his dream of Taiwanese independence and missionary work in China — matters with "untold difficulties" during the turbulent political era.

### From "Theology of Hope" to "Theology of Revolution"

The period from 1970 to 1972 was described as "the most tumultuous and terrifying time for Taiwan." In 1970, the April 24 incident saw Peter Huang shoot at Chingkuo Chiang. In 1971, Taiwan was expelled from the UN. Nixon's visit to China in February 1972 further worsened Taiwan's situation.

Inspired by Nixon's engagement with the CCP, Dr Lai believed the Taiwanese people must study the CCP to learn how to overthrow the KMT. He discovered that Mao Zedong had written articles in 1928 and 1936 supporting Taiwanese independence, and had broadcast support for Taiwanese independence from Yan'an after the February 28 Incident.

### Trade with China: "Little Asia Imports"

Dr. Lai established an import company called "Little Asia Imports" (LAImports), borrowing nearly HK$20,000 from friends and family plus his own savings, to procure Chinese handicrafts for wholesale in Los Angeles. He planned to use trade as a means to establish connections with the CCP. The first year ran smoothly, but the Red Guards' rebellion in China during the Cultural Revolution made international trade nearly impossible, and his "China Dream" ended abruptly.

### Taiwan Independent Revolution Army (T.I.R.A.)

While managing his trade business, Dr. Lai joined the "Taiwan Independent Revolution Army" (T.I.R.A.), a secret underground organization founded by Su Beng (史明). He practiced shooting at a range near Highway 60 and Rosemead Boulevard every Saturday, and learned about explosives and demolition. He opened a bank account for T.I.R.A. by placing a three-day DBA advertisement in the classified section of the Los Angeles Times.

The effort came to a standstill when his comrade Kenneth Wang graduated and returned to Brazil, leaving Dr. Lai as the only person in T.I.R.A. About a year and a half later, an FBI agent visited him, having obtained his information from the bank. The agent warned him that armed revolutionary activities are not allowed in the U.S. and fall under the category of terrorist violence.

### Maytai Companies and Chinese Community Yellow Pages

During the 1970s wave of Taiwanese refugees fleeing to the U.S., Dr. Lai shifted his focus. With his friend Jihsiung Chen (陳吉雄), a pioneer in international color printing in Taiwan, he founded "Maytai Companies" — the first advertising company, Chinese-language translation company, Chinese color printing factory, and Chinese photographic studio for Taiwanese people in Los Angeles.

By 1980, they established the "Chinese Community Yellow Pages" phone directory, which grew from fewer than 200 pages and 3,000 copies in its first year to nearly 3,000 pages and 100,000 copies within a few years. They later published city versions covering the top ten U.S. cities with large Chinese populations, receiving awards from the Yellow Page Publishers Association, including the Annual Achievement Gold Award.

### American Business & Industry and the Nixon Connection

The company's 1984 Los Angeles Olympic Guide Book attracted the attention of the Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs. In 1986, the company published "American Business & Industry" in Shanghai with the Shanghai Publishing Bureau. The press conference at the Jinjiang Hotel was attended by U.S. Consul General in Shanghai and then-Secretary of the Shanghai Municipal Committee Zemin Jiang (江澤民). This was reported on CCTV Evening News — the first time in history that a new book's publication was reported in a national evening news broadcast in China.

The book featured a "Letter to the People of China" signed by former U.S. President Nixon, which made it a reference for Chinese officials during the reform and opening-up period. This led to ASM becoming the officially designated U.S. training program partner of the State Administration of Foreign Experts Affairs (SAFEA), training over 6,000 Chinese government officials and corporate executives from 1988 to 2000.

### The 1996 Missile Crisis and Realization About the CCP

By 1996, when Taiwan held its first direct presidential election, the CCP's reaction — including missile tests over Taiwan — surprised Dr. Lai. He learned that all personnel in the PLA missile unit were very nervous, worried the missiles might fall back onto Taiwan.

After the 1997 handover of Hong Kong, Dr. Lai finally realized: "In the long run, the CCP is more brutal and terrifying than the KMT for the Taiwanese people." He gradually recognized that China did not have what he wanted — the secret to overthrowing the KMT. The CCP was not as powerful as it seemed; it was primarily the internal corruption within the KMT that pushed it to the brink.

### SIAS College and "Fisherman's Hall"

Dr. Lai shifted from government training to education, assisting his friend Chen in founding SIAS College (西亞斯學院) in Zhengzhou, Henan. He spent years commuting between Beijing and Zhengzhou, helping develop foreign resources, international student programs, and student clubs (which expanded from fewer than 20 to nearly 200 types).

He also played a key role in establishing "Fisherman's Hall Christian Church" near SIAS College to serve the 150-180 foreign teachers hired annually. Over twenty years, the church grew from a small chapel serving foreign teachers into a magnificent seven-story building with 40 clergy and staff — far beyond his expectations.

### Letting Go: COVID and the End of an Era

In mid-December 2019, Dr. Lai returned to Los Angeles from Beijing and did not return. COVID-19 led to flight suspensions lasting two to three years. During this time, he reassessed the changes in both the U.S. and China, including tightening control over universities and intensifying surveillance of Taiwanese people in China. He officially informed SIAS that he would not be going back.

By 2025, the number of foreign teachers at SIAS had dropped from 150-200 to fewer than 40-50. The party committee at SIAS had expanded from four stationed personnel to 102 party branches covering faculty and students. The U.S.-China relationship had shifted from "quasi-allies" fifty years ago to "quasi-enemies" today.

### Sixty Years in America

As Dr. Lai approached completing sixty years in America, he reflected on what he called a "pendulum effect" — sixty years ago, San Francisco was in the midst of a leftist cultural revolution; in 2025, under Trump 2.0, the "MAGA wave" seemed to be resurfacing. He noted that as a passionate youth, he may not have been "far left," but was not far from being "New Left" — the most popular phrase being "Don't trust anyone over 30." Now in his eighties, he found himself supporting MAGA, leaning toward the center-right.

> "After careful reflection and humble introspection through prayer, I realize that this dream has not been the one I initially had. I ultimately recognize that in my life, there has always been a dream, and this dream has been realized, but it is not the same dream I started with. God's arrangements are truly marvelous!"

## Postscript: Echoes of History

The Postscript positions Dr. Lai's dissertation as a prophetic document whose insights remain relevant fifty years later. The issues discussed — Taiwanese identity, political dilemmas, and the pursuit of hope — continue to resonate in contemporary Taiwanese society.

### Echoes of History: The Shaping of Taiwanese Identity

The Postscript emphasizes that the dissertation laid the foundation for understanding the unique and complex identity of the Taiwanese people. Through colonial rule by the Dutch, Spanish, Ming and Qing dynasties, and the Japanese government, Taiwan's history has always been closely linked to foreign influence. Japanese colonial rule (1895–1945) inadvertently contributed to "National Unity" among the Taiwanese people by severing connections with mainland China and improving living standards.

The 228 Incident (1947), estimated to have caused 15,000 to 25,000 deaths, "completely destroyed the Taiwanese people's illusions about their 'motherland' and became a distant cause of the Taiwanese independence movement." The subsequent martial law and systematic oppression, while consolidating KMT rule, inadvertently strengthened the shared identity of the Taiwanese people and drove many young individuals abroad.

### From Exile to Democracy: The "Quiet Revolution"

The Postscript traces Taiwan's democratization from the 1970s to the present: the Kaohsiung Incident (1979), the establishment of the Democratic Progressive Party (1986), the lifting of martial law (1987), the Wild Lily Movement (1990), the first direct presidential election (1996), and the completion of three transfers of power.

The "Quiet Revolution" nature of Taiwan's democratization lies in its avoidance of large-scale bloodshed — a lesson drawn from the bloody experiences of the 228 Incident and the Kaohsiung Incident. In the eyes of believers, this peaceful transformation is a manifestation of "God's grace."

In 2021, the Economist Intelligence Unit ranked Taiwan eighth in its democracy index, the highest among countries outside the Western world.

### A Community of Hope: Christian Faith in Times of Crisis

The Postscript concludes that the Formosan Presbyterian Church in Los Angeles was not merely a religious space but a "Mission of Identity" — providing a foundation of hope for "lost" Taiwanese, preserving and cultivating a unique Taiwanese identity abroad, and providing a platform for the Taiwanese language, culture, and shared experiences.

> "This sense of 'hope' in Christian faith is not only an ultimate concern but also a practical force that transforms individual suffering under authoritarianism into a commitment to social change, providing a deep moral and spiritual foundation for the 'Quiet Revolution.'"

### Conclusion: Lessons from History and the Sustainability of Democracy

Dr. Lai's dissertation, written in 1971, is described as "not only a profound depiction of the Taiwanese people's struggles at that time but also a prophecy for future transformation." The journey from Taiwan's authoritarian oppression beginning in 1945 to today's thriving democracy is widely praised by the international community as a peaceful "Quiet Revolution" — which can also be viewed as "God's grace."

> "Learning from history is not merely a retrospective endeavor; it is about continuously constructing a more just and free Taiwanese society, carrying on the mission embodied in 'Toward a Community of Hope,' and allowing the light of hope to continue shining on this land."
