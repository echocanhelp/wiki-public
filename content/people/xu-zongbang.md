# 許宗邦 (Xu Zongbang)

## Identity Snapshot
- **English:** Xu Zongbang
- **Chinese:** 許宗邦
- **Core roles:** Physician, medical missionary, humanitarian
- **Geography:** Taiwanese American (Iowa / Southern California)
- **Era:** 1980s–2020s

## Overview

**Xu Zongbang** (許宗邦) is a Taiwanese American physician and the driving force behind NATMA (North American Taiwanese Medical Association) international medical missions. After establishing a successful internal medicine practice in Southern California, he has led over a dozen medical mission trips to Central American countries, providing free healthcare to underserved populations while advancing Taiwan's informal diplomacy.

### Early Life & Medical Training
1. Graduated from Kaohsiung Medical University, then worked as an internist at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital in Taiwan.
2. In May 1982, immigrated to the United States, obtaining a green card within weeks.
3. Completed a residency in Family Medicine at the University of Iowa (Des Moines) in 1982–1983, where he first encountered Taiwan independence activism through Professor Lin Zongguang.
4. Completed three years of family medicine training at Iowa Lutheran Hospital, then moved to Ontario, California, joining Kaiser Permanente's medical staff.
5. During his early years in Iowa, was deeply influenced by Lin Zongguang (son of Lin Zongguang senior, a 228 incident victim), whose teachings helped him recognize the Kuomintang's oppression of Taiwanese people and develop a strong Taiwan consciousness.

### Awakening to Taiwan Consciousness
1. In 1981, the Chen Wen-cheng murder case shocked Taiwanese Americans; Professor Liao Shuzong established TAPA (North American Taiwanese Professors Association) in Chicago, leading to the formation of FAPA and other organizations.
2. Xu met Peng Ming-min at a 1985 TAPA annual meeting in Des Moines, Iowa; they became close friends, with Peng addressing him by his given name.
3. In Iowa, also befriended Taiwanese American writer Yang Yuanxun and the family of Hsu Hsiaojung, forming lasting connections.
4. In 1985, joined Kaiser Permanente in Ontario, California, beginning his long-term medical career in Southern California.

## Career / Legacy

### NATMA Medical Missions
1. Beginning in 2003, organized annual medical mission trips under NATMA, starting with Granada, Caribbean (10 doctors), then expanding to Costa Rica, Panama, Belize, Honduras, and Guatemala.
2. Led missions with 40–70+ physician teams including specialists in family medicine, internal medicine, pediatrics, surgery, ophthalmology, dentistry, and nursing.
3. In 2005, the Costa Rica mission treated over 1,300 patients in four days; in 2018, a Honduras mission treated 6,097 patients in four days with 71 doctors.
4. In 2012, a Guatemala mission saved a child's life when Dr. Ake performed emergency thoracotomy for a gunshot wound, spending four hours in surgery.
5. Missions were not purely medical; they served as instruments of Taiwan's informal diplomacy, with local mayors, first ladies, and government officials welcoming the teams.
6. The mission logo "Taiwan Care" was designed to convey care from Taiwan to the world.
7. In 2018, the Honduras first lady Ana García de Hernández arranged a presidential dinner for the team, and a child with congenital heart disease was referred to the capital's main hospital for surgery.

### Team Building & International Collaboration
1. Built a diverse, inclusive team drawing from Kaiser Permanente colleagues, including physicians from Ukraine, Iran, Canada, Thailand, the Philippines, and even a Chinese national from Beijing who participated three times.
2. The team welcomed physicians regardless of political views, uniting around the shared mission of serving the underserved.
3. Inspired by Dr. Ake from Nigeria, who modeled his larger-scale missions after Xu's model, demonstrating the reciprocal nature of medical missionary work.

### Community Service in the US
1. Conducted free monthly clinics at the Tzu Chi Institute in Alhambra from 1985 to 2005, providing two decades of community healthcare.
2. His wife (unnamed in the publication) actively supported mission trips, distributing eyeglasses and emphasizing that "giving is more blessed than receiving."
3. The team operated on a volunteer basis; each mission cost approximately $120,000, funded by NATMA member donations, fundraising, and personal contributions.
